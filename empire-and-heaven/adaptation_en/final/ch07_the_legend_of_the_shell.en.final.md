# Empire and Heaven (Volume I)
## Chapter 7 — The Legend of the Shell

The house of Andronicus had not sounded so alive in years.

Servants crossed the halls with trays and amphorae. Lamps burned in polished bronze stands. Garlands of flowers hung from the pillars and trembled whenever laughter passed beneath them. In the garden, children ran between the trees with hands full of bread, fruit, and roasted meat, their voices rising above the fountain like birds startled into flight.

Inside the largest reception room sat the elders of the Ephesian church.

John was there, white-haired and quiet, with Onesimus, Tychicus, Ignatius, Sosthenes, Papias, Polycarp, Prochorus, and many believers who had come under Andronicus's promise. The arrangement still felt improbable. Christians, many of them accustomed to cellars, caves, private courtyards, and meetings before dawn, now sat in the house of a Roman quaestor while slaves brought food in abundance.

Some ate gratefully.

Some ate with visible unease.

Outside, the orphans from Drusiana's school had claimed the garden as their own kingdom. None of them had ever eaten in such a house. Their delight was so fierce that even the servants smiled despite themselves.

Gaius, however, carried himself with unusual solemnity. The other children treated him with envy and respect, for he alone had been promised passage to Rome with the bride and groom. That promise had made him proud for an hour and troubled afterward. Drusiana would leave Ephesus, and with her departure the children understood more than adults wished them to understand: food might continue, perhaps; instruction might continue; charity might continue. But the woman who had known their names would be gone.

They discussed the matter with the gravity of senators.

At last they decided they would ask John.

If the apostle could answer questions about God, Rome, death, and resurrection, perhaps he could answer what would happen to hungry children when Drusiana went away.

In the reception room, conversation had gathered around Tychicus.

He had become a living treasury of roads no one else had walked: Paul's letters, Onesimus's return to Philemon, the apostasy in Ephesus, the fire of Rome, Nero's slaughter, the mines of Hispania, Glabrio's conversion, and the long-delayed letter to the Ephesians. Every time the old man stopped, someone asked another question.

Yet not all eyes remained on Tychicus.

John's gaze moved now and then toward the door.

At last he said, "Polycarp."

Polycarp looked up quickly.

"Yes, Apostle."

"Callimachus has still not come."

The room seemed to grow quieter around the question.

Polycarp felt Prochorus's eyes turn toward him. The two had said little of what had passed the night before. Callimachus had gone to see Drusiana. Andronicus also had gone to Tyrannus's house. The next morning Drusiana had been married under John's blessing. Since then Callimachus had vanished.

"He left no message," Polycarp said.

John watched him.

"No message at all?"

"None that reached me."

It was not a lie. It was not enough truth to quiet his conscience.

John did not press him.

Instead, the old apostle lowered his eyes and placed a hand inside his tunic.

Those who knew him well had seen the gesture before. From beneath the cloth he drew a cord. On it hung a small shell, pierced through and worn smooth by years against his breast. It was no larger than a child's thumb, but even in lamplight it shimmered faintly with colors the sea might have kept for itself.

John held it in his palm.

The sound of the feast withdrew.

Memory came for him with such force that for a moment the room of Andronicus disappeared.

Another wedding rose before him.

Cana.

Wine gone too early. Servants whispering in embarrassment. Mary speaking quietly to her son. Stone jars filled with water. The steward tasting what had become wine and not knowing where it had come from.

John had been young then.

Young enough to tremble before wonder and still be distracted by a face.

Behind the guests, half hidden beneath a veil, a girl had watched. Her eyes were large and sorrowful, her face pale as if sorrow had reached her before age could. Sophia. He had seen her before on the shore of Tiberias, when he and another young man who bore his same name had spoken of Rome, Israel, philosophy, revenge, and the future as though the world waited for their passion to explain it.

She had given him the shell.

Later, after Vespasian had ruined her and left in her wake a wound that turned two young men toward vengeance, the shell became more than memory. It became accusation. Desire. Loss. The ache of a life that might have gone another way if he had followed the girl instead of his anger, or his ambition, or even his calling as he understood it then.

He had wanted Rome to fall.

He had dreamed of it.

For years Vespasian's face had returned to him in sleep with cold laughter, and John had woken grinding his teeth like a man still sharpening a blade. Even disciples could carry hatred under the name of zeal. He knew that now. It had taken him most of his life to know it.

Whenever he saw young people joined in marriage, Sophia's eyes returned.

The old apostle closed his hand over the shell.

Then the house stirred.

Someone near the entrance called, "The bridegroom and the bride have come!"

The present rushed back.

Pipes sounded from the courtyard. Children began singing. John slipped the shell beneath his tunic and looked toward the door.

Andronicus entered first in formal toga, carrying his rank with the ease of a man trained from childhood to be seen. Beside him came Drusiana beneath her wreath and orange bridal veil. Her tunic was short-sleeved and pale, her waist bound with the woolen girdle tied in the knot only the bridegroom might loose. She looked radiant, but those who loved her could also see the strain beneath the veil.

The Christians rose and received them with applause.

Servants cleared the first dishes and brought lamb, venison, doves, greens fried in olive oil, figs, laurel, and honeyed delicacies. Out of respect for the many Jews among the believers, no pork had been prepared.

Andronicus escorted Drusiana to the room set aside for the bride, then returned to the hall.

Hesthenes leaned near him and spoke low enough that only his master would hear.

"Dominus, you have now enjoyed the most glorious Christian wedding ever held in Ephesus."

"Do not exaggerate."

"I never exaggerate on holy occasions."

"What should happen next?"

"At a proper feast? Music and dancing. At a Christian feast? I hesitate to guess. Perhaps they sing beautifully while refusing every pleasure known to civilized people."

Andronicus hid a smile and turned to the guests.

"My friends," he said, the unfamiliar word landing awkwardly but sincerely, "what music would please you?"

Before any elder could answer, Gaius sprang up from John's side.

"I know what song the apostle loves!"

John looked down at him with surprise.

"Do you?"

Gaius nodded solemnly, then signaled to the children in the garden. One by one they gathered near the doorway, still holding bread and fruit, and began to sing.

The Lord is my shepherd; I shall not want.
He makes me lie down in green pastures.
He leads me beside still waters.
He restores my soul.

The melody was simple, clear, and unlike any song Andronicus had heard at Roman dinners. It carried no theater, no seduction, no praise of a god hungry for blood or of an emperor hungry for loyalty. It moved through the room like water finding low ground.

He stood very still.

Around him, the faces of the Christians changed. Men who had endured prison lowered their heads. Women who had buried children closed their eyes. Tychicus wept openly. Onesimus covered his mouth with one hand.

John looked at the children and did not move.

The shepherd psalm entered him as if from far away, and for an instant he saw another table, another night, another Lord girding Himself with a towel.

When the song ended, silence remained longer than applause would have.

Then John lifted Gaius onto his knees.

"You remembered well."

"Drusiana taught us," the boy said.

Andronicus looked toward the door through which his bride had gone.

Again he felt it: he had married a woman whose life contained rooms he had never entered.

More food came. The feast resumed. Yet a gentler tone had settled over the house.

After a time the children, emboldened by full stomachs, brought their question to John. What would become of them when Drusiana went to Rome?

The old apostle listened as if they had brought a matter of imperial law.

"You will not be abandoned," he said.

"Who will teach us?" one girl asked.

"Polycarp will help. Prochorus will help. The brothers and sisters here will help."

Gaius frowned.

"But Polycarp is always looking for Callimachus."

The innocent sentence passed through the room like a draft.

Polycarp lowered his eyes.

John did not rebuke the boy.

He only set a hand on his hair.

"Then Polycarp must learn to do both."

The feast slowly quieted.

At length John stood.

Because of his age, everyone else rose with him. He gestured for them to remain seated, but no one did. The room understood without being told that the old apostle wished to speak.

He looked first at Andronicus and then toward the chamber where Drusiana waited with the women.

"Today," he said, "we have seen something strange and merciful. A Roman house opened its doors to those who are often feared by Rome. The poor have eaten beside the wealthy. Children have sung where officials dine. A bride beloved by this church has entered the house of a man who is not yet of us, and yet has given his word for our safety."

Andronicus felt every eye move toward him.

John continued.

"Do not think marriage joins only two desires. It joins two histories, two wounds, two hopes. If it is guarded by pride, it becomes another empire. If it is given to God, it becomes a little kingdom of mercy."

No one spoke.

"We have spent many nights defending the memory of Christ from falsehood," John said. "But the true Christ is not preserved only in scrolls. He must live among you. He must be seen in the way you feed children, forgive enemies, protect the weak, and remain faithful when fear has become reasonable. Remember what Matthew recorded from the Lord: 'Surely I am with you always, to the very end of the age.'"

His voice weakened slightly, but the room leaned toward it.

"If He is with us, then love must remain among us. Not the love that possesses. Not the love that flatters itself. The love that lays down its life."

He turned.

"Polycarp."

Polycarp rose, startled.

"Come here."

The young man stepped through the gathered believers and knelt before John.

John placed both hands on his head.

A murmur moved through the room. Those old enough to know the meaning of the gesture drew in their breath.

"Polycarp," John said, "in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, I lay hands upon you and entrust to you the church in Smyrna. Guard the flock of Christ. Feed them with truth. Strengthen the weak. Stand when standing becomes costly."

Polycarp trembled.

He had not expected this. He had come to the feast burdened by a missing friend and now found a church placed upon his shoulders.

John bent lower, speaking so softly that only Polycarp heard the next words.

"And remember Callimachus. He will need you."

Polycarp shut his eyes.

The name pierced deeper than the ordination.

John lifted his hands and faced the assembly.

"The Lord be with you."

The room answered, many voices broken with tears.

"And also with you."

For a moment it seemed the blessing might conclude the night.

Then John looked toward Andronicus.

Something in Hesthenes's face changed.

He knew before the others that the old man was about to do what prudence might have delayed.

John spoke again.

"I must also tell you a joyful thing. Today, with this bride and bridegroom, I too will begin a journey."

Confusion moved through the room.

Papias rose first.

"Apostle, where are you going?"

"To Rome."

The word struck like a thrown stone.

Voices broke out at once.

"Rome?"

"No."

"Apostle—"

"You cannot—"

Onesimus stood, pale with alarm.

"Why would you go to Rome now? Domitian is hunting the church there. Clemens has been seized. Domitilla is in danger. Informers are everywhere."

John looked at him with great tenderness.

"That is why I must go."

Andronicus turned sharply toward Hesthenes.

"What is this?"

Hesthenes lowered his eyes.

"I told him you had received imperial interest in him. I did not tell him to announce it during the feast."

"Imperial interest?"

"Rome has asked questions about John. You were going to be asked to bring him sooner or later. He has chosen sooner."

Andronicus's jaw tightened.

The room was in uproar now. Papias pleaded. Prochorus protested. Tychicus wept and tried to speak. Even Ignatius, ordinarily disciplined, looked shaken.

John raised one hand.

Slowly the room quieted.

"My children," he said, "I am not being dragged from you tonight. I am going as a witness. If Rome calls, shall I hide while others die? I have lived long enough to see many brothers finish their race before me. Perhaps the Lord now grants me my turn."

"We still need you," Papias said.

"You need Christ," John answered. "Not me."

The simplicity of it silenced him.

John turned toward the newly ordained Polycarp.

"You will go to Smyrna. Onesimus will remain here. Papias will keep writing. Prochorus, if you insist on following me, you will do so not as a rescuer but as a servant. Tychicus—"

"I will go," the old traveler said at once.

"You have walked enough roads."

"Then let this be one more."

John gave him a weary smile.

No one argued further.

The feast had changed. It was no longer only a wedding celebration. It had become farewell without warning.

Andronicus looked at John and felt a pressure he could not name. The old man had turned a Roman summons into obedience. He had taken the Empire's reach and made it into witness. In doing so, he had left Andronicus neither triumph nor refusal.

Drusiana returned from the bridal chamber then, having heard the commotion. When she saw the faces in the room, she understood that something had altered beyond repair.

"Apostle?" she whispered.

John opened his arms to her.

She came and knelt, tears already on her cheeks.

"You are going to Rome?"

"With you," he said. "For part of the road."

"But Rome is dangerous."

"So is every place where love must remain faithful."

She bowed her head against his hands.

Beyond the walls, Ephesus continued its festival. Somewhere in the city, Callimachus remained absent, unseen, and wounded. In the house of Andronicus, a bride wept beside an old apostle; a young man had been made bishop; a Roman husband stood uncertain inside his own victory; and children, no longer laughing, watched the adults as though childhood itself had begun to understand loss.

John's hand moved once more to the shell beneath his tunic.

He did not draw it out.

Some memories belonged only to God and the one who had carried them too long.

But the shell lay warm against his chest as the room prepared for farewell.

A wedding had summoned the past.

A journey would now summon the cross.

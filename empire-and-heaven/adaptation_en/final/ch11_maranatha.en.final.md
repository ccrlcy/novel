# Empire and Heaven (Volume II)
## Chapter 11 — Maranatha

They brought Jesus first to the house of Annas.

Torches burned at the entrances. Servants and temple officers moved in uneasy clusters. The house was awake with the brittle attention of men who knew they were doing something that must be finished before daylight gave it a proper name.

John entered without resistance.

He had been there often enough for the servants to know his face. He had hardly crossed the courtyard when he noticed Peter had not followed. The fisherman stood outside the gate, blocked by attendants who did not recognize him and did not like his Galilean accent.

John turned back and spoke to a maidservant he knew.

"Let him in. He is with me."

The girl looked at Peter more closely.

"You are one of that Nazarene's disciples, aren't you?"

Peter flinched as if struck.

"No," he said. "No, I am not."

He slipped through only after the denial had opened the gate.

John told him to remain near the fire and listen. Then he moved toward the inner court, where Annas had begun questioning Jesus.

"What have you taught the people?"

Jesus answered plainly.

"I have spoken openly. I have taught in the synagogues and in the temple. I have said nothing in secret. Ask those who heard Me. They know what I said."

The words entered John like a blade.

Ask those who heard Me.

He had heard.

For three years he had heard. He had leaned close enough to catch the Lord's breath at supper. He had heard parables, rebukes, promises, warnings, mysteries spoken by the road and in houses and on the mountain and by the sea.

And now he stood hidden among servants, saying nothing.

One of Annas's attendants struck Jesus across the face.

"Is that how you answer the high priest?"

Jesus did not curse him.

Annas, having found neither answer nor usefulness, sent Him to Caiaphas.

In the courtyard, Peter stood with the servants around the fire. The same maid came near again.

"You are one of them. I saw you with Him."

"I told you, I do not know what you are talking about."

Another man turned toward him.

"Your speech gives you away. You are a Galilean."

Peter spat into the dirt.

"I do not know the man."

Then the rooster cried.

The sound passed through the courtyard, thin and ordinary, and it broke him.

Before the servants could say more, Peter turned away. The firelight caught his face once, and John saw that it had collapsed inward like a wall. He went out into the dark weeping like a child.

John did not follow him.

He remained.

That too would remain with him.

Caiaphas needed proof, or at least the appearance of proof. Witnesses came forward, but their words did not agree. One said Jesus had threatened to destroy the temple and rebuild it in three days. Another repeated the charge differently. The law required harmony; fear supplied urgency instead.

At last Caiaphas faced Jesus directly.

"Are you the Messiah, the Son of God?"

Jesus lifted His head.

"If you knew God, you would see the Son of Man coming with the clouds of heaven."

The words were enough.

Caiaphas tore his garments and called it blasphemy. Men struck Jesus with fists and palms. They spat in His face and mocked Him. The council condemned Him before morning and sent Him bound to Pilate.

Pilate did not wish to inherit another province's religion.

The Roman governor knew enough to see that envy and fear lay beneath the accusation. The priests called Jesus a rebel. They claimed He had forbidden tribute to Caesar. Pilate had already heard the contrary: render to Caesar what belongs to Caesar, and to God what belongs to God.

"Are you the king of the Jews?" Pilate asked.

"You say so," Jesus answered. "But My kingdom is not of this world."

"What kingdom is that?"

"The kingdom where truth lives. I came to bear witness to the truth."

Pilate looked at Him and felt, against his will, the discomfort of being seen.

"What is truth?"

Jesus was silent.

Truth stood before him and did not defend itself.

A message arrived from Pilate's wife, warning him not to entangle himself with the righteous man. The warning made him more eager to be done. He sent Jesus to Herod, who mocked Him and sent Him back. He offered the crowd a prisoner for the Passover. Surely, he thought, they would ask for this quiet Galilean rather than Barabbas, a true man of violence.

They asked for Barabbas.

"Then what shall I do with Jesus?"

"Crucify Him."

The word rose once, then again, then became the crowd's single breath.

Pilate washed his hands before them.

"I am innocent of this man's blood."

But water has never removed cowardice.

Jesus was given to the soldiers.

They dressed Him in purple, twisted thorns into a crown, struck Him, spat on Him, bowed before Him, and named Him king because mockery was all they understood of majesty. When they grew bored, they laid the cross upon Him and led Him out.

John watched until the procession began moving toward the place of execution.

Then he turned away.

Everything was finished.

No angels came. No fire fell. No legion broke from heaven. Jesus had performed no final sign, no royal disclosure, no declaration that would turn shame into victory before the eyes of Jerusalem. He went beneath the cross like a condemned man.

John walked the other way.

Three years had dissolved like foam.

He tried to think of what remained. Galilee? Nets? His father's boat? He could not return to that smallness. The Zealots, then. If Jesus would not raise the kingdom, perhaps the sword still could. Perhaps Vespasian could still be found, Rome still opposed, Israel still avenged.

Then another thought struck him so sharply that he stopped in the street.

What if the final sign was still to come?

What if Jesus waited until the place of execution to reveal Himself? He had fed thousands in the wilderness. He had raised Lazarus after four days. If He spoke now from the cross and declared the kingdom, if all the others had fled, if Judas was gone, if Peter was broken, then John would be the one remaining.

His heart began to pound.

He ran toward Golgotha.

By the time he reached the hill, Jesus and the two rebels were already nailed to their crosses.

The rebels fought for breath, pushing against shattered feet to keep from suffocating. Jesus hung between them, His body already sagging beneath blood, exhaustion, and pain.

One of the men gasped toward Him.

"Are you not the Messiah? Save yourself and us."

The other rebuked him with the little strength left in his voice.

"We suffer what we deserve. This man has done nothing wrong." Then he turned his face toward Jesus. "Remember me when You come into Your kingdom."

Jesus lifted Himself enough to answer.

"Today you will be with Me in paradise."

Then His eyes found John.

John stood below Him, still clinging to the last thread of expectation.

But Jesus looked past his ambition toward the women near the cross: Mary His mother, Mary Magdalene, Mary the wife of Clopas, and Salome.

His voice was nearly gone.

"Woman, behold your son."

Then to John:

"Behold your mother."

The last hope of political miracle died inside him.

Jesus had not given him a throne.

He had given him His mother.

A burden.

A command.

A mercy he did not yet know how to receive.

A little later Jesus whispered, "It is finished."

And He died.

Because the Sabbath was near, the authorities wanted the bodies removed quickly. Soldiers broke the legs of the two rebels. When they came to Jesus, they saw He was already dead. One of them thrust a spear into His side. Blood and water flowed out.

Mary cried out and covered her face.

Afterward Joseph of Arimathea came with Nicodemus. Men from Jerusalem, men with standing, men who had lacked power to stop the sentence, now received the body the Galileans had fled from. They wrapped Jesus and laid Him in a new tomb nearby.

John could not lift his eyes.

He, the disciple who had dreamed of being first, had stood aside while others buried his Lord.

The women returned to Mark's house with Mary.

John followed.

Peter was already there, dust-covered and emptied. He told them Judas had hanged himself. The money had been thrown back into the temple. The man whom John had considered the most dangerous rival was dead, and the news gave him no relief. There were no rivals left because there was no kingdom to win.

They spent the Sabbath hidden, waiting for the next knock at the door.

At dawn after the Sabbath, Mary Magdalene and the other women went to the tomb with spices. They returned sooner than anyone expected, breathless and wild-eyed.

"He is alive!"

The house stirred in confusion.

Mary spoke too quickly. The stone had been rolled away. The tomb was open. The guards were gone. The body was not there.

Peter and John ran.

John was younger and faster. He reached the tomb first and saw the linen cloths lying inside. He stopped at the entrance.

If Jesus had risen, then everything John had hidden was exposed.

His ambition.
His schemes.
His jealousies.
His use of the Lord for his own imagined place in the kingdom.

If Jesus had risen, then John would stand before Him with nothing concealed.

Peter went in first and came out speaking in broken excitement. The body was gone. The cloths were there. Something had happened beyond fear, beyond politics, beyond the reach of priests or Rome.

John trembled.

Resurrection was not victory to him at first.

It was judgment.


By the time John finished speaking, the room in which he lay had fallen into a silence almost like death.

Papias slowly lowered the reed with which he had been writing. Prochorus, who had supported the old apostle's body for so long, could no longer feel his own legs. When Papias moved behind John to take his place, he felt at once how cold the old man's back had become.

John's breathing was thin.

Yet his eyes opened once more.

"His rising was my despair," he said. "If He had risen, then I stood before Him as a traitor. I had used Him for my ambition. I had helped turn Jerusalem against Him by spreading fear that the new kingdom would have no room for them. Judas sold Him for silver. But I had sold Him for myself."

No one moved.

"I wanted to be first. By His resurrection I learned I was last."

The words entered Callimachus like judgment and mercy together.

He had lived by rivalry, injury, resentment, the hunger to prove that the rejected could become greater than those who had rejected them. In the ruined confession of the apostle, he saw his own life laid bare.

John reached for him.

Callimachus came forward and took the old man's hand.

"Callimachus," John whispered, "at the final supper the Lord said, Agapate allelous. Love one another."

Then he reached toward Andronicus.

The Roman came quickly and knelt beside him.

John held the hands of both young men.

"The kingdoms of this world divide and devour. In the kingdom of God, men forgive and are reconciled."

Callimachus turned to Andronicus, tears running openly down his face.

"Andronicus," he said, "if you can forgive a ruined man, take your wife back."

Drusiana stepped forward, frightened by the simplicity with which men tried to settle what her own heart knew was not simple.

"And what am I to do?" she asked.

All eyes turned to her.

Her voice trembled, but she did not retreat.

"The people of Corama will not release me easily. They are wounded, sick, frightened people. They need someone who can heal. They need someone who will stay."

John heard her.

He did not answer her directly.

Instead he called, "Papias."

"Yes, Apostle."

"In the record you have written, do not use my name except where you must distinguish me from the Baptist. I have lived ashamed of it. I now entrust that name to these two young men. Let them carry it better than I did. Let the name John be forgiven through lives I could not live."

He motioned for Andronicus and Callimachus to kneel.

They obeyed.

John placed his hand first upon the Roman's head.

"John Andronicus," he said, "in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit, I appoint you bishop for the church in Cappadocia. Go to the wounded souls of Corama. Lead them toward the kingdom of heaven. Bring light into the caves."

Drusiana folded her hands and bowed her head.

She understood then.

She would not need to abandon Corama. The burden of healing would become a church, and Andronicus would enter it not as conqueror, but as shepherd.

John placed his hand upon Callimachus.

"John Callimachus," he said, "in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit, I appoint you bishop of the church in Ephesus. Follow the example of Timothy and Onesimus before you. Guard the banner of Christ with your life."

Those in white garments, those in black, and even the soldiers gathered there answered together:

"Amen."

For a moment enemies, fugitives, nobles, servants, Christians, former bandits, and Roman soldiers stood under the same word.

Gaius, standing beside Drusiana, lifted his hand as if a new administrative problem had been discovered.

"Then I still have one worry left."

A faint smile touched John's mouth.

"What troubles you, Gaius?"

"Should I go with Bishop Andronicus to Corama, or stay in Ephesus with Bishop Callimachus?"

John drew the boy closer and rested a shaking hand on his head.

"Then go back and forth. Gaius, the Lord will make use of you for the kingdom of heaven. I have prayed that your soul may prosper, and that in all things you may be strengthened."

The boy nodded as if receiving formal orders from heaven.

John's strength was nearly gone.

He looked from face to face: Drusiana, Andronicus, Callimachus, Papias, Prochorus, Tychicus, Polycarp, the people of Corama, the believers of Ephesus, the soldiers who did not know why their eyes had filled.

So many names.

So many roads.

So much had been lost, and yet nothing entrusted to Christ had been wasted.

His lips moved.

"Maranatha."

Come, Lord.

The word passed through the room like a final breath and a first prayer.

Then John, the son of Zebedee, the last witness, the son of thunder whom love had broken and remade, became still.

No one spoke for a long time.

Outside, the world remained what it had been: Rome still claimed its provinces, wounds still needed tending, churches still needed shepherds, and the kingdoms of men still contended for power.

But in that room two enemies knelt as brothers.

A woman was freed to heal.

A child received a road between two churches.

And the name the old apostle had carried in shame was given forward, not as a monument, but as a charge.

Love one another.

Maranatha.

# Empire and Heaven (Volume I)
## Chapter 2 — Strangers

Every passenger aboard the *Vesta* had come up to the deck.

No one spoke for a while.

Ephesus shone ahead of them in the evening light, rising from the harbor as if the sea itself had carried a jeweled city to the edge of Asia. Its marble caught the last fire of the sun. Its columns stood pale and golden. Beyond the docks, the Harbor Road cut straight through the city toward the great theater beneath Mount Pion.

Someone near the rail whispered, almost in prayer, "The jewel of Artemis."

At the center of the harbor stood the memorial gate, its Ionic columns soft with reflected light. Beyond it, the avenue stretched broad and clean toward the theater, whose tiered seats opened against the mountain like a vast shell. Mount Pion rose behind it, rounded and quiet in the glow of dusk.

Then a cry broke through the stillness.

"Artemis is coming!"

Bodies pressed toward the rail.

"Where?"

"There—by the road near the theater!"

At first Andronicus saw only the crowd.

People were pouring from the eastern streets in such numbers that the road itself seemed to move. Flowers and green branches rose above them, carried in thousands of hands. Color rolled forward in waves. Voices gathered, broke apart, and gathered again until the whole city seemed to be breathing through one throat.

Hesthenes leaned beside him.

"We have arrived at the spring festival."

The procession advanced slowly, filling the Harbor Road from side to side. By the time the *Vesta* lowered anchor, the first ranks of worshipers had reached the memorial gate.

They came through all three openings—the straight central passage and the two arches beside it—until the harbor square filled with bodies, flowers, torches, incense, and sound.

Andronicus did not disembark at once.

Neither did Hesthenes.

From the ship, the festival could be seen whole.

The image of Artemis appeared beneath a canopy of leaves and blossoms. Before her walked the high priest in white. Behind him came the temple attendants who guarded the goddess, men who had given their bodies to her service and carried the pain of that devotion like rank. After them moved the priestesses, also clothed in white, twenty-four women vowed to lifelong virginity in honor of the goddess's abundance.

The city roared when the image passed beneath the gate.

Music rose from flutes and drums. Incense smoke thickened the air. Flowers struck the stones underfoot and were crushed into color beneath the procession.

For a moment the harbor was no longer a place of trade. It was a mouth, and all Ephesus was crying through it.

Hesthenes watched the crowd with unreadable eyes.

"And Rome thinks it rules the world."

Andronicus said nothing.

The sacrificial bulls had already been bound near the harbor square. Their bodies strained against the wooden frames. Priests moved among them with polished knives while the crowd shouted, sang, laughed, prayed, and pressed closer.

Then the first animal screamed.

The sound cut upward through music and incense.

Blood struck the stones.

The crowd answered with a roar that was half worship and half hunger.

The priests lifted the offerings before the goddess. Dancers whirled near the altar fires. Drums pounded faster. Smoke rose from the prepared flames, and soon the smell of burning meat rolled through the harbor and drifted toward the sea.

Ephesus gave itself to the ritual without hesitation.

It was beautiful.

It was feverish.

It was impossible to tell where devotion ended and appetite began.

"Dominus."

Hesthenes touched Andronicus's arm.

A young Roman officer stood behind them in military dress. He saluted with practiced precision.

"Welcome home, Quaestor Marius Andronicus. By order of the governor, I have come to escort you through the city. As you can see, Ephesus is difficult tonight."

Behind the officer stood Lucius, steward of the Andronicus household. The older man bowed.

"Welcome home, my lord."

For a brief instant, Andronicus forgot the crowd, the goddess, the smoke, even the officer's salute.

Home.

The word struck him more sharply than he had expected.

There were questions he wanted to ask Lucius at once. One name rose above the others.

Drusiana.

But the deck was crowded. Soldiers waited. The governor's man watched him. A Roman noble did not show his private heart in public.

"Very well," Andronicus said. "Let us go ashore."

The soldiers opened a path through the festival crowd. Andronicus descended with Lucius, the Roman officer, his secretary, and his physician. Hesthenes followed on foot with the others.

They crossed the harbor square through heat, blood, smoke, and the smell of roasting meat.

Beyond the memorial gate, horses were waiting.

Andronicus mounted. Lucius rode beside him. Hesthenes and the rest of the household party walked behind while the soldiers pressed the crowd aside.

The Harbor Road unfolded before them.

Columns lined both sides of the avenue. Upon them stood statues of heroes, emperors, philosophers, and gods, all gazing down as though the city were a stage prepared for their judgment. Shops crowded the marble way: jewelers, perfume sellers, cloth merchants, money changers, bronze workers, ivory carvers, booksellers, and vendors of small sacred images.

At the edges of the street, men bent over gaming boards carved directly into the stone. Dice clicked. Coins changed hands. Laughter rose, followed by curses. A man shouted in triumph. Another spat and called upon three gods to witness that he had been cheated.

Ephesus was alive in every direction.

To the left stood the harbor gymnasium and the baths, their halls still busy despite the festival. Athletes, merchants, teachers, magistrates, sailors, physicians, and travelers moved through the colonnades. Rome loved strength. Greece loved argument. Asia loved trade. Ephesus had learned to profit from all three.

Andronicus rode in silence.

Everything was familiar.

Yet nothing returned to him as it had been.

The city seemed unchanged, but he was not the boy who had left it.

He had seen too much of Rome to believe in marble.

Citizens turned as his escort passed. Some recognized the young heir of the Andronicus family and raised their hands politely. Others only saw rank and soldiers, and bowed because rank and soldiers were safest when acknowledged.

Lucius guided his horse closer.

"Your mother has been waiting for you," he said.

"Is she well?"

"Better since she learned you were coming."

Andronicus received the answer with a nod, though another question still pressed against his teeth.

Ahead, the theater grew larger, its stone tiers darkening as the sun dropped behind the city. Near the Roman Agora, colossal statues of Augustus and Vespasian rose on high marble pillars. Their faces watched the avenue with imperial calm.

Andronicus raised his hand in formal salute.

"How is the city?" he asked.

Lucius answered as a steward should answer in public.

"Peaceful. Trade prospers. The authority of Rome remains firm. The schools are full, and the citizens are content."

Behind them, Hesthenes almost smiled.

Rome called many things education. Most of them were forms of ambition taught early and polished well. Parents spent fortunes so their sons might avoid labor and enter the machinery of wealth, law, finance, and public honor. The Empire produced clever men in abundance.

Wise men were rarer.

As they neared the theater district, Hesthenes glanced back.

The old traveler from the *Vesta* was there.

Tychicus.

He moved slowly through the festival crowd with a fish tied by string in one hand and a worn bundle over his shoulder. He did not seem lost, exactly. Nor did he seem hurried. At times he stopped and lifted his eyes toward the darkening sky as if listening for something beneath the noise.

Hesthenes watched him until the crowd swallowed him again.

The old man's words from the ship returned without invitation.

*Truth remains.*

The procession turned into Curetes Street.

Here Ephesus displayed another face: wealthy houses, polished doors, painted walls, bronze lamps already being lit for the evening. From upper windows women in bright garments leaned out laughing and calling down to the men below. Perfume drifted from balconies wrapped in flowers.

They were passing the pleasure houses.

Some of the women recognized Andronicus's rank, if not his name, and waved with practiced warmth. Painted tiles near the entrances bore names, symbols, and promises. A few young men in the street called up with coins already in hand.

Andronicus answered the women with a polite lift of his hand.

A Roman nobleman was expected to know how to move through such streets without appearing either shocked or eager.

Then he drew his horse to a halt.

Ahead, on a terrace that had not existed when he left, stood a massive temple. Its columns were new, its steps broad, its platform lit by torches. Beside it rose the gigantic image of a man.

Andronicus looked up.

"That was not here before."

Lucius lowered his voice.

"The Temple of the Divine Emperor Domitian. Citizens passing this road are expected to honor the emperor's image."

The stone face of Domitian stared down upon Ephesus with cold authority.

Lord and god.

That was how the emperor wished to be addressed now. Not merely ruler. Not merely Caesar. Lord and god.

People passing beneath the terrace bowed their heads almost without pausing.

Some did it from loyalty.

Some from fear.

Most because everyone else did.

Andronicus watched them.

The temple did not feel like devotion. It felt like a question being asked in public, over and over, until every citizen learned the safest answer.

A line of incense burners stood before the lower steps. A boy no older than twelve guided his father toward them. The man pinched incense between his fingers, cast it into the flame, and bowed. The smoke rose thin and obedient.

Lucius spoke carefully.

"The governor encourages visible loyalty. Especially now."

"Now?"

"There has been unrest in other provinces. And the Christians continue to cause difficulty."

The officer riding ahead looked back with a short laugh.

"Difficulty? They are fools. A grain of incense would save them from questioning. But they would rather suffer than perform one harmless courtesy."

Andronicus did not answer.

He knew little of Christians beyond rumor: stubborn people, secretive meals, strange hymns, refusal at public rites, a crucified god, and a loyalty that did not fit neatly into Roman categories.

Yet the officer's words unsettled him.

Not because he admired the Christians.

He did not understand them.

But there was something disturbing about men and women willing to suffer for what could not be seen.

The escort moved again beneath the shadow of the imperial temple.

Hesthenes looked back once more.

Far behind them, nearly lost among worshipers, merchants, soldiers, priests, and dancers, the old traveler continued along the street. He moved without urgency, untouched by the fever of Artemis or the cold command of Domitian's image.

He seemed small in the crowd.

Yet to Hesthenes, the crowd suddenly seemed less certain because he was in it.

The first stars appeared over Ephesus.

Festival noise rolled through the streets behind them. Torches flickered against marble. Smoke from Artemis's sacrifice mingled with incense offered to Caesar. The city shone with wealth, order, appetite, and faith in itself.

Ahead, beyond the crowded avenues and towering façades, the estate of the Andronicus family emerged beneath the darkening sky.

Torchlight burned along the outer walls.

Servants moved through the courtyards, preparing for the return of the household's heir.

Andronicus slowed his horse.

For six years he had imagined this moment.

Now that it had come, he found no rest in it.

Ephesus was more beautiful than memory.

More prosperous.

More alive.

And yet, beneath the music, the incense, the laughter, the blood, and the marble, something in the city felt strained—as though all its splendor were being held together by voices too loud to stop.

Behind him, somewhere in the festival darkness, Tychicus kept walking.

Not like a stranger arriving in a city.

Like a witness returning to a place where something unfinished still waited.

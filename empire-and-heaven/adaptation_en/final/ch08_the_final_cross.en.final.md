# Empire and Heaven (Volume I)
## Chapter 8 — The Final Cross

After landing at Puteoli, the party traveled through Capua and took the Appian Way toward Rome.

The road was old, older than the ambitions of many men who had marched upon it. Four centuries earlier, Appius Claudius had driven this stone artery southward so Rome might govern what it had conquered. Its dark blocks of volcanic stone lay fitted together with such severity that wagons, legions, merchants, slaves, exiles, prisoners, and triumphant commanders had all passed over them as though the road itself cared nothing for the feet it bore.

Olive trees lined the way in places. Evergreen shrubs grew low beside the stones. Here and there oleander lifted its flowers in the heat.

Andronicus had offered John a horse.

The old apostle refused.

He also refused to travel as an honored guest. He insisted that he was being taken to Rome under imperial interest and should therefore walk as one in custody. Andronicus argued at first, then yielded. He ordered the soldiers to bind John's hands loosely enough not to injure him.

So John walked.

Beside him went Tychicus and Prochorus, their faces set in troubled silence. Others in Ephesus had wanted to follow, but the elders had chosen carefully: Tychicus knew the roads and dangers of Rome, and Prochorus had the courage—and perhaps the stubbornness—to remain near the old apostle whatever came.

Prochorus still watched every turn of the road as if it might offer a chance to flee.

If one moment opened, if one careless guard looked away, he told himself he would throw John over his own back and run into the fields, into vineyards, into forest, into death if necessary.

But John walked as though he had no wish to be rescued.

His face was full of memories.

The Appian Way was a road of triumph.

Generals who had broken foreign kings had traveled this road while Rome waited at the gates with laurel, incense, and cheers. Caesar had passed this way. Antony had passed this way. Octavian, after defeating Antony, had passed this way. Vespasian had come along roads like this when imperial power turned toward him. Titus, destroyer of Jerusalem, had entered Rome in glory after reducing the city of God to ash and lamentation.

But the Appian Way was also a road of crosses.

After Spartacus and the slave rebels were crushed, Pompey had lined the road with thousands of crucified bodies so Rome could teach obedience through spectacle. Six thousand crosses had stood here once, the dying arranged as a warning along the queen of roads.

John looked down at the black stones beneath his feet.

Peter had walked toward Rome.
Paul had walked toward Rome.

Both had entered the city not to triumph over it, but to be poured out inside it.

Peter, the fisherman who had followed Jesus with open heart and foolish courage, had come here to die. Paul, the scholar of Gamaliel who had become a wandering leatherworker and apostle to the nations, had come here to finish his race beneath the sword.

And now John, son of Zebedee, was walking the same road.

For many years he had watched Vespasian's rise with the concentration of hatred.

When John had been fleeing with Mary, hungry and hunted after Stephen's death, Vespasian had been rising through the cavalry and offices of Rome. When John had wandered through Samaria, guarding the mother of the Lord from danger, Vespasian had married well and advanced. When believers hid, scattered, prayed, and buried their dead, Vespasian marched, commanded, received honors, and moved toward the power that would one day place his family over the world.

At one time John had measured his own losses against the Roman's ascent and felt madness burn inside him.

He had wanted revenge.

He had wanted Rome's marble to crack, its generals to fall, its eagles to burn. He had watched for news of Vespasian the way a starving man watches for bread—not to be fed, but to keep hatred alive.

Then Jerusalem rebelled.

Then Rome burned.

Then Peter died.

Then Paul died.

Then Timothy was killed in Ephesus.

And still he had lived.

He had once tried to run toward the revolt, toward Galilee, toward the old fire of vengeance. Mary had stopped him. The memory still wounded him. She had not argued politics. She had only asked him to remain faithful to what her Son had entrusted to him from the cross.

*Behold your mother.*

For years that command had held him more firmly than chains.

When Mary died among the olives of Ephesus, the old fire had tried to rise again. John had gone as far as Antioch, intending to join the struggle against Rome. There, on a marble street near the Orontes, he had seen Vespasian at last.

Not in dream.
Not in memory.

On horseback, surrounded by officers, already marked by destiny.

John had stood in the road and stared at the man who had haunted half his life. Vespasian had looked down at him without recognition.

That had been the wound deeper than hatred.

To be consumed by a man who did not even remember you.

Now, walking the Appian Way under loose bonds, John saw how small his vengeance had been. Small, and yet terrible enough to have ruled him.

The Lord had not preserved him for vengeance.

The Lord had preserved him for witness.

A wagon passed, its driver shouting at oxen. Dust rose and settled over the travelers' sandals. Roman soldiers rode ahead and behind. Drusiana traveled in a covered carriage with a maidservant and young Gaius. Hesthenes rode near Andronicus, who watched the old apostle more often than he wished to admit.

John's pace was slow but steady.

At midday they rested beneath a cluster of trees.

Prochorus brought him water.

"Apostle, please sit longer."

John accepted the cup and smiled faintly.

"If I sit too long, you will begin planning how to carry me away."

Prochorus flushed.

Tychicus laughed softly, though there was grief in it.

"He has planned that since Puteoli."

"Then he is faithful," John said. "But this road is not his to break."

"Must every road lead to surrender?" Prochorus asked.

John looked at him.

"No. Some roads lead to obedience. They appear similar only from a distance."

The words gave Prochorus no comfort, but he stored them away.

Andronicus approached a little later.

He had removed his riding cloak, and the sun caught the metal fittings of his belt. He looked every inch the Roman official: disciplined, controlled, responsible for the safe movement of a difficult party toward the heart of the Empire.

Yet something in his face had changed since Ephesus.

"You are not required to remain bound," he said to John. "No one here doubts your willingness to come."

"I asked for the bonds."

"Why?"

"Because Rome should see what it does, even when it calls it courtesy."

Andronicus looked away.

"You speak as if I am Rome."

"You are not Rome. But Rome speaks through you often enough that you must learn when it is using your mouth."

The answer angered him. It also refused to leave him.

"You came willingly," Andronicus said.

"Yes."

"Then what does Rome do?"

John lifted his bound hands slightly.

"It calls a witness and thinks it has summoned a prisoner."

Hesthenes, listening from behind, lowered his eyes to hide whatever expression had crossed his face.

Andronicus was silent for a while.

Then he said, "You believe you may die in Rome."

"Many better men have."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the only answer an old man needs."

Before Andronicus could respond, Gaius came running from the carriage with bread wrapped in cloth. He stopped when he saw the soldiers and slowed his steps.

"Drusiana said the apostle should eat."

John received the bread with both hands, though the cord around his wrists made the gesture awkward.

"Tell Drusiana she still feeds more people than she knows."

Gaius nodded solemnly, then looked at the bonds.

"Do they hurt?"

"Not much."

"Why do you wear them?"

Andronicus stiffened slightly.

John considered the boy.

"So that I will remember the brothers who wore worse ones before me."

Gaius looked troubled.

"Will Rome hurt you?"

"Rome can only do what God permits."

That answer did not satisfy the child, but children recognize when adults are hiding grief inside truth. He went back quietly.

That evening, they lodged near a roadside villa whose owner owed Andronicus's family favor. The party was received properly. The Roman travelers were given clean rooms and warm food. John, Tychicus, and Prochorus were offered a corner of the servants' quarters. Andronicus, after one glance at Drusiana's face, ordered that they be treated as his guests.

The villa steward obeyed, confused and uneasy.

Later, when most of the house had settled, John stood in the courtyard beneath the stars.

Tychicus came beside him.

"Do you remember Paul's first sight of Rome?"

"I was not there," John said.

"No. But I remember what Luke told me. Brothers came out to meet him as far as the Forum of Appius and the Three Taverns. When Paul saw them, he thanked God and took courage."

John looked toward the dark line of the road.

"Will brothers come for us?"

"If they know."

"And if they do not?"

Tychicus smiled with old sadness.

"Then Rome will still not be empty. There are prayers beneath it now. More than Caesar guesses."

They stood silently.

The stars above Italy did not differ from the stars above Ephesus, Galilee, or Patmos. Yet roads made men feel the world was large; the heavens made it small again.

John touched the shell beneath his tunic.

Sophia. Mary. Peter. Paul. Timothy. Onesiphorus. Onesimus. Tychicus beside him still. Polycarp left behind. Callimachus vanished into his wound. Drusiana entering Rome as a bride. Andronicus carrying Rome without knowing how heavy it had become.

All the names moved through him like beads on a cord.

He had outlived too many.

"Lord," he whispered, "if this is the last road, let me walk it without hatred."

The next days hardened into rhythm.

Morning departure. Dust. Hoofbeats. Wheels over stone. Brief shade. Roman way stations. Soldiers exchanging news. Merchants asking questions. Drusiana watching the road from behind the curtain of the carriage. Gaius growing restless, then solemn, then curious again. Prochorus counting guards and opportunities. Hesthenes saying little. Andronicus reading dispatches by lamplight, then looking toward John as though the old man were a question the Empire had not taught him to answer.

At the Forum of Appius, they stopped again.

There, traders filled the air with noise. Muleteers cursed. Sailors from the canal bargained over transport. Women sold fruit and oil cakes. Soldiers drank sour wine and exaggerated their campaigns. Rome drew everything toward itself: grain, wealth, petitioners, captives, ambitions, rumors, gods.

A few men near a stall watched John too closely.

Tychicus noticed first.

Then Prochorus.

Then Hesthenes.

Andronicus moved before anyone else could. He stepped between the watchers and the old apostle with the easy authority of rank.

"Is there something you wish to report?" he asked.

The men lowered their eyes and withdrew.

John observed this quietly.

"You protect what you are bringing to judgment."

Andronicus did not turn.

"I protect those under my charge."

"Even when the charge troubles you?"

"Especially then."

John said nothing more.

But that night, when the travelers slept, Andronicus remained awake longer than usual.

Rome had always been a shape of certainty to him. Office, law, discipline, road, army, ambition, honor. Yet the closer they came to the city, the less certain those words felt. They did not collapse. They thinned. He could see through them now to the fear that made men build permanence out of stone.

John had said on the sea that empires rise loudly and fade in silence.

The road under Andronicus's feet had been built to deny that truth.

Still, the old man's words walked beside him.

On the final morning before Rome, the air changed.

Everyone felt it. Traffic thickened. Villas appeared more frequently along the road. Tombs stood beside the way, some simple, some proud, all testifying that even Rome's great families could not command the dead to remain.

The city was near.

John stopped once beside a weathered roadside cross, long empty now, its wood blackened and half rotted.

No one knew whose body had hung there.

Perhaps a slave.
Perhaps a rebel.
Perhaps a thief no one remembered.

John looked at it for a long time.

Prochorus moved closer, afraid the sight had wounded him.

But the old apostle's face was calm.

"Rome thinks the cross is its final word," John said.

No one answered.

"It has never understood how God uses Rome's final words."

Then he continued walking.

By late afternoon the walls and smoke of Rome lay ahead.

The road descended toward the city that claimed the earth.

Drusiana drew back the curtain of the carriage. Gaius leaned beside her, silent for once. Tychicus closed his eyes. Prochorus tightened his hands. Hesthenes watched the gates with the caution of a man entering a beast's mouth. Andronicus sat straight in the saddle, his face once more disciplined into the mask Rome expected of him.

John walked between soldiers with loose bonds on his wrists.

He did not look like a prisoner entering the capital.

He looked like a fisherman who had followed one final summons.

The Appian Way bore him onward over its dark stones, past tombs, villas, memories, victories, and crosses, toward Rome.

# Empire and Heaven (Volume I)
## Chapter 9 — The Emperor and the Fisherman

Emperor Domitian slept long into the morning.

The sun had already risen high over Rome, spilling light across the Circus Maximus and the roofs beyond the Palatine, but the ruler of the world lay sweating beneath linen sheets. His thick neck and ruddy face gave him the appearance of health, almost of abundance. Yet cold sweat gathered on his brow as if his body knew what his title refused to confess.

At the window, Empress Domitilla stood watching the city.

Rome was already awake. Servants moved through corridors. Guards changed posts. Wheels sounded faintly from the streets below. In the palace, no one truly slept when Caesar slept. Men whispered, waited, listened, and tried to discover what mood would govern the day.

Domitilla turned from the window, sat beside the bed, and touched a cloth to the emperor's forehead.

Domitian's breath stopped.

His right hand slid beneath the pillow.

"My lord," she said quickly. "Be still. It is Domitilla."

His eyes opened.

For one instant he looked at her as though she had emerged from a conspiracy, not from marriage. His fingers tightened around the dagger he had drawn from beneath the pillow.

"You were watching me."

"You were sweating. I wiped your brow."

"Why were you awake?"

She managed a smile.

"It is late morning. In this palace, I believe only Your Majesty was asleep."

He looked toward the window, displeased.

"Only I slept while everyone else remained awake. That is an unpleasant thought. Next time Caesar sleeps, perhaps the whole palace should sleep with him."

"If the lower servants sleep too much, they may only become lazy," Domitilla said, careful to make the words light. "Or produce too many children."

"This is not a morning for jokes."

He returned the dagger beneath the pillow but did not remove his hand at once.

Domitilla clapped for the attendants.

"The emperor has risen."

Servants entered with bowls, cloths, oils, razors, powders, and instruments of care. Domitian submitted to them with a stillness more dangerous than anger. His face was washed, his beard shaved, his skin anointed. Nails were trimmed. Limbs were rubbed. The machinery of imperial dignity began its daily work over a man who had woken with a knife in his hand.

A wheeled breakfast table was brought in.

There was little on it: fig juice and a small plate of seasoned asparagus. Domitian had long feared the softening effects of excess upon the body, though excess surrounded him in every stone of the palace.

Domitilla sat nearby while he drank.

"Did the dreams trouble you again?" she asked.

"I govern the world," he said. "Do you imagine such a man sleeps peacefully?"

"The world is in Rome's hand. No province is openly in revolt. The citizens obey. The Senate bends when it must. Perhaps Your Majesty may allow himself some rest."

He turned on her sharply.

"That is precisely how rulers rot. A man who commands others must never sink into comfort. Ease breeds softness. Softness breeds contempt. Contempt breeds daggers."

The last word remained in the room.

Before Domitilla could answer, a chamberlain entered and bowed.

"Musicius, liaison of the Third District, requests audience."

The Third District meant Ionia and Asia Minor.

Domitian set down the cup.

"Send him in."

Musicius entered in a plain white toga. Nothing in his dress declared power. That was by design. Men who lived by information survived by appearing ordinary. He bowed deeply.

"You came early," Domitian said.

"I arrived earlier and waited outside, Majesty."

"Asia. Report."

"The province remains outwardly calm."

"Outwardly?"

"Governor Lycomedes presents no visible defect. His administration appears loyal. However, certain Greek philosophers have been seen entering the governor's residence from time to time. Apollonius among them."

Domitian's eyes narrowed.

"Apollonius. The philosopher with an appetite for contradiction."

"He is admired by those who dislike obedience, Majesty."

"Then plant men closer to Lycomedes. Not outer servants. Inner ones. Men who hear after wine, before sleep, and at doors believed to be empty."

Musicius smiled faintly.

"It has already been arranged."

Domitian nodded.

"Good. And Andronicus?"

"Quaestor Marius Andronicus obeyed the imperial instruction. He arrested the old man called John of Ephesus and arrived in Rome yesterday. The prisoner has been placed in the Mamertine prison."

Domitian leaned back.

"His conduct?"

"No open irregularity. In Ephesus he devoted himself chiefly to locating John. We have no report of secret meetings with seditious elements."

"And when John was taken? The Christians?"

"Andronicus handled the matter with considerable political skill. On the day of his marriage, he invited the Ephesian orphans into his household and gained the sympathy of the Christians. He persuaded John to accompany him voluntarily rather than provoke a public struggle."

"Marriage?"

"Yes, Majesty. The day after reaching Ephesus, he married Drusiana, daughter of the house of Tyrannus. The governor, scholars, and notable citizens attended the public ceremony. A private Christian blessing was also held afterward, though no arrests were made."

Domitian looked toward Domitilla.

"An Ephesian bride."

The empress understood the tone.

"A pity. A man seeking greatness in Rome ought to choose a Roman house."

"Perhaps he lacks ambition."

Musicius lifted his head slightly.

"Majesty, I would not conclude that too quickly."

Domitian's eyes sharpened.

"No?"

"Men with political instinct sometimes avoid the center while they build safety elsewhere. Your father, before he was emperor, knew how to appear provincial when Rome was dangerous."

The comparison displeased Domitian, though he did not reject it.

"My father did not marry like a provincial."

"No, Majesty. But Andronicus's marriage may not indicate dullness. It may indicate calculation—or sentiment carefully used."

"Sentiment is useful only when it belongs to others."

"Just so."

Domitian drank the last of the fig juice and motioned for the table to be removed.

"What else?"

"Before entering Rome by the Appian Way, Andronicus was received at the villa of the former consul Flavius Clemens."

The room changed.

Domitilla's hand tightened around the cloth in her lap.

Domitian turned slowly.

"My cousin's house received him?"

"More precisely, the invitation appears to have come from Flavia Domitilla. The party remained some time inside. We do not know the substance of the conversation."

"Why not?"

Musicius bowed lower.

"The brothers Nereus and Achilleus, formerly attached to Clemens's household, kept the grounds tightly watched. Since our men lacked official cause to enter openly, close approach was difficult."

"Difficult." Domitian repeated the word as if tasting poison.

"We judge the matter less dangerous than it might appear," Musicius said quickly. "If Andronicus had old ties to the house of Clemens, the principal man has already fallen under suspicion and will no longer serve as an axis of influence. More likely, Domitilla wished to see John. She is believed to favor the Christians."

"Believed?"

"Strongly suspected."

Domitian rose.

The attendants withdrew without being told. Domitilla remained where she was, pale but composed.

"Christians," the emperor said. "Always Christians. They refuse the gods, refuse civic rites, refuse the genius of Caesar, and yet they breed in houses of rank as easily as in alleys."

"Their stubbornness is their danger," Musicius said. "They appear weak, but they obey another loyalty."

"No loyalty exists above Caesar."

"That is precisely the point on which they fail, Majesty."

Domitian walked to the window and looked toward the Circus.

Below, Rome moved under the light. A city of stone, smoke, desire, law, victory, hunger, and fear. From this height it seemed governable. Men were always easier to govern from a height.

"John," he said. "What is he?"

"Very old. A fisherman from Galilee by origin. One of the earliest followers of the crucified Jesus. The Christians in Asia treat him as the last living witness."

"A fisherman."

Domitian smiled without warmth.

"The Empire has grown anxious over a fisherman."

"Not over the man alone. Over what men believe he remembers."

The answer held Domitian's attention.

Memory was dangerous. Rome understood monuments, records, decrees, triumphal arches, inscriptions carved in stone. But memory that passed from mouth to mouth, table to table, prison to prison—memory that did not require permission—was harder to command.

"He predicted my death?" Domitian asked.

Musicius hesitated.

"There are reports he spoke against Your Majesty's fate before leaving Asia. The accounts differ."

"They always differ when cowards repeat treason."

He turned from the window.

"I will see this fisherman."

Domitilla spoke before she could stop herself.

"Majesty, is it wise to trouble yourself with an old prisoner?"

Domitian looked at her.

The room went still.

"Do you plead for him?"

"No," she said. "I plead for imperial dignity. An emperor loses stature when he appears irritated by the aged and powerless."

It was a clever answer. Perhaps too clever.

Domitian studied her.

"An emperor loses more when powerless men become symbols."

He turned back to Musicius.

"Bring me fuller reports on Clemens's household, Flavia Domitilla's movements, Nereus, Achilleus, and every servant who has carried messages to Christian burial grounds. Watch Andronicus. He is talented, and talented men are never innocent by default. Watch his wife. Women often carry loyalties men underestimate."

"Yes, Majesty."

"And the boy?"

"A child called Gaius traveled with them from Ephesus. Formerly among the orphans under Drusiana's care."

"Children hear what adults conceal."

"He will be noted."

Domitian dismissed him.

Musicius backed from the room, bowing again before he disappeared.

When he was gone, the emperor remained standing.

Domitilla watched him carefully.

"You are troubled by shadows," she said at last.

"Shadows are where knives wait."

"Not every shadow hides treason."

"Only those who survive long enough to speak so gently believe that."

He touched the pillow where the dagger lay hidden.

"Rome endured because my father was not sentimental. My brother enjoyed affection and died before he understood what affection conceals. I will not rule by being loved."

"No," Domitilla said softly. "You rule by being feared."

He turned.

The silence that followed was dangerous.

Then he smiled.

"Fear is more reliable."

But after she left him, Domitian did not return to the table or call for papers.

He stood alone near the window, looking at the city he believed belonged to him. The morning light had grown white and hard. Statues gleamed. Roof tiles burned. Smoke lifted from thousands of hearths. Somewhere beneath that glitter, men and women gathered in rooms, crypts, kitchens, and courtyards to speak the name of a crucified Jew as Lord.

Lord.

The word belonged to Caesar.

Domitian's jaw tightened.

No fisherman would take it from him.


The Mamertine prison lay in shadow even at midday.

Stone held cold there the way memory holds grief. The passage down was narrow, damp, and dark enough that men entering from the sunlight felt at once that Rome had placed part of the underworld beneath its own heart.

John sat on the floor of the lower chamber.

His hands were no longer bound. The guards had found no need. Age itself looked like restraint, and the old man had not once tried to resist.

Prochorus had been refused entry. Tychicus had been turned away with him. Andronicus had argued for better conditions and won only a small lamp, water, and permission that food might be sent. Rome was sometimes generous in forms and merciless in substance.

John did not complain.

He leaned against the stone and listened.

Above him Rome moved.

He could not hear the whole city, but he could feel it: wheels, boots, voices, orders, bargains, accusations, prayers. The Empire did not sleep. It turned over itself endlessly, grinding men into usefulness or dust.

A guard opened the hatch above.

Light fell in a brief square.

"Old man," the guard called. "Caesar has asked about you."

John lifted his eyes.

The guard expected fear. He saw none.

"Then Caesar is nearer to mercy than he knows," John said.

The guard spat.

"You Christians speak strangely."

The hatch closed.

Darkness returned, softened only by the lamp.

John closed his eyes.

He thought of Galilee.

Nets. Boats. The smell of fish. His father Zebedee's voice. James laughing. Peter shouting before thinking. Jesus standing on a shore as though every road in the world began from His feet.

How far a fisherman could be carried.

From Galilee to Jerusalem.
From Jerusalem to Ephesus.
From Ephesus to Rome.
From ambition to obedience.
From thunder to love.

He smiled faintly.

The emperor would see a prisoner.

John would see a man afraid to sleep.

And somewhere beyond both of them, the kingdom that did not tremble continued to come quietly, as it always had: through witness, through suffering, through bread shared in hidden rooms, through names Rome could not keep from being remembered.

The lamp flickered against the prison wall.

John waited.

# Empire and Heaven (Volume I)
## Chapter 6 — Lost Coordinates

The old wall of Lysimachus ran along the ridge of Mount Coressus like a broken spine.

Below it lay Ephesus, bright and restless in the noonday heat. Above it, the mountain kept the memory of another city. Before the harbor had widened, before the new streets had been forced toward Mount Pion, people had lived among these slopes. Now only weed-choked paths, collapsed stones, shallow caves, and bits of wall remained.

Two riders moved through the ruins.

Branches scraped at their shoulders. Dry grass brushed against the horses' legs. The path was narrow enough that one careless turn might send horse and rider against stone.

Andronicus lifted a branch from his face.

"Hesthenes, how did you learn their meeting place was here?"

"Careful, Dominus. That thorn will cost you more blood than the Christians will."

"You returned to Ephesus only last night."

"And you returned as Rome's rising star. It is only proper that your servant should rise in usefulness with you."

"Too useful an informant leaves blood behind him," Andronicus said.

"Then let us hope I am useful enough to live and not useful enough to be noticed."

The answer was light, but Andronicus heard the caution beneath it.

Domitian's informers were everywhere. The Empire had made suspicion a profession. Even men loyal to Rome learned to lower their voices.

They rode on beneath the broken wall.

"Will the old man still be there?" Andronicus asked.

"I believe so. Their Eucharist ended at dawn, but today is no ordinary gathering."

Andronicus turned in the saddle.

"No ordinary gathering?"

Hesthenes looked offended at himself.

"There. I have spoken too much again."

"You followed Tychicus last night."

"I never said that."

"You did not have to."

A faint smile touched Hesthenes's mouth.

"Then let us speak of what you must do. You are going to meet John and ask him to preside over your wedding. That is your purpose. Nothing more."

"I know my purpose."

"Good. Then do not allow ambition to discover a second one."

Andronicus looked at him sharply.

Hesthenes continued as if he had not noticed.

"If you find not only John but half the Christian leaders of Asia in one cave, do not begin counting arrests."

"Do you think so poorly of my honor?"

"I think very highly of your future. That is the trouble. Rome rewards men who see opportunities before other men see them."

The horses stepped over a scatter of fallen stones.

Andronicus was silent.

The servant's warning irritated him because it was not foolish. He had come seeking a wedding, not a triumph. Yet the Empire had trained him to recognize value in every circumstance. A secret Christian gathering in the hills of Ephesus would be valuable to the governor, valuable to the emperor's agents, valuable to any young official wishing to show zeal.

But Drusiana's face rose before him.

Not Rome.

Drusiana.

"I will not arrest them," he said.

"Then your wedding may still happen."

"Explain."

"If Drusiana is to be pleased, you must invite those she loves."

"Her family will be invited. The governor as well. Scholars, magistrates, officers—"

"I did not ask whom Rome would admire. I asked whom Drusiana loves."

Andronicus frowned.

"The Christians."

"Yes. The same people who would rather face beasts in the arena than step willingly into a Roman official's house."

"If I guarantee their safety?"

"They may believe you. Jews are inconveniently serious about promises. Christians worse still."

"And after I leave Ephesus? Their identities would be known."

"That is why the wedding must be held twice."

Andronicus drew up his horse.

"Twice?"

Hesthenes stopped ahead of him and turned.

"Once at the house of Tyrannus, before the governor, your mother's friends, the city's learned men, and every person who must be satisfied that Marius Andronicus has married properly. Then, when the official rites are completed, you and Drusiana go to your family's estate above the theater. There, separately, John may bless the marriage before the Christians. No one from the first gathering needs to see the second."

"You have planned this already."

"Lucius has been preparing both houses for a month. I only improved the meaning of his labor."

Andronicus stared at him.

"You are becoming dangerous."

"Only in service of your happiness, Dominus."

"I have never heard of a man marrying the same woman twice in one day."

"For Drusiana, I suspect you would do it a hundred times if necessary."

The remark carried too much truth to dismiss.

Andronicus urged his horse forward again.

"You said there is a special gathering today. What is happening?"

Hesthenes hesitated.

"Since you have pledged Roman honor, I will answer. Important men from their churches have come to Ephesus. Bishops from other cities. Teachers. Witnesses."

"For a rebellion?"

"No. Something more dangerous to them."

"What?"

"Their own uncertainty."

Andronicus looked ahead.

The ruined path bent upward through scrub and broken stone.

Hesthenes lowered his voice.

"Their strength is built on the resurrection of Jesus. They believe He died, rose, remained forty days, and ascended. They also believe He promised to return before this generation passed away. But the years have passed. Those who saw Him are nearly all dead. The churches now belong to men who know Him through memory, letters, and stories. Some are beginning to explain Him according to their own minds."

"So they quarrel over absence."

"They quarrel over meaning. That is worse."

Andronicus considered this.

He had thought of Christians as obstinate, secretive, politically troublesome. He had not imagined them as fragile. Yet perhaps any loyalty built around a dead and unseen king carried within it a danger Rome did not understand.

Rome had statues.
Rome had armies.
Rome had coinage, law, roads, oaths, and the emperor's image.

The Christians had memory.

And memory could change hands.

"If they are divided," he said, "why should I fear them?"

"Because divided men are unpredictable. And faithful men, divided or not, are harder to frighten than ambitious ones."

They reached a rise where the old road widened briefly. From there Ephesus could be seen below: the harbor, the theater, the imperial temple, the squares, the long bright streets. Festival smoke still hung faintly over the city.

Above the glitter, the ruined mountain was silent.

Hesthenes pointed with his chin.

"There."

Beyond a cluster of fig trees, half hidden by stones and brush, was the mouth of a cave.

It did not look like an entrance to anything important. A shepherd might have passed it without thought. Yet the ground nearby bore signs of careful use: trampled grass, a recently moved stone, faint marks where sandals had come and gone in numbers larger than the path itself could explain.

Andronicus dismounted.

So did Hesthenes.

They tied the horses among the trees and approached on foot.

The cave mouth gave little light. Cool air breathed from within, carrying the smell of earth, lamp smoke, and human presence. Voices sounded somewhere deeper inside—not loud, but many.

Andronicus paused.

The young Roman had entered governor's halls, barracks, courts, and temples without hesitation. But before this rough opening in an abandoned hillside, he felt the odd sensation that he was approaching a border no map had marked.

"Remember," Hesthenes murmured beside him. "A wedding. Not a conquest."

Andronicus gave him a cold look.

Then he stepped into the dark.

The passage bent inward and down. For several paces the light from outside followed them, weakening with each turn. Then the tunnel opened suddenly into a chamber whose walls had been cut from old stone and patched in places by human hands.

Oil lamps burned along the walls.

Men were gathered there.

John sat near the far side, white-haired and still, his hands resting on his knees. Around him were Onesimus of Ephesus, Ignatius of Antioch, Sosthenes of Corinth, Papias of Hierapolis, Polycarp, Prochorus, Tychicus, and others whom Andronicus did not know. Rolls of papyrus lay on a low table. A loaf of bread had been broken. A cup stood beside it.

The conversation stopped the moment Andronicus entered.

No sword was drawn.

No one fled.

That stillness unsettled him more than panic would have done.

Polycarp rose first.

"Quaestor Andronicus."

His voice was polite, but guarded.

Hesthenes remained half a step behind his master.

Andronicus bowed with formal precision.

"Forgive the intrusion. I come under my own word, not under warrant."

Several men exchanged glances.

Onesimus stood.

"A Roman official's word can mean many things."

"Today it means safety."

John looked at him then.

The old apostle's gaze did not accuse him. It simply received him, and somehow that was harder.

"Why have you come?" John asked.

Andronicus drew a breath.

The speech he had prepared seemed suddenly too polished for the stone room.

"I have asked for Drusiana's hand," he said. "Her family has consented, but I would not have the marriage wound her conscience or her love for those gathered here. I have come to ask whether you will bless the marriage."

The room remained silent.

Polycarp's face tightened. Prochorus glanced toward him. Tychicus lowered his eyes as though he had expected pain but not the form it had taken.

John said nothing for a long moment.

At last he asked, "Does Drusiana desire this?"

"I believe she is willing."

"Willing is not always the same as free."

Andronicus felt the words strike his pride.

He answered carefully.

"I will not force her."

"Rome often does not call its force by that name."

The men in the room watched him.

A flush rose in Andronicus's face, but he held his voice steady.

"Then judge me by what I ask, not by what Rome has done. I ask for your presence. I ask for the presence of her brothers and sisters. I give my word that any Christian who comes under my roof for this wedding will leave unharmed and unnamed."

Onesimus looked at Hesthenes.

"And who advised this wise promise?"

Hesthenes bowed slightly.

"A servant who dislikes unnecessary disasters."

Despite the tension, a few faint smiles moved around the chamber.

John's eyes remained on Andronicus.

"And the governor?"

"He will attend the official ceremony at the school of Tyrannus. The gathering of believers will be separate, at my family's estate. No record will be made. No soldier will be stationed inside."

"You offer much," Ignatius said.

"I ask much."

"You ask the church to enter the house of a Roman official in a time when Christians are dying in Rome."

"Yes."

The honesty of the answer weighed more than persuasion.

Papias, who had been silent, spoke quietly.

"Why do you want this blessing? A Roman marriage does not require it."

Andronicus looked toward the broken bread on the low table.

"Because Drusiana does."

Then, after a pause, "And because I do not understand the world that has shaped her. If I am to marry her, I cannot pretend that world does not exist."

This answer changed the room, but only slightly.

John leaned back, his old hands folded now.

"A man may enter another world out of love," he said. "But he may also try to bring it under his own roof and call that love."

Andronicus had no easy answer.

For the first time since entering the chamber, he allowed his uncertainty to show.

"Then teach me the difference."

Polycarp looked up sharply.

The request was dangerous, though perhaps not in the way Rome understood danger.

John regarded Andronicus a long time.

"Marriage is not possession," he said. "A wife is not an ornament given to complete a man's honor. She is one entrusted by God, and the one who receives her must learn to die to himself."

The words were simple. To Andronicus they sounded more severe than any legal contract.

"I will remember," he said.

"Remembering will not be enough."

"Then I will learn."

Again the old apostle studied him.

At last John turned to Onesimus.

"What do you say?"

The bishop of Ephesus was slow to answer.

"Drusiana is beloved among us. If she has consented freely, we should not deny her joy. But the danger is real."

"Danger has never left us," Ignatius said.

Sosthenes added, "A promise given openly must bind the one who gives it."

Tychicus looked toward Andronicus.

"Some promises are tested only after they become inconvenient."

"Then let mine be tested," Andronicus said.

John closed his eyes for a brief moment.

When he opened them, the decision had been made.

"If Drusiana consents freely, I will bless the marriage. And those who choose to attend may do so under your promise."

Andronicus bowed his head.

"You have my gratitude."

"Do not give gratitude too quickly," John said. "A blessing does not make a path easy. It only names the One before whom the path must be walked."

The chamber quieted again.

Outside, faintly through stone and distance, Ephesus still made its festival noise. The city shouted for Artemis, traded beneath Caesar's image, and glittered in the sun. Here, beneath abandoned rock, a Roman official had given his word to men his own emperor might call enemies.

No one mistook the arrangement for peace.

It was only a narrow passage, cut through stone, where trust might pass for one day without being crushed.

Andronicus turned to leave.

At the mouth of the tunnel, he looked back once.

John was watching him.

The old man's face held neither approval nor suspicion. It held something more difficult: grief disciplined by hope.

Hesthenes touched Andronicus's sleeve.

They stepped out into the noon light.

The ruins of the old city lay silent around them. Below, Ephesus shone as if nothing hidden could threaten it.

Andronicus untied his horse.

"You heard everything," he said.

"Yes, Dominus."

"What do you think?"

Hesthenes glanced toward the cave, then toward the bright city below.

"I think we have lost the coordinates."

Andronicus looked at him.

"Meaning?"

"Rome teaches a man where he stands. Rank, household, office, patron, province, emperor. Everything has a place. But these people stand somewhere else. Their map is not ours."

The words unsettled Andronicus more than he wished to admit.

He mounted.

"Then we had better find the road back before night."

Hesthenes swung into the saddle.

"Yes," he said. "If there is still a road."

They rode down from the ruins toward the city, leaving behind the cave, the broken wall, and the hidden gathering beneath the mountain.

Above them the old stones kept their silence.

Below them Ephesus glittered.

Between the two, the path narrowed and disappeared more than once, as though the mountain itself had forgotten where the way should be.

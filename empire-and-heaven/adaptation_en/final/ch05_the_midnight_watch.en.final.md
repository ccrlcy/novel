# Empire and Heaven (Volume I)
## Chapter 5 — The Midnight Watch

The cry of an owl passed through the olive trees and came thinly against the walls of the little room.

At the center stood a square table. Upon it burned a single lamp. Its flame wavered whenever the spring wind moved outside, and the five shadows seated around the table shuddered against the walls as if listening with them.

The oldest man sat farthest from the door.

His hair was white, his face deeply furrowed, but his eyes still flashed when he lifted them. Those who had known him long ago could not forget that once men had called him a son of thunder.

He turned to the man on his right.

"And what did Bishop Clement say?"

Sosthenes, bishop of Corinth, was not as old as John, though his hair and beard had already gone largely gray. He folded his hands on the table before answering.

"Clement of Rome has written to Corinth. He is deeply troubled. He fears the zeal of our church has begun to outrun wisdom."

Papias, the youngest among them, leaned forward.

"But if fasting, celibacy, and even the desire for martyrdom are spreading among the believers, is that not a sign of fervor? Persecution is increasing. The Lord's return is longed for more urgently than ever. Should faith not burn hotter in such an hour?"

Sosthenes looked first toward John, then back to Papias.

"Fervor is not the question," he said. "The Spirit is no invention of ours. The Lord promised the Advocate. Paul and the apostles preached with signs, healings, prophecies, and gifts. I do not deny any of it. But Corinth is a dangerous city for zeal without order."

He paused, and his gaze moved toward Onesimus of Ephesus.

"You know something of harbors here. Corinth is worse. Men pass through and leave nothing rooted behind them. Commerce, pleasure, argument, appetite—everything travels there, and very little remains pure. When Paul first came, he judged rightly that Corinth needed the gospel. He labored there with Aquila and Priscilla for a year and a half. Because the city was corrupt, many repented with great intensity. The church rose like fire."

"You were the ruler of the synagogue then," Onesimus said quietly.

"Yes. I heard Paul, received baptism, and was beaten for it. But the greater danger came after he left. Apollos came from Ephesus, and his eloquence moved many hearts. Then factions began. Some claimed Paul, some Apollos, some Peter, and some claimed Christ in a way that despised all the others."

The lamp fluttered.

"At first," Sosthenes said, "I thought our divisions came from the city's rootlessness. But I have come to believe the deeper problem was ungoverned fervor. When men call every impulse holy, the smallest disagreement becomes a wound. Habit, opinion, teaching, discipline—everything turns into a battlefield. That is how zeal becomes division. That is how division becomes false doctrine."

"That was when Paul wrote to Corinth from Ephesus," Onesimus said.

Sosthenes nodded.

"He told us to desire the gifts, but to pursue love. Prophecy and tongues were to build up the church, not scatter it."

Papias lowered his eyes.

"I remember the copy of that letter," he said. "God is not a God of confusion, but of peace."

For a moment the five men listened to the wind moving in the olive grove.

Ignatius of Antioch, who had remained silent across from Sosthenes, now spoke. He was already old, though not as old as John. His voice was careful, as if each word had to pass first through prayer.

"Paul struck the heart of it. Gifts must be desired, but we must remember the hour in which we live. The church faces a danger greater than we wish to admit."

John turned toward him.

"Speak, Ignatius."

The old bishop inclined his head.

"You know Antioch well, Apostle. You lived among us for eleven years. In the early days many prophets served there. Agabus, who foretold the famine under Herod Agrippa and later the imprisonment of Paul, was from Antioch. Philip's four daughters, who now live near Hierapolis, also came from that soil. The church was filled with the Spirit, yet it did not break apart. Even the quarrel between Paul and Barnabas over Mark did not become a mortal wound. But now another sickness has appeared. Men are beginning to interpret Christ according to themselves."

He stopped and looked at John.

The old apostle sat half in shadow, his silver hair loosened about his face. Age had softened much in him, but the gaze remained strong enough to make younger men lower their eyes.

Ignatius was the only man in the room who had known John's earlier fire. He remembered the anger, the ambition, the quickness that once frightened even friends. Time had not erased the thunder. It had buried it under years of obedience.

"Perhaps," Ignatius continued, "this has come because the Lord's return has seemed delayed. On the Mount of Olives, He spoke of the things to come and said that this generation would not pass away until they were accomplished. Yet nearly sixty-five years have passed since His resurrection. Except for you, the apostles have gone to the Lord. Jerusalem has fallen. The fire of Rome, the burial of Pompeii, persecutions in city after city—signs have not ceased. But the Lord has not yet come as many expected. And now Domitian sharpens the sword again. Men who cannot bear waiting begin inventing explanations."

Papias lifted his head.

"Then the danger is inside us. We can endure sword, beast, flame, prison—but if Christ Himself is altered among us, what remains to die for? We must guard Him. We must guard His true face with our lives."

John closed his eyes.

A memory rose in him—not this room, not this night, but the shore of Tiberias and a voice he had never escaped.

*Feed my sheep.*

Now Papias, with the urgency of youth, had placed that burden before him again. Not merely to tend souls. To guard Christ from the hands of those who loved their own interpretations more than truth.

Ignatius spoke again.

"Nicholas of Antioch may have begun with necessity. Perhaps even with sincerity. But excessive zeal opened the door. To despise the body as evil and exalt the spirit alone; to say the law has no place; to turn liberty into disorder—these things do not remain harmless. They deform the life of the church."

Onesimus of Ephesus, who had listened with grave attention, folded his hands.

"Those branches still spread among us. Marcion came recently to the apostle and explained his views. His love for Paul was fierce, but it had become narrow. He rejected the Scriptures of Israel entirely and pressed a severe celibate discipline as though only such renunciation could be pure. When the apostle judged his teaching unsound, he left in anger."

"Polycarp had words for him," Sosthenes said, and a brief smile passed through his severity.

Onesimus gave a reluctant laugh.

"Yes. As Marcion left, Polycarp cried after him, 'You firstborn of Satan!'"

A small ripple of laughter moved around the table and quickly faded.

Sosthenes shook his head.

"Marcion now travels about gathering disciples. He says those who cling to the rotten law are unworthy, and only Paul understood Christ truly."

Papias frowned.

"And the opposite error is no better. Near Smyrna some cling to the law as though the Messiah's coming depends upon their own purity. The apostle has warned them also."

Onesimus added, "Cerinthus still wanders near Ephesus. He says the Spirit of Christ descended upon Jesus at His baptism and left Him at the cross. If that is so, then the suffering and resurrection of the Lord become empty shadows."

Ignatius looked toward the lamp.

"The errors wear different clothes, but they spring from the same root. Some despise the body and run to extreme asceticism. Others say the body has nothing to do with salvation and fall into secret pleasure. In Thyatira, a woman they call Jezebel draws the fervent into rites of impurity under the name of revelation."

"More dangerous than Rome," Sosthenes said.

No one contradicted him.

Rome could kill the body. Falsehood could teach men to kneel before another Christ while using the name of the true one.

Ignatius turned again toward John.

"For this reason, I believe the authority of the bishops must be strengthened. Without shepherds, every fevered voice calls itself prophecy."

Sosthenes nodded.

"And more than that, Apostle, the churches need your voice. Those who saw the Lord have almost all departed. In this room only you and Ignatius saw Him with your own eyes. Your witness is not ornament. It is protection."

John remained silent.

The lamp moved. The owl cried again.

Papias spoke, more carefully now.

"The disciples here in Ephesus have gathered writings concerning the Lord. Mark's account, Matthew's account, Luke's Acts, letters from Paul, copies from many churches. But I ask something more. You are the last remaining apostle. Would you leave a record that completes what the others have written?"

The room fell still.

The question had arrived at last.

John opened his eyes and looked at Papias.

"Is there something lacking in Mark and Matthew?"

Papias took a breath.

"Not error. I would not say error. But there are silences. Anyone who has heard the stories from Jerusalem knows of Lazarus. His raising was known among the people. Many say it stirred the welcome that greeted the Lord and hardened the council's resolve to kill Him. Yet Mark and Matthew say nothing of it."

Ignatius leaned forward.

"I have studied Matthew closely. The events near the Feast of Dedication, the withdrawal beyond the Jordan, the matter of Lazarus—all are absent. Then the account moves to teachings in Galilee."

"And there is more," Papias said. "When the Lord entered Jerusalem, the people laid garments and branches before Him and cried, 'Hosanna to the Son of David.' Five days later they cried for His crucifixion. What happened between those cries? Why did He eat at Simon the leper's house in Bethany rather than Lazarus's? Why, after entering as Messiah, did He and the disciples leave Bethany hungry enough that He cursed the fig tree?"

His voice was quiet, but the questions were sharp.

All eyes turned to John.

For a long time the old apostle did not answer.

Then he said, "Papias, you have studied much."

"In Hierapolis, Philip's daughters still live. Through them I have collected many accounts from Jerusalem and Caesarea. I do not ask from curiosity. I ask because the churches are being pressed by confusion."

John looked down at the trembling flame.

When he finally spoke, his voice had changed. It was lower, and the years lay heavily upon it.

"Shameful as it is to say, we ourselves did not understand who He was. Most of us were fishermen from Galilee. He called, and we followed. We saw signs and thought perhaps, when His kingdom came, we might gain places beneath Him. We interpreted Him as others now interpret Him—according to ourselves. That was where many of our sins began."

No one moved.

"What is an idol?" John said. "Is it not a god shaped by human thought? Men long for God and then carve what their minds prefer. So the gods of nations differ from one another, and their gods go to war as their men do. But Israel was called to receive God as He reveals Himself, not invent Him. I AM WHO I AM. Scripture is not man's portrait of God; it is man's trembling record of God's speech."

His eyes brightened.

"Christ had to come. He had to be born of a virgin. Because He is truth, power had to be in Him. Because He came as light into darkness, He had to suffer. Because truth cannot die, He had to rise. Because the Spirit was to be sent, He had to ascend. These things were not arranged by human understanding. They were fulfilled because God is God."

The room seemed smaller around the words.

"The errors troubling the churches are born from the same arrogance as idols. Men make God with their own minds. Simon the magician thought the gift of the Spirit could be bought like a skill. Others take Greek knowledge of nature and try to reason their way upward into God. But truth imagined by man remains dead truth. We do not find God by mastering the universe. We know the universe rightly only after God has first made Himself known."

Papias heard in the words the answer he had hoped for, but not the consent he desired.

"Then, Apostle," he said, "this is why your record is needed. The churches must preserve the living truth of Jesus without injury. You must leave this for us."

John looked at him gently.

"Mark and Matthew have not injured Him. Their records are faithful. Paul's letters also witness to the meaning of the Lord's life with great clarity."

At the name, several men lifted their eyes. John had called Paul an apostle without hesitation, and those who knew the old disputes felt the generosity of it.

"If a poor fisherman leaves a clumsy writing," John continued, "it may create another occasion for confusion. Let us wait upon the will of God. He does not leave His own testimony at the mercy of human ambition."

Sosthenes spoke next.

"Then if not a Gospel now, perhaps a letter. Apostle, write to the churches. Correct the fever. Call them back from division."

John smiled faintly.

"Paul already wrote to Galatia that the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control. Is that not counsel enough for Corinth?"

"It is counsel," Sosthenes said. "But the churches need your voice. We ask it together."

This time John did not resist long.

He turned to Papias.

"Do we have papyrus and ink?"

Papias was already rising. He opened a small side door and returned with rolls of papyrus, a small vessel of ink, and reeds. Onesimus lifted the lamp from the middle of the table and set it higher on the wall bracket. Papias smoothed the roll before him and trimmed the reed with a small knife.

John closed his eyes.

The room waited.

Then the old apostle began to dictate.

"We write concerning the Word of life, which was from the beginning. We have heard Him. We have seen Him with our eyes. We have looked upon Him, and our hands have touched Him."

Papias wrote quickly, and yet the words seemed to enter the room more slowly than ink could receive them.

The men around the table understood at once the force of the witness.

He had heard.
He had seen.
He had touched.

Against every teaching that made Christ a phantom, a temporary spirit, a mere appearance, a divided being, John set down the authority of flesh, sight, sound, and hands.

"That which we have seen and heard," John continued, "we proclaim to you, so that you also may have fellowship with us. And our fellowship is with the Father and with His Son, Jesus Christ. We write these things so that our joy may be complete."

The lamp burned steadily now.

John spoke of sin and forgiveness, of children, fathers, and young men, of victory over the evil one. He warned them not to love the world or the things of the world. He told them not to believe every spirit, but to test whether it came from God. The test was not cleverness, not fervor, not severity of discipline, not ecstasy, but confession: Jesus Christ has come in the flesh.

And through the whole letter, like a hidden stream flowing beneath stone, one command returned again and again.

Love one another.

"Beloved," John said, "let us love one another, for love is from God. Whoever loves has been born of God and knows God. Whoever does not love does not know God, because God is love."

The words entered the papyrus. They entered the men who heard them.

This was no longer the harsh young John who had wanted fire from heaven upon a Samaritan village. This was not the restless man who had strained toward rank, rivalry, and judgment. The thunder had become rain. The fire had become warmth.

Years of silence had ripened into a single command.

Love one another.

When Papias had finished, he laid the reed aside and carefully lifted the written roll.

Before anyone could speak, a knock came at the barred door.

Every man in the room stiffened.

The times were too dangerous for careless visitors. Domitian's persecution had spread enough that any gathering of church leaders could become a death sentence before dawn.

Onesimus rose at once.

"Who is there?"

"It is I. Polycarp."

Only then did the room breathe.

Onesimus removed the bar. Three men entered: Polycarp, Prochorus, and the old traveler they had found at the theater.

Onesimus stared.

"Tychicus?"

The two old men moved toward each other and embraced before either could say more.

"Onesimus," the traveler said, his voice unsteady.

"Where have you been all these years? I thought you had died in Rome."

"Let me greet the others first," Tychicus said. "There are more witnesses in this room than my old heart can bear at once."

Onesimus turned toward John.

"Apostle, this is Tychicus—the swift-footed brother of whom Paul wrote."

Tychicus approached and bowed deeply.

"Because I have lived too long, I have been granted the honor of seeing you."

John rose and took his rough hands.

"I have heard of you from Onesimus. You walked with him to Colossae when Paul sent him back to Philemon."

Onesimus wiped at his eyes.

"I had stolen from my master and fled. In Rome I met Paul in prison and received Christ. Paul wrote to Philemon, pleading for mercy, but he feared my master's anger and sent Tychicus with me. Because of that journey, I was forgiven and freed—and became a servant of the church."

Introductions followed: Ignatius, Sosthenes, Papias, Polycarp, Prochorus. The room seemed suddenly crowded not by bodies only, but by histories converging after long delay.

When they had prayed, silence returned, now charged with expectation.

John leaned toward Polycarp.

"Callimachus did not come?"

Polycarp hesitated.

"He had business in Ephesus. He said he would attend the dawn service."

"He said?"

"Yes," Polycarp corrected quickly. "He said he would come."

John watched him a moment longer, then said nothing.

Onesimus gestured toward Tychicus.

"Many here have longed to hear what became of you. You have returned to Ephesus after thirty years. Tell us, if you can."

Tychicus set down the fish and the bundle he had carried all evening.

"I went first to Colossae with Paul's letter to Philemon," he began. "That was before the great fire in Rome. Afterward I returned to Ephesus and found not martyrdom, as I had feared, but apostasy. Onesiphorus, Phygelus, Hermogenes—men Paul trusted—had led many away. The church was scattered. I hurried back to Rome and told Paul. Soon after, the fire came, and the brothers there were hunted again. Paul was arrested once more. In prison he wrote a letter for Ephesus and gave it to me, asking that I return and strengthen the scattered believers."

He looked down.

"I did not arrive."

No one spoke.

"A Jew who knew my face betrayed me as I left Rome. I was dragged back and sent into the Circus of Vaticanus. There were beasts and men with blades, though we Christians were no gladiators. We were given enough training to make our deaths interesting."

The men around the table listened without breathing.

"There were two lions, two leopards, and eight of us. We managed, by God's mercy, to net one lion and one leopard. The others died. I alone remained alive when a young officer drove a chariot into the arena—Achilius Glabrio, famed for his skill in the games. He charged a lion, but a leopard sprang toward the back of his neck. I threw my blade and struck it. Then I cast the net before it could tear me apart. Glabrio saw then that I had saved his life. He appealed to Nero for me."

"And you were spared," Papias whispered.

"Spared for another prison. I was given a choice: continue as a gladiator or go to the mines in Hispania. I chose the mines. Before I left, Glabrio came to see me. I entrusted Paul's roll to him and told him I would return for it one day."

Tychicus smiled without joy.

"Thirty-five years passed. I preached Christ to slaves underground. Then, without warning, I was released. I went to Glabrio's house, hoping against sense that the letter might remain. His servants told me he had been arrested as a Christian."

A murmur moved through the room.

"The old steward gave me the roll. Glabrio had found it years later in a storeroom and read it out of curiosity. It drew him to the secret gatherings. He became a fervent believer. He remembered the man who had entrusted it to him, found my name after long searching, and arranged my release. Soon after, an informer denounced him. As he was led away, he ordered the steward to give me the roll if I came."

Tychicus untied his bundle.

Layer after layer of cloth opened until an old papyrus roll appeared, worn but carefully preserved.

He lifted it with both hands and placed it before John.

"Paul's letter to the church of Ephesus has arrived thirty years late."

John's hands trembled as he took it.

No one in the room moved.

The last letter of a man long dead had crossed prison, arena, mine, noble house, conversion, and betrayal to arrive at last in the very city for which it had been written.

John loosened the cord.

The roll opened. The letters were dark and firm, and here and there the papyrus bore stains, as if someone had wept over it.

John began to read.

"Paul, an apostle of Christ Jesus by the will of God, to the saints in Ephesus, the faithful in Christ Jesus: grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ."

The words filled the room with another presence.

Tychicus closed his eyes.

Onesimus covered his face with his hand.

John read of the God who had chosen them in Christ before the foundation of the world, of adoption, grace, redemption, and inheritance. He read of those once dead in trespasses being made alive with Christ. He read that they were no longer strangers and foreigners, but fellow citizens with the saints and members of the household of God, built upon the foundation of apostles and prophets, Christ Jesus Himself the cornerstone.

The irony was almost too much to bear.

A church that had once scattered through fear now received, after thirty years, Paul's plea that it be built together into a dwelling place for God.

John's voice grew stronger.

"Be eager to maintain the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace. There is one body and one Spirit, just as you were called to one hope; one Lord, one faith, one baptism, one God and Father of all."

They were all meeting Paul again.

Ignatius remembered Antioch.
Sosthenes remembered Corinth.
Onesimus remembered Rome and forgiveness.
Tychicus remembered prison, road, arena, and mine.
The younger men imagined the apostle's final road to the sword.

At last John reached the closing words.

"So that you also may know how I am and what I am doing, Tychicus, the beloved brother and faithful servant in the Lord, will tell you everything. I have sent him to you for this very purpose, that you may know how we are and that he may encourage your hearts. Peace be to the brothers, and love with faith, from God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ. Grace be with all who love our Lord Jesus Christ with love incorruptible."

The letter ended.

John held the open roll a long time after the last word had passed his lips.

It felt as though Paul might yet speak again if they waited.

Tychicus broke the silence.

"At his second imprisonment, Paul knew the time was near. His body was worn, but his eyes were like those of a commander entering triumph. He longed to see people. Many had scattered. Only Luke remained with him. He wanted Timothy. He wanted Mark, whom he had once refused. But I do not think they reached him before the end."

The loneliness of Paul's death entered the room and sat among them.

After a long while, John asked, "And the church in Rome?"

Tychicus's face darkened.

"Under Titus, the believers had some room to breathe. But with Domitian, darkness has returned. The gospel has worked powerfully even among the great. Achilius Glabrio became a Christian, as I have told you. More recently another important believer has been arrested."

"Who?" John asked.

"Flavius Clemens, consul of Rome."

The men stared at one another.

Ignatius whispered, "Hallelujah."

The word was barely sound, but it trembled with awe.

Jesus of Nazareth, executed outside Jerusalem in shame, had bent the knee of a Roman consul and kinsman of Caesar.

"His wife, Flavia Domitilla," Tychicus continued, "has been one of the greatest supporters of the Roman church. Beneath lands belonging to her family on the Via Ardeatina, believers gather in the catacombs."

"Has she also been arrested?" Onesimus asked.

"Not yet. But I fear the danger will reach her soon."

"Why has Domitian renewed the persecution?" Sosthenes said. "Even under Titus, though he was the destroyer of Jerusalem, the charity of the Christians was sometimes praised."

Tychicus looked down.

"The accusation has come through the Jews. To preserve the synagogues recognized by Rome, they place the Christians before Caesar as a scapegoat. They accuse us of being a forbidden association, a secret society hostile to the order of the Empire. Recently, even relatives of the Lord—the sons and grandsons of Jude—were imprisoned after Jewish informers denounced them."

The night had deepened.

Outside, the owl cried again and again. The spring wind passed through the olive grove with a sharper sound now, like something unseen moving among the trees.

Inside the small room, the lamp burned over two letters: one just spoken by the last living apostle, and one carried across thirty years by a brother the church had thought dead.

The watchmen of the midnight church sat together in silence.

They had spoken of false teachers, delayed hope, persecution, memory, witness, and love. They had feared the church might be broken from within before Rome could crush it from without.

Yet on that table lay the answer neither strategy nor authority alone could give.

One Lord.
One faith.
One baptism.

And above all, love that did not tremble before the darkness.

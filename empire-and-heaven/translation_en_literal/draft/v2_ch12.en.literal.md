# Empire and Heaven (Volume II)

## Chapter 12. Author's Note (After Writing)

There had once been a man who had become a Christianus, but because of his own fault he was imprisoned again. Yet the Christiani not only tried in every possible way to secure his release, but also bribed the jailer and came to visit him continually, brought him carefully prepared meals and every supply he needed, and outside the prison gate old women stood in line to pray for him.

In AD 312, Constantine, who was governor of Britannia, was marching toward Rome to challenge the imperium when, while riding across the fields of Italy, he suddenly saw a shining cross appear in the sky. He made this cross his military standard, advanced toward the Milvian Bridge, defeated his rival Maxentius at last, and rose to the imperial throne.

In AD 313 he legalized Christianity, and in AD 321 he designated Sunday, which the Christiani kept as the Lord's Day, as a public holiday.

Not long afterward, along Harbor Avenue leading from the harbor of Ephesus into the city, a great memorial church to Saint Mary was built, two hundred and sixty meters in length, and at the ecumenical council held at Ephesus in AD 431, Mary, the mother of Jesus Christ who is one with God, was acknowledged as the Mother of God. Mary, who had landed at the harbor of Ephesus with solemn resolve and walked Harbor Avenue under the stones thrown by the citizens of Ephesus, finally drove out the goddess Artemis from that city and gained the glory of becoming the mother of all humankind. The stone columns of the Temple of Artemis, thrown down by earthquake, were afterward carried to Istanbul and used in the building of Hagia Sophia. More than a hundred years later, on Mount Ayasoluk where John lay asleep and buried, a memorial church to Saint John one hundred and thirty meters in length was built, and its form was the shape of the great cross that John had so desired to bear.

At the entrance to Harbor Avenue from the harbor of Ephesus to the great theater, four tall stone columns were set up, and upon them were placed statues of the four Evangelists who recorded the life of Jesus, namely Mark, Matthew, Luke, and John, thereby proving that the church of Ephesus had been the chief seat of the Gospel, gathering the records of Christ and defending Him from heretics.

The great underground cities in the Korama Valley of Cappadocia continued to be used as refuges for the Christiani, and even after their faith was legalized, many monks gathered there and established churches and monasteries, making it a forge of faith. Even today Korama is a holy place visited by many pilgrims, and tourists and scholars also come there to admire and inspect the unique engineering and precise excavation of the underground cities.

In the end, the faith of the Christiani went so far as to make even the emperor a Christianus, yet their struggle against the powers of this world and their building of the kingdom of heaven still continue. The world has only expanded now from the Mediterranean coast to the entire earth; even today Roman-style bureaucratic administration and politics of surveillance rule the world, and those who are pushed out and alienated from elite society form groups of their own and sharpen the blade of revenge.

In the midst of this, the determination of the Christiani to bring light into the world of darkness is flashing in Ethiopia, in Calcutta, in South Africa, and in every nation and every city of the world where truth is being strangled. At times they are arrested, at times imprisoned, and at times persecuted and made to bleed. Yet as long as the people of that heavenly kingdom are alive on this earth, as long as those armies of love are alive, there is hope for humankind. Like the dawn over Ephesus that John beheld, the kingdom of heaven is being created and expanded.

Originally I began this novel out of curiosity about the magnificent design of the kingdom of heaven revealed in Scripture by Jesus, and about the career of John, the ambitious man moving beneath His disciples; but another attraction that bound me to this novel despite the difficulty was the fateful confrontation between Andronicus, the Roman quaestor, and Callimachus, the brigand of Korama. As I wrote out their conflict, I was able to discover the image of God as a great artist conducting the majestic orchestra of the universe. That vast providence, which embraces all opposing forces and grants even perfect freedom while flawlessly staging death and resurrection, darkness and light, disappearance and creation, held me without ceasing.

I hope that the career of John, which I have arranged in this novel through authorial interest and literary inference, will not become a fixed preconception for those who read the Bible. Many people have written using the contents of Scripture as their material, but no matter how much there is, it can never be too much. Thus John too confessed at the end of his Gospel:

"If all the things concerning Jesus were written down, even if the books thus written were to fill this world, still it would not be enough."

Therefore I want the readers of this novel to remember clearly that my literary discovery concerning John is only one among those books that would fill the world.

In writing this book, I tried, as much as possible, to keep the historical figures, the biblical figures, and the events that appear in Scripture close to reality. Dates that remain unconfirmed even in Josephus's history of the fall of the Jews, Eusebius's church history, and various classical studies were treated as assumptions so that the fictional development might proceed naturally, and even the names of Andronicus, Callimachus, and Drusiana, which belong to the fictional part of this novel, were taken directly from the names that appear in the apocryphal Acts of John. The ties involving John, Vespasian, and Sophia are entirely the author's fiction. I find myself wishing that the small romance an author invented and placed in the hands of the Apostle John, who had to live the lonely life of a celibate until his hair turned white, might have been a gift of consolation enough to make him smile.

As many people who have studied church history from the early church onward have done, I too encountered many problems while writing this novel. Theological interpretations for which no settled orthodox view has yet been established were chosen in the direction that seemed most natural for the progress of the novel. For example, some scholars assume that Mary the sister of Lazarus and Mary Magdalene were the same person, but in fact the characters of the two women as they appear in the record are very difficult to regard as identical. Mary the sister of Lazarus was so inactive that even when Jesus visited Bethany she sat only at His feet; when Jesus arrived again at Bethany to raise Lazarus she still remained seated inside the house; and she was so inward that people were startled when she suddenly acted by pouring perfume on the head of Jesus. Mary Magdalene, however, had originally been a fallen Galilean woman, was present at the place of execution on Golgotha, and was bold and active enough to go to the tomb at dawn.

Therefore I treated Lazarus's whole household as having fled with the Greeks just before the passion of Jesus, and arranged for Mary Magdalene, while serving Saint Mary, to follow them to Lugdunum in Gaul, that is, to what is now the region of Lyon in France, where Lazarus's household had gone first.

The same was true regarding the discrepancy between the genealogies of Jesus recorded by Matthew and Luke. Some scholars hold the view that the genealogy in Luke is the family line of Mary, the mother of Jesus, but on this point the interpretation of Eusebius, who explained it by the Jewish custom of inheritance, seemed far more natural. Moreover, since the apocryphal Gospel of James already presents Joachim and Anna as the parents of Mary, it feels somewhat unfamiliar to say that Mary's father was Heli. Seen in that light, both Matthew's record that Jacob begot Joseph and Luke's record that Joseph's father was Heli can stand only if one follows the view of Eusebius.

More than anything, what was difficult while writing this novel was the method of rendering the names. That is, even the name written as Paul in the Revised Korean Bible becomes Paulo in the Common Translation Bible, Paulus if read in the Roman way, and Paul in English.

For that reason, the rendering of proper nouns in this novel followed the principle of convention. That is, famous biblical names followed the spelling of the most widely distributed Revised Korean Bible, while the names of persons and places appearing on the Roman or Greek side followed the Roman or Greek forms.

I cannot help introducing here the people who encouraged and helped me in beginning so difficult a work. I once again offer my thanks to Father Jung Dal-yong of Gwangju Catholic University, Pastor Park Yong-woo of Keimyung University in Daegu, and Pastor Lee Jong-jin of Kyungdong Methodist Church, who gathered and provided the materials needed to write this novel.

December 27, 1986
On the Great Feast of Saint John

Kim Seong-il

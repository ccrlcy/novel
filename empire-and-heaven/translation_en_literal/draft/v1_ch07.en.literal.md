# Empire and Heaven (I)

## Chapter 7. The Legend of the Shell

The great mansion of the house of Andronicus, said to be the largest even in the city of Ephesus, had suddenly become full of life. In the broad drawing room sat the Apostle John together with the elders and believers of the Ephesian church, and in the garden thick with beautiful flowers and trees many of Ephesus's orphans, including the boy Gaius, were holding a feast. Among them the boy Gaius drew upon himself the envious eyes of the other children. It was because young master Marius Andronicus had promised that when he went to Rome with the bride Drusiana, he would take Gaius with him.

But only Gaius could go to Rome, and in truth the rest of the children were sunk in anxiety. If Drusiana left, there would be no one to care for them. Even while eating, they discussed the matter with seriousness like grown people. In the end they finished the discussion for the moment, deciding that later they would hear the opinion of the Apostle John.

Inside the drawing room, the conversation was still centered on old Tychicus. As Paul's courier he had traveled among many churches; not only that, he was a witness to the mass apostasy in Ephesus and to the fire of Rome, and all the way to his experience in the mines of Hispania he had countless matters to speak of.

In truth, it was not only Tychicus. Ignatius, who had received the laying on of hands directly from Jesus; Onesimus, who had once been Philemon's slave and run away after stealing money; Sosthenes, who had been ruler of the synagogue in Corinth; Papias, who had gathered materials from Philip's four daughters: all their stories were concentrating upon living memories of Christ Jesus.

Thus their conversation moved once more to the writings of Mark and Matthew, and Papias, Polycarp, and Prochorus, their eyes shining, were raising their questions.

As John sat to one side listening to them and sunk in thought, his gaze turned toward Polycarp.

"Polycarp, it seems Callimachus is still nowhere to be seen."

At John's question Polycarp flinched and drew in his neck. Only last night he had reported that Callimachus would attend the morning worship. Yet Callimachus had not come to the morning worship, and even now his whereabouts were unknown.

"Did he say where he was going?"

"No... he said nothing."

Polycarp and Prochorus looked at one another. Last evening Callimachus had said he would go to the house of Tyrannus to meet Drusiana. They had also seen Andronicus going toward the school of Tyrannus, and perhaps there he, Drusiana, and Callimachus had held their final discussion over the marriage. They did not know what had happened then, but in any case Andronicus and Drusiana were being married today. And the one officiating it was the Apostle John, who loved Callimachus most of all.

(Where in the world has Callimachus gone...?)

That was truly the matter nearest to the hearts of Polycarp and Prochorus. Fortunately, John pressed no further. Sitting silently, he felt at his chest and slipped his hand inside. It was one of his occasional habits.

A cord hung around John's neck, and on the cord was threaded a shell with a hole through it. He was drawing it out now and looking at it. It was a small shell, yet unusual, shining with an iridescent five-colored light. Kept for so long, it had grown smooth and glossy.

As he stroked that shell, John's memory turned rapidly and went back more than sixty years.

The wedding feast at Cana, where Jesus of Nazareth first displayed a wondrous miracle... At that time too the wedding house had been merry, just as it was today. Whenever the gathering is lively, the wine is apt to run out sooner, and the wedding house had emptied its store of wine. Just then Mary, the mother of Jesus, told Jesus, who was still waiting for the time of God, about the matter, and Jesus declined her gentle urging, saying that the fact that the wine had run out had nothing to do with him. Yet Mary's human desire did not stop there, and she called the servants.

At last Jesus told them to draw water and pour it into six empty stone jars. Then he told them to carry it to the master of the feast. Needless to say, what they carried was fine wine. The one more astonished than anyone was John himself, who had hidden among the guests and witnessed it. Seeing this unbelievable fact, his heart pounded, and he missed the girl he had so wanted to meet.

It was just then, while an immense event had taken place and water had become wine, and while the people around Jesus were in amazement, that the girl appeared. A girl standing behind the startled crowd, half her face covered with a head cloth, looking this way... Those sorrow-filled eyes and that pale face still hurt the heart of the old John.

(Why was it that I pretended not to know her then...?)

That wedding feast at Cana was the last time John ever saw the girl. When he came to himself afterward, John inquired everywhere why she had come to the feast at Cana, but it was useless. At least in that wedding house at Cana there was no one who knew her.

John's recollection went back once more in time. It was when he was seventeen, one year before he met Jesus, the thirteenth year of Emperor Tiberius, and also the year when Pontius Pilate took office as governor of Judea.

The place where John first met that girl was on the shore at Tiberias.

Tiberias was a rising city that the tetrarch Herod Antipas had built south of Magdala on the western shore of the Sea of Galilee, intending to use it as the capital of Galilee. Dedicated to Emperor Tiberius, the city had been named Tiberias. Though many Jews had been forcibly relocated there, the majority were Gentiles who had drifted in from elsewhere.

John, who had originally lived in Capernaum, often went to Tiberias to visit. Partly it was out of curiosity to see the new civilization of the new city; but it was also to meet a boy of his own age who lived there. This boy, who had moved from Gischala, was also named John. Not only were they the same age and shared the same name, but their temper, quick as fire, and their fierce forward-driving attraction to anything new were so alike that they became close as though they were twins.

That day too John was with John of Gischala, walking along the shore at Tiberias and talking widely about Rome's system of rule, the philosophy of Hellas, their homeland that was fading day by day, and the fate of Judea. Tiberias, fitting for a place Herod Antipas had built as a new capital, commanded a very fine view. Along the left shoreline one could see at a glance the great towns of Magdala and Capernaum, and on a clear day even Bethsaida and Gerasa across the water could be seen.

Originally the Jews scattered along this Galilean shore had been people who had flowed in as they resisted the persecution of Jews that began from the days of Alexander the Macedonian conqueror. Therefore, in one sense, they were more conservative and hard-line Jews than the people who still remained in Jerusalem and Judea. Thus, while the leaders in Jerusalem were submitting to the Roman government, the Zealot party, which sharpened its sword underground and sought the nation's restoration, was organized with this Galilean region as its center.

The two Johns who were speaking that day on the shore at Tiberias about the international situation were still young, yet they were Jews troubled by the shameful state of their homeland and wishing to rebuild it. They agreed with one another that they urgently needed to understand and absorb the civilization of the world, which advanced day by day. With an old way of thinking like frogs in a well, it would be impossible to overturn the Roman Empire. Thus they both believed they had to learn Greek. Though the golden age of Hellas had already passed, Rome too was so busy ruling that at its roots it was founded on Hellenic civilization, and the culture established by Alexander and his mighty successors was governing the whole world from the very ground up.

As they walked by the sea talking in this way, they came upon a girl sitting on the sand looking out at the water. Looking into the clear eyes with which she stared in surprise at the two boys approaching, the two of them felt in her a startling atmosphere of foreignness.

It was John of Gischala who first spoke to the girl, who had taken a guarded posture. He said in Aramaic,

"There is nothing to be afraid of. I am John, who moved here from Gischala. This friend came from Capernaum, and we are both Jews. You seem to be from another country. Where are you from?"

Aramaic, being similar to Hebrew yet also the common tongue of merchants, was used by nearly everyone in Galilee. The girl too seemed to understand Aramaic, for she parted her red lips in a small smile and one short word rolled out.

"Achaia."

Achaia meant the southern region of Hellas.

"If Achaia, then Athens? Corinth? Sparta?"

"Nicopolis."

Nicopolis was a name meaning the city of victory. Located on the western coast of Achaia, it was the city built by Octavianus to commemorate his crushing of Marcus Antonius in the Battle of Actium.

Perhaps the girl had been sitting on the Galilean shore longing for the sea of Nicopolis, her hometown. John, who had been listening beside them, felt that somehow he was a step behind John of Gischala, and so he cut in between them.

"You have come from a fine place. As my friend just said, I live in Capernaum, and my name is John too. To tell us apart, you can call us John of Capernaum and John of Gischala. But what is your name?"

"Sophia."

After hesitating a moment, the girl answered briefly, a soft blush spreading over her white cheeks.

"I am glad to meet you, Sophia."

Again John of Gischala stepped forward.

"This is good, Sophia. We have been hoping to meet someone like you."

"You see, we want to learn Greek, and we have been looking for a friend who could teach it to us."

His words meant simply that he wanted to become friends with her, and for a moment the girl did not know what to do. John of Gischala spoke again.

"You need not be so frightened. If it is truly difficult, we will go and ask your parents ourselves."

The approach of the two quick-tempered boys was, in truth, a lightning offensive. At any rate, in this way the fates of the two boys, the same in name, the same in age, and alike even in character, began to split apart. Both boys loved the beautiful foreign girl equally, and she too had to waver between the two boys who resembled each other so much. Yet the one who yielded first in the end was John of Capernaum. Perhaps John of Capernaum was the more impetuous of the two.

Yet there was a reason he could yield so quickly. All Jews were rigid and strictly forbade marriage with Gentiles, but in the case of John of Capernaum it was absolutely impossible. His father Zebedee, though a fisherman on the Sea of Galilee, owned several boats and was one of the most influential leaders among the Jews of Capernaum. More than that, he had broad ties even with the chief priests who led the Sanhedrin in Jerusalem, and repeatedly sent his sons James and John to Jerusalem to receive instruction from the priests and teachers of the Law. In that sense John's father Zebedee was somewhat unusual compared to other Galileans. He was that much of a realist.

At any rate, it was only natural that John, son of such a Zebedee, could never dare even to utter words about marriage to a Gentile woman.

In that sense the situation was a little more favorable for John of Gischala. First, his father was not alive, and his character, which at times chose action rather than thought, seemed perhaps capable of binding him to Sophia.

Yet this quick calculation of John's brought an unexpected result. He intentionally made chances for Sophia to be with John of Gischala, and even avoided the place himself, trying to draw them closer together. But it seems that such friendship instead moved Sophia's heart. One day in the autumn of that year John arrived at Sophia's house a little earlier than usual. While they waited for the other John, Sophia suddenly removed the cord hanging around her neck. On it were threaded a pair of small but beautifully shaped shells. She untied the knot, pulled one shell free, pressed it into John's hand, and then ran outside in haste.

Holding in his hand that shell which still seemed to keep the warmth of her body, John's heart beat wildly. Greek lessons or anything else no longer mattered. Even after he returned to Capernaum he thought only of her; even when he went out to catch fish he would stand absent-mindedly with the thing in his hand and be scolded by his older brother James.

Whenever he was alone he formed the habit of taking out the shell and looking at it. (Since when had Sophia been wearing this? Perhaps she picked it up on the shore of her hometown Nicopolis....)

The fact that she had pulled out one of the two matching shells and given it to John was a sign that she wished to be joined to him. John could no longer hand her over to John of Gischala. At last John was about to make a decision to possess her. Even if his parents opposed him, even if it meant leaving his parents, even if the synagogue of the Jews cast him out, he meant to join himself to Sophia, prepared even for excommunication.

But John's tremendous resolve never reached its conclusion. A terribly shocking event came upon them. And at last that event clawed and tore apart the fate of the two boys.

On the unforgettable day when that event took place, the two boys and Sophia were walking along the shore where they had first met, talking together in Greek. Then... they saw two horses running toward them. The two young men dressed as soldiers shot past them at speed, then for some reason reined in their horses, stopped, and came back toward them. One was a bodyguard under Herod, and the other wore the uniform of the Roman cavalry. Though he looked young, his face was very cold.

They dismounted and, without even glancing at the two boys, went straight toward Sophia. The bodyguard roughly seized Sophia by the chin and said in Aramaic,

"She appears to be a Greek girl. A pretty enough one."

Then the youth in the cavalry uniform spat out in a cold tone,

"Put her on the horse. That is enough hunting for today."

When the bodyguard lifted Sophia by the waist, she screamed, and at the same moment the two Johns shouted together,

"Put her down!"

At that, the cavalry youth with the cold face drew his brows together.

"Fearless country boys. Step back before you are hurt."

"Who are you to do such a thing?"

As Sophia struggled not to be mounted on the horse, the bodyguard grabbed and tore her outer garment. Sophia's white breast was exposed, and in the middle of her milk-white bosom a small shell glittered. The two boys could endure no more. John of Capernaum sprang at the bodyguard, and John of Gischala rushed at the cavalryman.

Not long after that, John of Gischala collapsed clutching his belly. Bright red blood was gushing between the fingers pressed over the wound.

"You wretch!"

John lunged at the cavalry youth, who stood holding a bloody sword. Dodging the point thrust toward him, he stepped sideways and struck the man's side with his fist. When the other bent at the waist from the sudden counterattack, John seized the sword-hand and twisted it. There came a cracking sound from the other's arm, and the sword fell onto the sand. It was then. Suddenly John felt a dull ache down his back and dropped to the ground on the spot. Something wet was running down from his back. He had been struck there by the sword of the bodyguard who had been behind him. The cavalry youth, whose arm John had twisted, picked up the sword again as though extremely angry and set its point at John's neck. Panting, John glared at him.

"From the way you act, it seems the Roman Empire has not much time left!"

The young man gave a cold smile and withdrew his sword.

"Very well. I shall leave you alive, so that you may see clearly the prosperity of the Roman Empire. You asked who I am, did you not?"

He shoved the sword into its sheath, fixed his face into seriousness, and said,

"Listen well. My name is Vespasianus. A day will come when men tremble at my name alone."

Sophia, who had been carried away that day, never came home. She did not appear that day or the next. The two boys, their bodies gravely wounded, lay side by side in Sophia's house waiting for her, but it was useless. Sophia's father searched for her from Herod's palace even to the barracks of the Roman cavalry, but no one would tell him where she had gone. In the end he went to look for the cavalryman named Vespasianus, exactly as John had told him, yet returned without even meeting him.

By the time their wounds had nearly healed, John of Gischala told John of Capernaum of his decision.

"John, I have decided to join the Zealot party. Moses too taught, 'an eye for an eye, a tooth for a tooth.' I will join the Zealots, sharpen my sword, and wait for the day I can strike down the Roman Empire. John, what will you do?"

"I... have one other thing to do first."

"What is it?"

"What do you think of the prophecies of the prophets, who said that Jehovah would send a Savior to this nation?"

"A Savior... John, we have lived already four thousand years in that hope. But there is no promise of when the Savior will come, no date at all. We must not merely wait; we must rise first. Judah Maccabee rose against Antiochus Epiphanes, and in the end drove out the Greek dynasty and won freedom. If we rise, God will be with us."

"I want to join the Zealots with you this very moment. But before that there is one thing I want to confirm."

"Confirm...?"

"I heard this from people who came recently from Jerusalem... They say that lately a strange figure has appeared by the Jordan River."

"Another Savior story? There have already been several who called themselves the Savior."

"But this man seems different. They say he is baptizing people in the Jordan and saying that the time is near. First I want to go to Judea and see what kind of man he is. In any case our common goal is the Roman Empire. We must keep fighting until we overthrow the Roman Empire."

The two boys clung to one another's wounded bodies and wept. They were Abraham's descendants, blessed by Jehovah, and this nation had been brought out of Egypt by Jehovah's own hand. Yet the humiliation and abuse that this people had to suffer from Gentiles was so sorrowful that they bit their lips.

It was truly an unforgettable bitterness. Night after night John dreamed of the Roman Empire collapsing, and shuddered at the cold smile of Vespasianus. Even after living through so many turbulent years, the knot tied in John's heart had not been loosened. And whenever he saw young people getting married, Sophia's great eyes rose before him.

Suddenly it seemed the people were murmuring, and John came back to himself. Someone shouted loudly from the direction of the door,

"The bride and bridegroom have entered!"

Apparently the banquet at the house of Tyrannus had now ended. Indeed, from outside came the sound of pipes and the singing of children. Quickly John pushed the cord with the shell hanging from it back into his chest and turned toward the door.

Andronicus, dressed formally in a toga, was entering together with the bride wearing an orange veil beneath her wreath. The bride wore a short-sleeved tunic, and at her waist was a woolen girdle tied in the knot of Hercules, which only the bridegroom could untie. The Christiani guests all clapped their hands to welcome bride and bridegroom. The servants cleared away the appetizers from the tables and began bringing out roasted lamb, braised venison, dishes of fig leaves and bay leaves fried in olive oil, and dishes of turtledove. Out of consideration for the many Jews there, no pork at all was being used.

As Andronicus came back after escorting the bride to an inner room, Hesthenes said to him in a low voice,

"My lord has celebrated today the most glorious wedding among the Christiani."

"Because it has received the apostle's officiation?"

"That is not all. As I told you this morning, in this Ephesus there are now gathered the responsible men of each region to discuss the problems within their ranks."

"Is that so?"

"The old man sitting beside the apostle is Bishop Ignatius of the church of Antioch. They say he received the laying on of hands directly from the carpenter of Nazareth when he was six years old. And the one sitting beside him..."

"Ah, that old man is the one I met yesterday aboard the ship Vesta..."

"That is right. He is an old man called Tychicus, a native of Ephesus, and he followed the man named Paul, who was their leader. They say his feet were very swift and agile, and he served as Paul's courier. He was arrested in the time of Emperor Nero, worked in the mines for thirty years, and only yesterday returned to Ephesus. And... the old man sitting to the apostle's left is Bishop Sosthenes of the church in Corinth..."

When a serving girl carrying dishes passed by, Hesthenes closed his mouth for a moment. On the tray she carried were piled all manner of delicacies, including mullet from the island of Corsica, pine mushrooms and asparagus brought from the region of Gaul.

"That Bishop Sosthenes was originally ruler of the Jewish synagogue, but after hearing Paul's preaching he received Christian baptism. And the man seated below him is Bishop Onesimus of the Ephesian church. He was originally the slave of a rich man in the region of Colossae, but after stealing his master's money and running away, he met Paul in a prison in Rome, repented, and received baptism. At Paul's request his master made Onesimus a free man. And..."

Hesthenes pointed with his finger at the young man seated beside old Tychicus.

"That young man is Bishop Papias of the church at Hierapolis..."

Listening to Hesthenes's rapid whispering, Andronicus stared at him.

"...Why do you look at me like that?"

"Where in the world did you learn so much? Are you too a Christianus?"

"Heavens, no. I am simply gathering information thoroughly for my lord's sake."

"If you were one of the emperor's delatores, no one would survive."

"For the day when my lord becomes emperor, I too am building up my training as a delator."

"Hush, be careful what you say."

"Do not worry. Everyone here is a Christianus. But..."

"?"

"How would it be? Do you have any thought of arresting all these chiefs of the Christiani and winning merit before His Majesty Emperor Domitianus?"

"Hesthenes, it seems you have forgotten that I was baptized today."

"Dominus, you make me too sad."

"Do you too ever grow sad?"

"I had meant to go to hell attending upon my lord, but at this rate it looks as if I may have to go alone."

"It seems I should sell you and find another servant."

"Then we should have to begin again from the caves of Coressus."

"But... the watch around the house is being kept well, is it not?"

"Do not worry. Even if the emperor's Roman legion should attack, my lord's house will not so much as shake."

Before they knew it, they had come all the way to the place where John and the elders of the other churches were seated together. Andronicus bowed his head toward John and said respectfully,

"Thank you very much for coming like this. It is an infinite honor for Drusiana and for me. Please eat to your heart's content, and if there is anything lacking, tell me."

Then John beckoned him.

"..."

"Please speak, apostle."

"It is just... whenever I eat good food, I grow anxious whether there may be someone going hungry..."

"Ah, do not be concerned. Already our garden is full of orphans and widows. Drusiana warned me again and again, lest I neglect that point."

He clapped his hands and called a servant girl, then told her to bring the children inside once they had finished eating. Not long after the servant girl bowed and went out, as if she understood, the door of the drawing room opened and the little children, including Gaius, came pouring in. All wore satisfied faces as though they had eaten well, and some were still chewing and swallowing something. At a gesture from the adults, the children each went and embraced one of the adults. In their hands they held wedding cakes made of grain flour soaked in wine and baked on bay leaves.

Again the tables began filling with rare desserts of every kind, from fruits to dates fried in honey with walnuts and pine nuts, all the way to fried rose blossoms. Hesthenes asked his master,

"My lord, a banquet ought to have music and dancing. What shall we do?"

"Let me see... is there any way to know what kind of music the Christiani like?"

"You who were baptized do not even know that?"

"If you keep talking like that, it will become a habit."

Andronicus again raised his voice and asked the guests,

"What kind of music do our guests like?"

Then the boy Gaius, who was sitting in John's lap, said loudly,

"I know what songs our apostle likes!"

Looking at Gaius's face, Andronicus finally understood why the name Gaius had sounded familiar to him in the cave family tomb. Perhaps that boy Gaius was of the bloodline of the one who had miraculously survived when the whole family was slaughtered.

"Then, Gaius, tell us."

At that the boy Gaius signaled with his eyes to the friends gathered around him. Then all the children began to sing together.

The Lord is my shepherd;
I shall not want.
He maketh me to lie down in green pastures;
He leadeth me beside still waters.
He restoreth my soul;
He leadeth me in the paths of righteousness for His name's sake.

Andronicus was almost beside himself at that clear melody, which seemed like nothing he had ever heard in his life. It was a song never heard in the greedy banquet halls of Rome. Even more astonishing to him were the words themselves. Certainly they seemed utterly far removed from the life he had lived until now, and yet at the same time like a song he had known from long ago. The mysterious song of the children continued.

Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death,
I will fear no evil;
For Thou art with me;
Thy rod and Thy staff, they comfort me.
Thou preparest a table before me in the presence of mine enemies;
Thou anointest my head with oil;
My cup runneth over.
Surely goodness and mercy shall follow me all the days of my life,
And I will dwell in the house of the Lord forever.

To Andronicus it was truly a wonder. The children's song was drawing with strong power the man who had lived only among war, intrigue, and rivalry. When the song ended, without knowing it he suddenly lifted up one of the little children before him and kissed the child. Though the child wore dirty clothes and a grimy face, to him the child seemed a hundred times, a thousand times more beautiful than the children of Rome.

Holding the child in his arms, he began to speak.

"Dear little friends, I have something I must say to you for a moment."

At once the children all looked at Andronicus and grew quiet.

"Little friends, today I must bring you some disappointing news."

He paused a moment, then caught his breath and continued.

"As you know, today I have married older sister Drusiana, who has been taking care of you. And for a time we must leave Ephesus and go live in Rome."

At once the children's eyes filled with sadness.

"And as you also know very well, older sister Drusiana cannot take all of you to Rome, and so we have decided to take only Gaius."

Again the children's eyes turned toward Gaius. Young Gaius lowered his head deeply, ashamed that he alone was going to Rome.

"So I... made a special request to my mother for the sake of you who must remain here."

All in the room looked at Andronicus with curiosity.

"I asked my mother to take good care of you while I am away. As you know, the house of Andronicus is the largest house in Ephesus. I will open this house for all the orphans in Ephesus, and I promise to provide you with as much food as you need, just as Drusiana did for you."

At once the eyes of the children in the room brightened, and they burst into cheers. Some even clapped.

"There is only one thing for which I must ask your understanding. It will be difficult to treat you every day to such costly foods as the braised venison or the pine mushrooms you ate today."

The children burst into merry laughter.

"So eat as much of these things as you wish today. My mother is a very thrifty woman and dislikes to see food left over."

Within Andronicus's joke, people were able clearly to perceive the sound family custom of that house and how the family had become the richest in Ephesus. Once again the people felt that today's wedding was a blessed wedding. It was the ideal union of the noble house of Andronicus, a house of excellent tradition, and the house of Tyrannus, a house of learning.

Papias, who sat beside old Tychicus, looked toward John and said,

"Now that it has come to this, it seems it is time for the apostle to say a few words."

Suddenly named, John opened his eyes wide. The expression was so amusing that the children again burst into laughter like scattering flower petals.

"Apostle, since we have received such fine food, you must give words of greeting on behalf of us all."

Polycarp and Prochorus knew well why Papias kept speaking to John. It was his fixed desire to gather even one more word from John's mouth while he was still alive in this world.

John set down the teacup he had been holding and rose from his place. Again the children clapped. Unsuitable to his white-haired age, he coughed awkwardly. While the whole room became quiet as though water had been poured over it, suddenly one child seated farther inside cried out in a loud voice,

"Agapate allelous!"

At once the children again burst into laughter. Whenever he was called to stand like this, the Apostle John would think a long while about what to say and then say only one thing before sitting back down.

"Agapate allelous."

This time even the adults burst into laughter together. Yet for some reason John did not sit back down after saying those words. He delayed a little longer, then spoke again.

"Brothers, today it seems that for the first time in a very long while we are laughing together. And above all, our great joy is because of our new brother whom we gained today, Marius Andronicus. Many people say that the way we believe is a way of suffering and a road of sorrow. In truth, when we look back over our past until now, that is not altogether a mistaken word. The face of our Lord Jesus Christ was always shadowed with grief, and his life was stained with blood and tears. Not only so, but the apostles and the fathers who went before us in faith all walked such a road."

John's two eyes were shining, full of feeling. He gathered his breath for a moment and then continued.

"Yet... what we must never forget is that the road we walk is the road of joy, the road of festival. We all know that only those who know the truth obtain freedom, and only those who love obtain joy. Therefore Christ's first miracle took place at the wedding feast in Cana. By changing the water in the stone jars into wine, Jesus made clear the festal meaning of his coming. Therefore the way of the cross begins from victory."

Again he broke off and looked quietly toward Andronicus before opening his mouth once more.

"In that sense... today I will give all of you brothers one very joyful piece of news."

Then all those in the room looked at John with puzzled eyes. For the Christiani, who had always heard only fearful and shocking news, joyful news was a word they had not heard in a very long time.

"Brothers... together with the couple who were married today, I too am setting out on a journey."

At once an uncontrollable turmoil arose throughout the drawing room. They all began murmuring at once, and the elders were looking at John with astonished eyes. Not understanding what was happening, Andronicus seized the sleeve of Hesthenes, who stood beside him.

"What is this?"

"I gave that old gentleman a hint beforehand."

"I did not know something would break out on a day like this..."

While everyone was in an uproar, the young Papias spoke first.

"Apostle... where is it that you say you are traveling?"

Looking at Papias, John answered without hesitation.

"I am going to Rome."

The people's shock was great. Certainly Rome had been the final destination of gospel mission ever since the time of Paul and Peter. Rome was the capital and heart of the worldly government that ruled the whole world. No one among the Christiani had denied the proposition that the gospel must be planted there. But now, although the church in Rome was in immense tribulation, it was still being guarded by many warriors of faith. Clement, bishop of the Roman church in the midst of affliction, had even been sending letters deeply concerned about the aberrant tendencies of the churches in Achaia and Asia.

And yet there remained something hard to understand in the decision of the Apostle John now to go to Rome. In the present state of things, what was needed more desperately than anything else was the authority of the apostle who had directly accompanied Jesus Christ. And John was now the last surviving apostle. This time Sosthenes of the Corinthian church asked again.

"Apostle... for what reason are you now seeking to go to Rome?"

"There is no particular reason. Domitian, emperor of Rome, ordered that John of Ephesus be brought in, and Andronicus the quaestor, who became our brother today, came to Ephesus in order to carry out that command."

Then all in the room looked toward Andronicus at once with hostile eyes. Before Andronicus even had time to excuse himself, John continued speaking.

"Brothers, as the Apostle Paul always said, we cannot rebel against the masters of this world. Already from the time our forefather Adam ate the fruit of the knowledge of good and evil, we ourselves acknowledged the powers of this world as well. All of us must understand the position of our brother Andronicus. For just as farmer, fisherman, and leatherworker are each one kind of profession, so too is public service."

"But, apostle."

The one who stepped forward this time was the firmly built Prochorus.

"I do not object to your saying that we must understand brother Andronicus's position. But surely there is no need for you to surrender yourself voluntarily to Rome in order to build up brother Andronicus's merit. Even if he fails to escort you this time, how much disadvantage will brother Andronicus really suffer?"

Prochorus shot a glance at Andronicus, almost as though protesting. Andronicus nodded and was about to open his mouth in explanation, but John's words continued before him.

"Then what shall brother Andronicus report to the emperor when he returns to Rome? Must he not report that he did not see the old man named John?"

John's counter-question was sharp. Not lying was a fixed rule of the Christiani, and if Andronicus had to return to Rome and make a false report, then the apostle would in effect be causing him to bear false witness. Yet Prochorus did not yield and kept pressing.

"Rahab, the harlot of Jericho, who hid Joshua's spies, became a blessed woman by deceiving the soldiers who came to seize them and saying they had already gone away. Rahab married Salmon of the tribe of Judah and became the great-great-grandmother of David, and from David's line was not Jesus born?"

The logic of Prochorus, protesting with a story from Scripture, was bold. Pressing his advantage, he unfolded his own opinion still further.

"Even so, I am not saying that brother Andronicus must be made to give a false report. There are many ways. After this feast ends today, you might hide yourself somewhere else. Then brother Andronicus can simply report that by the time he sought you, you had already withdrawn and he could not possibly find you."

Andronicus nodded. In truth, even if he failed to arrest John on this mission, he would not necessarily receive a fatal rebuke. The emperor might mark him as an incapable official, yet perhaps that might even turn out to be more favorable for the safety of his life.

While Prochorus was speaking thus, John had remained with bowed head, deep in thought. For some time afterward only a heavy silence flowed through the room. At length John lifted his head again.

"...From the time Deacon Stephen was martyred outside Jerusalem's Sheep Gate, the sufferings of the saints began. Yet even when my own elder brother James died under the blade of Herod Agrippa, I always had to keep avoiding their eyes. It was because of Jesus' request that I care for his mother...."

To calm his surging heart, he again had to pause a long while.

"After that, Matthias, who took the place of Judas Iscariot, who sold the Teacher for thirty pieces of silver, and became one of the twelve disciples, was martyred in Ethiopia; and after I moved to Antioch, Jesus' brother James too was martyred. As you know, in Ephesus the household of Gaius and Bishop Timothy were martyred, and Peter and Paul died in Rome. In Achaia, Andrew was hung upon an X-shaped cross; Barnabas died in Cyprus, and Mark in Alexandria.... While they were dying, I brought Mary also to Rome, yet again I heard the heartbreaking news of the destruction of Jerusalem. John of Gischala, the leader of the Zealot rebellion, was my childhood friend, and he too met a miserable end at the hands of Titus's army after defending the temple of Jerusalem to the very last."

At last John was wiping away with his fingertips the tears that had filled his eyes.

"Brothers, please pray for the scattered brothers of Jerusalem. This John, lacking in faith... still does not understand the miserable end of Jerusalem. Many fervent brothers say their end came because they nailed the Son of God to the cross and killed him... yet among the one million people who died so terribly, most would never even have seen the face of Jesus.... Only one thing is certain: our Lord did not come into this world for curse and judgment. He was the one who prayed to the very end for forgiveness and salvation."

It was a truly astonishing declaration. John had already cast off even the authority of an apostle and was confessing his own insufficient faith.

"Not only the people of Jerusalem, but the sufferings of the apostles too continued. Thaddaeus and Simon in Persia; Thomas and Nathanael in India; Philip in Hierapolis; and Matthew again in Ethiopia.... When you hear the news of their sufferings, you cannot know how much I have longed to be called by the Lord. Now all those friends have gone to the Lord's side, and only I remain. Mary has already departed from the world, and even after that I have lived too long. Now the Lord has given me that time. What is time? It is what God gives and man obeys. Even on the Mount of Olives, when Jesus was saying farewell, because we did not know what that time was, we asked, 'Lord, is it at this time that you restore the kingdom to Israel?' Then the Lord said that the times and seasons the Father had set by his own authority. That time is something no one can resist."

While all were solemn and silent, John looked once more at Andronicus.

"Today, more than anything, I am glad that I am going to Rome together with brother Andronicus. And I am grateful that he has promised to care for the orphans of Ephesus. Yet as you know, for one individual to complete the relief of the poor is difficult. Therefore I ask you all to help this house from time to time with your offerings."

Again he looked around at the elders and the younger disciples.

"We have worried greatly over the corruption of the churches. But now those matters have been left only to you. Just as the death and resurrection of Jesus appeared as power to the apostles, so now he must live again within your faith. We have fought in order to guard Jesus Christ... yet the true Christ is not one who is inside a book, but one who is living here among you. Remember the words of him recorded by Matthew: Lo, I am with you always, even unto the end of the world...."

When John finished speaking, he gestured and called Polycarp. As Polycarp came forward through the children, John made him kneel and laid his hand on his head.

"Polycarp, in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, I lay my hands upon you and entrust to you the church of Smyrna..."

It was the laying on of hands for the appointment of a bishop. Polycarp was so astonished that he held a hand to his chest. Keeping his hand upon his head, John prayed still longer, and then said in a low voice so that only Polycarp could hear,

"...Guard the church of Smyrna. And there is one more reason I entrust Smyrna to you. Do not forget Callimachus. He will come to need your help."

John opened his mouth toward the congregation.

"May the Lord be with you all."

The congregation answered in voices choked with tears.

"And also with the apostle...."

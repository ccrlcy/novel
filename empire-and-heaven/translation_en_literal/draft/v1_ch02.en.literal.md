# Empire and Heaven (I)

## Chapter 2. Strangers

All the passengers of the Vesta had poured out onto the deck and were gazing, entranced, at Ephesus, which shone beautifully in the evening sunlight. Someone muttered as though groaning, "Oh, beautiful jewel of Artemis..."

In the middle of the harbor of Ephesus, gilded by the light, stood the beautiful form of the harbor monument, composed of soft Ionic columns. Behind it, the broad, straight Harbor Avenue cut across the heart of the city of Ephesus and stretched toward the great theater spread out like a fan. Mount Pion, rising softly like a woman's breast as it embraced that great theater, which could seat twenty-four thousand people in sixty-six tiers, was also full of evening sunlight.

"Ah! The goddess Artemis is coming out!"

When someone cried out so, everyone craned their necks and looked toward the harbor.

"Where, where?"

"Look there! She is coming out from the road on the right of the great theater!"

What they first made out was the crowd surging out from the direction of the great theater. Countless people began to fill the road, and the crowd in turn was covered with flowers and leaves.

"We are fortunate. We have arrived at the spring festival of Artemis!"

Filling the broad Harbor Avenue, the crowd gradually moved toward the harbor. Thus the Vesta's dropping anchor at the quay and the festival crowd's passing through the harbor monument occurred at almost the same time. Through the three exits of the monument, the straight central gate and the arched gates to left and right, people were pouring out like a tide.

Because the quay was so noisy, Andronicus and Hesthenes had not yet thought of disembarking and stood by the gunwale. And because it was better to remain on the ship if they wished to watch the festival, they were looking down at the quay from aboard.

At last the statue of Artemis, surrounded by flowers and leaves, began to pass through the central gate. At the very front stood the high priest in white; behind him walked male priests guarding the divine image. They were all guards of the goddess who had cut off their own testicles with their own hands and buried them under the altar of Artemis. Behind them followed priestesses, also dressed in white. The number of priestesses, who had to spend their whole lives in virginity, was twenty-four, the same as the number of Artemis's breasts.

Originally, the procession of this spring festival began from the temple of Artemis in the marshland behind Mount Pion. The image of the goddess Artemis, emerging from the temple and leading the procession of the frenzied crowd, entered the city of Ephesus from the east through the Magnesian Gate, passed the eastern gymnasium and the Tetragonos market, went by the front of the odeon, and reached the front of the city hall of Ephesus. Inside that city hall stood another image of Artemis looking outward. When the crowd's frenzy reached its height between the two divine images, the governor of Ephesus was to come out from his residence and pay homage to the goddess. After receiving the governor's greeting, the goddess would begin to move again, pass Crete Street, go by the library, and reach the square of Ephesus; then, in front of the square, she would turn left and enter Harbor Avenue. When the goddess passed down Harbor Avenue and reached the quay, the sacrifice of bulls and the festival would finally begin. When the festival ended, the procession was to go back toward the great theater, turn left in front of it, pass the great stadium, and go through the Koressos Gate. When the divine image passed through the Koressos Gate, went around the western slope of Mount Pion, turned north again, and arrived at the temple, this noisy spring festival would come to an end.

"Look. The blood sacrifice is finally beginning!"

In the direction Hesthenes pointed, many bulls could be seen. The bulls were tied fast among logs woven crosswise and lengthwise so that they could not move. Andronicus knew that all those bulls were being offered to Artemis, and that their number was twenty-four.

Before long, the desperate cries of the bulls began to ring out, and the crowd began to burst into ecstatic shouts. The male priests had just begun to cut off the bulls' testicles with sharp knives. Amid blood-colored cries, the severed testicles of the bulls were washed in fragrant oil, strung on cords, and hung around Artemis's neck.

At last, when the testicles of all twenty-four bulls had been hung around the goddess's neck, the dance of naked female dancers began to music that swelled as though it would burst. Flames rose on the altar prepared for roasting meat, and the bulls, blood streaming from between their legs, were slaughtered one after another and placed upon the altar.

Soon the smell of burning meat rose all over the quay and flowed out toward the sea.

"My lord!"

Hesthenes pulled at the young master's arm. When Andronicus turned his head, a young officer in military dress was giving a military salute before him. He spoke in a vigorous voice.

"Welcome home, Quaestor Marius Andronicus! I have come by His Excellency the governor's command to receive the quaestor's party. As you see, Ephesus is now very noisy because of the festival."

From behind the officer, Lucius, Andronicus's steward, stepped forward and bowed.

"You have had a hard journey."

"Oh..."

Andronicus was more pleased to see his steward than the governor's subordinate, but since he could not ask about Drusiana in front of several people, he spoke to the officer. "Thank you for coming out like this. Now then, let us all disembark."

The six soldiers the officer had brought were pushing people aside and opening a path. Hesthenes, who was about to disembark after his master, suddenly saw old Tychicus still standing by the gunwale and looking at something. As if he had no interest in the festival, he was staring blankly at the watchtower on the hill of Astyages to the right of the harbor. After walking a few more steps, Hesthenes glanced once more at the old man and started in surprise. The old man was looking at the watchtower on the hill, and tears were running down his face.

The party that had come down from the ship crossed the square of the quay, thick with the smell of blood and burning meat, and passed through the harbor monument. The soldiers had already made ready the horses they would ride behind the monument.

Andronicus, the steward Lucius, the secretary and personal physician who had followed from Rome, and the officer sent by the governor mounted horses, while Hesthenes and the rest of the party followed on foot. Upon the colonnades standing on both sides of the road, statues of famous heroes and scholars of Rome and Hellas looked down upon their procession.

On both sides of the Harbor Avenue, splendidly paved with marble, all kinds of luxurious shops stood in rows. Along the colonnades formed by white, gray, and pale-brown marble pillars, fifty lamps in each row extended as far as the statue of the wild boar. This boar was the animal that had appeared and guided Androcles, the founder of Ephesus, when he reached this region.

On both sides of the road as well, many people stood gathered around gaming boards, absorbed in gambling. On the marble pavement of this Harbor Avenue, boards for gambling had been engraved from the beginning. People were playing around gaming spaces carved in the shape of round wheels or square window frames. From among them came bursts of cheering now and then, and sometimes curses.

On the left side of the avenue were the harbor gymnasium and baths. Roman citizens, who originally valued the spirit of the gentleman, were expected to cultivate strong bodies and keep them clean; therefore, wherever they went, they built gymnasia and baths. Moreover, as the ruling class increased and the population engaged not in direct labor but chiefly in office work such as trade, distribution, and finance grew rapidly, while diet moved rather toward excessive nutrition centered on meat, the number of people becoming obese increased. Exercise facilities and bathing facilities were therefore essential. Yet in spite of everyone's concern for health, the temperament of the Roman people became increasingly violent and cruel. Thus large arenas multiplied in every city, and gladiatorial contests were held without ceasing.

Ephesus alone had several large gymnasia and baths, but this harbor gymnasium and bathhouse in particular were facilities of extreme luxury. The gymnasium was fully equipped with indoor athletic facilities capable of holding several thousand people and with equipment for every sort of physical training. Its marble interior was filled with luxurious murals and sculptures, enough to call a palace to mind, and those who finished exercising could move directly into the bathhouse connected beside it. The bathhouse too was built of marble and designed to admit bright sunlight from above. The baths were divided by temperature into three types, tepidarium, caldarium, and sudatorium, so that one could move among them at will. The most splendid feature of this bathhouse was that it was designed so one could look out over the beautiful harbor of Ephesus while bathing. Guests enjoyed their baths while admiring the cargo ships bringing various ores, marble, timber, and foodstuffs into Ephesus, and the healthy male slaves and beautiful female slaves being bought and sold in the slave market.

The bathhouse was also perfectly equipped not only with spacious lounges and changing rooms, but with meeting rooms where people could hold conferences naked during their rest, reading rooms, and galleries. It was the center of citizens' cultural life, and countless people visited it, from politicians, scholars, and artists to public officials, businessmen, bankers, and every traveler who came to Ephesus.

Among all of Ephesus, which possessed everything human beings desired, this Harbor Avenue was the center, the dream street that women of the whole world wished to walk.

Watching Andronicus's party enter the city with several attendants and even a military escort, the citizens whispered among themselves. Among them were some who seemed to recognize the young heir of the house of Andronicus, smiling and waving their hands.

When the steward Lucius's horse drew near, Andronicus asked,

"Is my mother well?"

"Yes. Since hearing that her son was coming, her health has become much better these days."

The theater of Ephesus, facing them at the end of the avenue, approached in an ever more majestic form the nearer they came. The shape of this open-air theater, as if spreading its arms toward the sky, seemed to proclaim the grand ideal of the Roman Empire itself.

When they came near the theater, at the entrance to the Roman square on the left, they could see statues of the great rulers of the Roman Empire, Octavianus and Vespasianus, standing upon enormous marble columns.

Andronicus paid homage to the two statues with a Roman military salute, then asked Lucius again.

"What is the mood in Ephesus?"

"Peaceful. The majesty of the Roman Empire fills the whole city. The citizens are all absorbed in business, and because zeal for education is very high, excellent teachers are well treated."

Walking on foot behind them, the servant Hesthenes let a sneer flow over his lips. The Romans' zeal for education was not, as with the Hellenes of old, a zeal for the search for truth. As farms became great estates and the trade of goods increased, knowledge industries such as commerce, finance, and distribution, rather than direct production, increased; in the end, because everyone rushed into those fields, competition could not help becoming fierce.

In the end, Romans were investing enormous educational expenses in order to advance their children or let them live comfortably without doing rough labor. Not only regular schools, but also several specialized private tutors attached to a child were commonplace. In the midst of such fierce study, only philosophy was excluded; what they needed intensively was not the philosophy pursued by the Hellenes, but more practical law, accounting, politics, and the military arts, physical training, music, and miscellaneous knowledge required for upper-class life.

Because of these enormous educational expenses for their children, Romans had no choice but to practice birth control even more. Even babies of ordinary married couples, not merely the result of pleasure, were thrown recklessly into the Tiber. It was to the point that almost every Roman citizen had some memory of having abandoned a baby.

Ephesus too was not a place outside the storm zone of this abnormal education. That was why Hesthenes could not help smiling bitterly when the steward Lucius spoke of Ephesus's zeal for education.

When they reached the front of the theater and were turning right along the marble road that crossed from the direction of the stadium, Hesthenes suddenly looked back and discovered a person walking along at a slow loiter, and his expression again became tense.

An old man walking with hunched shoulders, peering into the shops along the road... It was none other than the man named Tychicus whom they had met on the deck of the Vesta. Somehow he had obtained a fish, which he held in his hand, strung on a cord, and he had a bundle slung over his shoulder.

He did not now seem to be looking for anyone, nor did he seem like a person with somewhere to go. Like a ghost, he peered into shops and then stood blankly looking at the evening sky.

...A man of Ephesus returning to his hometown after thirty years!

Even after withdrawing his gaze and continuing to walk, for some reason Hesthenes could not erase the old man's image from his mind.

...There may be something one would exchange even the whole Pax Romana to obtain... That old man had said so. There was something pointed in those words!

The party passed along the splendid marble road and entered Crete Street, the center of Ephesus. Splendid shops stood in rows on both sides of the street, and three- and four-story buildings and apartment houses formed a forest. On the hill north of the road, the luxurious mansions of Ephesus's upper class shone golden in the evening sunlight.

Andronicus turned his eyes toward the place from which women's laughter had suddenly burst. Women in splendid outing clothes and Egyptian-style makeup were waving toward him. They were passing the famous high-class brothel of Ephesus. At every window of the three-story building decorated with beautiful flowers, girls thrust out their heads, looked down at Andronicus, and raised cheers. At each entrance to the brothel there were portraits of women, and guide tiles showing their names and footprints.

Among Roman women, the shape of the foot was a symbol of sex. It was the Romans' statistical observation that if the feet were beautiful, the figure and character were beautiful as well. Andronicus lifted his hand in reply. It was the duty of a Roman gentleman to treat women courteously.

After walking for a while among the tall buildings, sunk in emotion, Andronicus suddenly pulled the bridle and stopped speaking. Far away, at the entrance to the Tetragonos market, a large temple building could be seen, and on the high terrace extending to the east of the temple stood a gigantic statue about four times the height of a person.

"That... was not there before I left Ephesus..."

Then the steward Lucius drew his horse alongside and explained.

"That temple is the shrine serving His Majesty Emperor Domitianus, who is our lord and god. And that gigantic statue is the emperor's image. Every citizen who passes here must bow before that statue."

"I see...?"

Andronicus slowly urged his horse and approached the temple. The face of the emperor, with thick neck and short jaw, looked down upon the human beings on earth. Thirteen columns on the long side and eight on the short side supported a splendid canopy. Andronicus dismounted and raised a military salute toward the dizzyingly high statue.

"Quaestor Marius Andronicus offers worship to His Majesty Emperor Domitianus, consul, censor general, lord and god..."

Even after finishing the salute, Andronicus remained standing there, looking at the emperor's enormous statue. Domitianus, whom Vespasianus was said to have fathered at the age of forty-two... now fourteen years had already passed since his brother Titus had died and he had risen to the imperial throne. As if symbolizing the Roman Empire still ruling over the world, the emperor's statue had caused his own temples and statues to be erected in every provincial city.

Still not mounted, he looked at Hesthenes standing behind. Hesthenes raised his right hand and said,

"One day in Ephesus... another Colossus will stand."

It was certainly like Hesthenes's sarcasm. Colossus meant a gigantic statue, and he was speaking of Nero's Colossus, which until recently had stood before Rome's Colosseum.

Nero and Domitianus... They shared the trait of liking to set up statues of themselves. Mischievously, Hesthenes was adding Marius Andronicus after them. Andronicus deliberately pretended not to have heard him, looked at the sky with a solemn face, and sprang back onto his horse. The party again went out onto the avenue, walked toward the outdoor music hall, and stopped before the governor's residence on the left. The young officer who had come to receive them jumped down from his horse and walked into the residence.

When Andronicus dismounted, the officer, returning from inside, and the governor's secretary came out and guided him.

"Please come inside."

He instructed the party he had brought to wait in the waiting room and went inside with the steward Lucius. After passing through the splendid garden inside the residence, the entrance of the main building appeared. When he entered the entrance decorated with marble sculptures, a cheerful voice flew from within.

"Oh, the morning star from Ephesus has arrived!"

The sturdy man walking out from inside was formally dressed in a toga with purple stripes. He was formally dressed in order to go out and greet the procession of the goddess Artemis as it passed before city hall.

"It has been a long time, Your Excellency."

The governor came up to Andronicus, took his shoulders in his large two hands, and looked deeply at his face with emotion.

"Quaestor Marius Andronicus, seeing you makes me truly realize that time has passed."

"Your Excellency has not aged at all."

He spoke while carefully looking at the governor's graying sideburns.

"When you left here you were a foolish boy, but now you have become a gentleman who even knows how to give courteous greetings. Now Ephesus will truly have reason to be proud because of you."

"Not at all. Your Excellency Governor Lycomedes, a former tribune, a great general, a magistrate, and a senator representing Ephesus, is truly the pride of Ephesus."

The tribune, that is, tribunus, was an office of leadership representing the power of the plebeians. In early Rome, the office of praetor, or magistrate, had been occupied only by men from noble families, but in the end they could not help allowing plebeian forces to enter politics. For the powerful national strength of Rome itself rested on the heavy infantry of the common people centered on tribal forces.

Therefore, the office of tribune grew stronger and stronger, and at last the Roman government came to appoint one of the consuls, the highest ruling office, as well as the magistrates, from among the former tribunes.

Governor Lycomedes was a tribune from Ephesus, had advanced as far as magistrate, had become a senator, and had been appointed governor of Ephesus. Holding Andronicus as if embracing his shoulders, he laughed heartily.

"Now that young stars like this are rising, old things like me should quickly give up our seats and retire."

"What are you saying? Rome does not permit personal ease."

"Ha, ha... You too have already become a patriotic public official who knows how to subordinate the individual to Rome. Now... until the bathwater is prepared, first sit here and let us talk a little."

He pointed to the splendid chairs in the wide marble reception room and invited him to sit.

"So... how is Rome these days?"

"As always... the citizens of Rome still go about looking for spectacles... and Rome's holidays are still increasing. Last month, His Majesty the Emperor's deceased son was again proclaimed the son of a god, and that day was also announced as a holiday. With that, Rome's holidays have become one hundred seventy-four in all."

"So that happened. Truly, the people of Rome are a happy people."

But in fact it was otherwise. People who could not enter the bureaucratic society of the gifted and fell behind; farmers who could no longer continue farming without profitability, sold their land, and streamed into the cities; and people flowing in from the provinces all formed a class of poor without occupations. Their dissatisfaction was directly connected to the dissatisfaction of the citizens. Thus Rome kept proclaiming holidays, opening festivals, and staging stimulating athletic contests in order to turn the citizens' dissatisfaction and attention elsewhere. The government distributed bread to the poor and drove them into the excitement of the stadiums.

"So... what games were held that day?"

"At the Circus Maximus there was a chariot race on the largest scale. Citizens and people who came up from the provinces gathered from the night before and even camped outside the stadium."

The Circus Maximus was Rome's largest stadium, capable of admitting two hundred sixty thousand spectators.

"It must have been magnificent. So who was the victor?"

The victor of the chariot race in the great stadium received the honor of a laurel wreath directly from the emperor and at once became a hero of the citizens.

"A completely new competitor. A man named Marius Andronicus..." "Is that true?"

Governor Lycomedes opened his eyes wide and suddenly embraced the young nobleman.

"Splendid, splendid! To think I had not yet heard such important news... truly remarkable. I had heard well enough of your deeds in suppressing the revolt, but I truly did not know you were a young man with enough courage to enter a chariot race."

He pushed Andronicus away again and looked at his face with emotion.

"You... know Ephesus also has a great stadium, do you not? It is slightly smaller than the Circus Maximus, but in Ionia it is the largest. How is it? We could hold a chariot race to welcome you."

"I decline, Your Excellency. Having returned to my hometown after so long, I do not wish to be covered in dust."

"So it would be. Even a warrior of the laurel wreath must wish to apply fragrant oil on his first night..."

"..."

Andronicus sprang up from his seat. A middle-aged man in a white toga stood there, stroking his long beard.

"Have you been well, Teacher Apollonius?"

He was Apollonius, the world-famous great scholar, rhetorician, and philosopher, as famous as Artemis of Ephesus herself. Though he was a Hellene, he was a great figure of the Neo-Pythagorean school who associated even with the governor like a friend, and the people of Ephesus, respecting even his hometown, called him Apollonius of Tyana. Since he was such a master, it was no wonder that the young quaestor Andronicus sprang to his feet.

When Andronicus had spoken so far, a deep voice seeped in from the direction of the entrance.

"Why do you rise, Quaestor Andronicus? Is it because you are glad to see me, or because you are impatient for the words you wish to hear from my mouth?"

On the great scholar's lips there flowed a playful sarcasm.

"My late father respected you, Teacher. Is it not natural that I should show respect?"

"Ah... Had it not been for your father, I might have nearly received a slap today."

Sitting in the chair the governor offered, he again looked at Andronicus.

"So... how is the emperor, our lord and god, Emperor Domitianus...?"

A sneer still seeped across his lips. To prevent any more jokes from coming from his mouth, Andronicus straightened his face and answered.

"Yes, he remains in good health."

"That is fortunate. Seeing that he is still healthy, it seems His Majesty the Emperor's delatores, informers, are faithfully carrying out their duty. Are senators still being dragged away by the guard these days, beaten, and killed?"

When Andronicus could not answer and hesitated, Apollonius leaned his upper body forward, and the governor too examined the young quaestor's grave expression as if curious.

"Seeing that you cannot answer, it seems there has been another imprisonment?"

Emperor Domitianus's disease of suspicion was growing worse day by day. If anything looked even a little strange, the emperor's delatores reported it, and once reported, a person was invariably arrested and imprisoned, saddled with absurd charges, and executed. The governor pressed him.

"Who was recently imprisoned?"

"His Excellency Acilius Glabrio, the former consul, and..."

At that, the governor and Apollonius alike opened their mouths. He was the very man who, until four years earlier, had been in the highest ruling office together with the emperor.

"And you mean there was another?"

"Yes. His Excellency Consul Flavius Clemens has been arrested."

"What?"

It was no wonder they were astonished. Flavius Clemens was the husband of Flavia Domitilla, daughter of Titus and niece of the emperor; he was also the current consul and a powerful candidate for emperor. Everyone had acknowledged that even if all the powerful men of Rome fell, he alone would be safe. Now they could not help being shocked that even he had been imprisoned.

Apollonius began to speak sarcastically again, in his habitual way.

"The emperor has at last begun to resemble an emperor!"

No one answered the words, but they understood their meaning. It was a sarcasm that Emperor Domitianus was coming to resemble the Emperor Nero of the past. It was also a terrifying omen that just as Nero had killed his half-brother, his teacher Seneca, and finally his mother Agrippina, then at last met his end, Domitianus too was now moving toward his final hour. To stop the great scholar's venomous words, the governor changed the subject.

"What sort of games are they holding these days in the amphitheater on the Palatine, the Colosseo?"

The Colosseo was the four-story amphitheater Emperor Vespasianus had built on the Palatine Hill, where Nero's large golden statue had stood.

"As ever. Every day there are bloody fights staged to drive the spectators into frenzy... At the games celebrating His Majesty Emperor Domitianus's birthday on October 24 last year, the number of beasts slaughtered in a single day, including lions, leopards, and elephants, reached five thousand; three hundred gladiators lost their lives, and the number of Christiani torn to death by beasts exceeded one thousand."

Lycomedes's face darkened again.

"The emperor's persecution of the Christiani is growing severe again."

"Yes. After Emperor Vespasianus died, under Emperor Titus it had been somewhat quiet, but now it seems to be beginning in earnest again."

"Just how many Christiani are there in Rome?"

"There is no way to know. Though so many are eaten by beasts in the arena, those being arrested never cease. No matter how many die, they appear again..."

Christiani was the name given to a group among the Jews who held a rather peculiar faith. In every Roman province along the Mediterranean coast, many Jews were scattered and living, and from ancient times they had believed in the god called Jehovah, whom their people served.

They had once been a people forming a considerable power. Around the time the Trojan War ended and city-states began to form in Athens and elsewhere, and when the Etruscans entered the Italian peninsula, capable monarchs such as David and Solomon appeared in Judea and extended their power. But after that, for some reason, the country split into two parts, began gradually to decline, and at last was swallowed by the kingdom of Babylon.

From that time Jews began to scatter to various places, and afterward, through continuing national suffering, namely Alexander's invasion and the religious persecution of Antiochus that followed, the number of those flowing out of the country grew ever larger. These scattered Jews were called the diaspora in Greek. They stubbornly served the god Jehovah just as in their homeland and built synagogues wherever they went.

A considerable number of this diaspora had flowed into Rome as well. And the Roman government, according to its principle of embracing the religions of every province, had permitted their synagogues and their worship of the god Jehovah. But after the middle years of Emperor Tiberius's reign, a strange change began to occur among the diaspora. That is, among the diaspora another strange faction appeared.

They were those who believed that a carpenter named Jesus, executed in Jerusalem in the seventeenth year after Tiberius ascended the throne, was the savior for whom they had been waiting. With their appearance, disputes began among the Jewish diaspora; at times they asked the Roman government for mediation, or one side accused the other.

Romans called this other faction that had arisen within the diaspora Christiani. Christus was the Greek Christos, and it meant the anointed one, that is, the Hebrew Messiah. Therefore Christiani meant those who believed in Christ.

"I heard the Christiani were only a very small part of the Jewish wanderers. I cannot understand that there are so many."

"That is so. Moreover, recently Asians who are not Jews, Hellenes, and even Roman citizens have been arrested on suspicion of being Christiani."

"Even Roman citizens...?"

"Yes. What is more astonishing is that the captured Roman citizens refuse apostasy and voluntarily walk into the place of execution."

"A frightening thing."

Apollonius muttered as though sighing.

"I had thought it strange that the Roman government, which embraces all religions, cannot accept only the Christiani... but indeed, there may be reason."

"But... what is even more astonishing..."

When Andronicus spoke so with a grave expression, the governor and Apollonius looked at each other's faces and then again fixed their attention on his mouth.

"...is that the arrest of His Excellency Glabrio and His Excellency Consul Flavius Clemens this time came because of an accusation that they were suspected Christiani."

"What?"

The governor and Apollonius asked back at the same time and opened their eyes wide.

"You mean only suspicion, surely? Surely... you do not mean that His Excellency Glabrio and His Excellency Clemens are Christiani?"

"It cannot yet be known. Not yet... because the two have made no defense at all."

"That cannot be... The husband of Domitilla, the emperor's niece, and a consul, cannot possibly have fallen into such a cult group. Perhaps... someone must have framed him. That is precisely the tyranny of the delatores."

This time Apollonius spoke toward the governor.

"As far as I know, there is still no evidence that the group of the Christiani is a cult group..."

Then Andronicus received those words.

"That is true. When I was in Rome, I too once investigated the contents of the Christiani's faith under instructions from the Senate. But... the bizarre rumors circulating among the people seemed different from the facts."

"How so...?"

"Among the rumors spread among Roman citizens, there were two that were strangest. One was that whenever they gathered for worship, they ate human flesh. The other was that in the midst of worship they held a banquet of sexual promiscuity."

"And what was the result when you investigated?"

"Those rumors had arisen precisely because of the Christiani's rites of worship. Their worship generally begins around dawn, and in its important rites there are two things. One is a rite called baptism, given to those who have resolved to enter the faith; after preparing to receive baptism by fasting, the officiant pours water three times on the believer's head. And the other is..."

A female slave dressed in an Eastern style entered, placed three cups of a drink made of fruit juice mixed with honey on the table, and slipped away.

"And there is another rite, and that is what they call the Lord's Supper."

"The Lord's Supper?"

"Yes. Jesus, whom they call Christ, had supper with his disciples on the night before he was arrested, and at that time there seems to have been something like words of farewell. That is, he broke bread and gave it to his disciples, saying that it was his body and that they should take and eat. He also passed a cup of wine to his disciples, saying that it too was his blood shed for the people of the world, and that they should take and drink. The next day he was sentenced to crucifixion, and by that the meaning of the bread and blood became reality."

Andronicus, as the governor urged, took a sip of the drink and then continued.

"The Christiani repeat this rite within their worship. That is, the officiant lifts bread, gives thanks, breaks it, and distributes it, saying that it is Christ's body and that they should eat. Next he likewise passes the cup of wine, telling them to drink Christ's blood. It seems the contents of this rite were distorted outside and gave rise to the rumor that they eat human flesh and human blood."

Apollonius nodded quietly and said,

"Mm... somehow it does feel rather eerie. But why... did that man named Jesus give his flesh and blood to the people of the world?"

"They say it was to forgive the sins of the people of the world. In other words, he took upon himself the sins of all humankind and received punishment."

"A rather romantic idea. But what in the world is sin, that they keep digging into sin? In a way it feels somewhat self-tormenting, but perhaps that is a national characteristic born because the Jewish people have suffered for far too long. But... what is the reason for saying they live in sexual disorder?"

"After the Lord's Supper, they eat together. They call this meal the love feast, and in Greek they call it agape. Because the meaning of agape in the Roman tongue is simply love, it seems the rumor arose that they hold a banquet of sexual mixing. Teacher Apollonius is here, but I have heard that originally in Greek the word that expresses love between man and woman is eros, while agape expresses philosophical, devoted mercy."

"Andronicus, you are giving a lecture in front of your teacher." "Forgive me. In any case, I think the Hellenic culture that created the word agape is great."

"What is great is not the people who created the word, but those who use it. Now... how did that man named Jesus use the word agape?"

"They say the most important words Jesus gave his disciples can be summarized in two things. One is to love God with all one's heart, will, and character... and the other is to love one's neighbor as one's own body. They say that is the meaning of agape."

"That is indeed an astonishing expression. The Jewish people truly are a people who know how to think. So do the Christiani merely say with words that they love one another?"

"When one enters their group, everyone calls one another brother or sister. Among them, rather, the rules regarding relations between men and women are very strict; if a married person has relations with someone other than the spouse, that person is immediately excommunicated."

"What... regardless of whether man or woman?"

This time Governor Lycomedes asked so.

"Yes. They say trust between husband and wife is the foundation of their faith."

Governor Lycomedes smiled faintly and said,

"This thing called Christiani... does not seem suitable for men."

"They... besides having strict relations between husband and wife... what is stranger is that they do not abandon children."

Andronicus's story made Apollonius laugh softly. For in Rome, where abandoning children was in fashion, those who did not abandon them might rather seem strange, but to Apollonius, who was listening, it was a matter of course.

"They... not only do not abandon children, but even pick up and raise children abandoned by others."

"Could that be... perhaps to raise them and sell them as slaves?"

"I too suspected that point and investigated closely, but there was no suspicion. The children raised by them, except for having become Christiani, had grown up well and were living as free persons. Also, they not only raise orphans, but care for the sick, send money and goods to slaves doing forced labor in the mines, and provide private food to those imprisoned. Such facts greatly moved the previous Emperor Titus."

Titus, who not only had trampled Jerusalem in terrible fashion according to his father's command, but was rumored to have poisoned his father and become emperor, was deeply shocked by the burial of the city of Pompeii in the very year he ascended the throne. When the wretched scene from the time he had ravaged the city of Jerusalem came alive again in his mind, he was tormented beyond measure. He could endure it no longer and poured all his strength into relief for the sick and the poor. In doing so, he met the Christiani, who were devoted to the same work as he. Though they were wanderers from the provinces, their devotion to relief astonished Titus greatly. Titus, following their example, devoted himself to relief work and died the following year.

"Then..."

Apollonius, who had been stroking his beard, looked at Andronicus while drinking the fruit juice.

"Then what part of the Christiani violates Roman law?"

Andronicus kept his mouth closed for a moment and then looked at Apollonius.

"Only... that they are dangerous."

"What is dangerous? Are they armed with spears or swords?"

"They possess nothing. And yet... many people believe their way. Not only provincial peoples from many nations, but Roman citizens too, and now even large numbers of slaves, are joining their group. They not only call even slaves brother or sister; there are even cases of free maidens marrying male slaves."

"Are such things dangerous to Rome?"

"At least... His Majesty Emperor Domitianus thinks so. They refuse to worship any god other than Jehovah..."

"And they must have refused to pay homage to Emperor Domitianus's statue too."

"...Yes."

"And they are digging into Rome's sore places. The urban poor, prisoners, failures, abandoned children, slaves in the mines..."

As Apollonius said, it was a dangerous matter. The reason only the Christiani were not accepted in Rome, where all the gods of every nation were embraced and images of those gods stood as if in competition, was precisely the danger that they dug out that truth.

"What is frightening first is their number. Because they act so secretly and gather in unexpected places such as underground tombs, it is hard to catch their tail, but once one is seized by the collar, they come out linked one after another without end. Though so many are burned to death in the arenas, nailed to crosses, and torn apart by beasts, they still pour out in masses. It is truly a frightening thing. Moreover, since the fall of Jerusalem in Emperor Vespasianus's time, even more people have been flocking into Rome. At times I myself feel as if I am living entirely among Christiani."

"Perhaps..."

Apollonius opened his mouth again.

"His Majesty the Emperor likely feels so as well."

"But... Your Excellency."

Andronicus looked at Governor Lycomedes.

"What of the Christiani in Ephesus?"

"As you know, Ephesus is a trading port, so all sorts of people come and go. In a city that lives by commerce like this, if we display a wave of arrests or a harsh atmosphere, it harms business."

Andronicus was nodding.

"But since His Majesty the Emperor's edict regarding control of the Christiani came down, I too arrested several core figures and, as an example, executed some among them, issuing threats. Thanks to that, they cannot openly preach or conduct rites of worship, but even here gatherings in underground tombs and the like seem to continue. There are times when the administration of a province must be a little different from the center. In any case, the highest goal of the Roman administration is the stability and prosperity of the empire..."

"About how many of them are there in Ephesus?"

"..."

Apollonius smiled faintly again and said,

"If they were left alone as something of little consequence, as in Ephesus, they might be nothing. Is Rome perhaps making them that much more of a problem by behaving too nervously?"

"That may be. But, Your Excellency... in fact, the special command I have brought this time also concerns just such a matter."

"A special command..."

Lycomedes leaned his upper body forward.

"Have you brought a special command to persecute the Christiani in earnest?"

Andronicus quietly shook his head.

"I have come under orders to arrest a certain person and escort him to Rome."

"Who is the person you mean to arrest? Surely not me?"

"A dreadful joke, Your Excellency."

"No, these are such murderous times that it is entirely possible."

"Your Excellency, who is the central figure of the Christiani in Ephesus?"

"As far as I know... a man named Onesimus from Colossae is said to be the leader..."

Andronicus shook his head.

"According to what I heard in Rome, there is still one very important person living in Ephesus."

"...?"

"They say Jesus, whom they believe to be Christ, originally had twelve disciples. Beginning with Peter, who was captured and killed in Rome in Emperor Nero's time, all the disciples have died, but one among them is still living here in Ephesus."

"A disciple of Jesus?"

"Yes. In particular, they say Jesus kept three among the twelve close to him, and the disciple said to be living in Ephesus is one of those three."

Lycomedes tilted his head.

"Then Lycomedes's information net is indeed so weak compared with the emperor's that it cannot even hold its own. So what do they say the survivor's name is?"

"John... They say he is called John."

"John...?"

"Yes. Among the Jews, John is a very common name. The man who appeared before Jesus and was pointed to as perhaps the savior was also named John, and the leader of the Zealots, who rose in revolt in Emperor Nero's time and met his end with the fall of Jerusalem, also had the name John."

"John... In any case, it is interesting that so important a person is in Ephesus. But Andronicus, how did you come with such a murderous mission when you were coming because of marriage?"

Only then did Apollonius bring up the matter of marriage.

"Did... did my mother ask you, Teacher?"

He nodded and gazed into the eyes of the young prospective bridegroom.

"You are a good bridegroom... but the other side is not easy either."

Andronicus's face grew tense.

"...Is it a refusal?"

"It is not a refusal, but... there is a condition attached."

"A condition...?"

"Miss Drusiana said she would consent if young Master Andronicus guarantees freedom of faith."

"Could it be... that Drusiana too has become a Christiana?"

"There is no certain proof, but... you may know this as well: the school of Tyrannus has long been used often as a meeting place for the Christiani..."

Gooseflesh rose on Andronicus's arm.

The Christiani. Who in the world are they? How have they come even to Drusiana...?

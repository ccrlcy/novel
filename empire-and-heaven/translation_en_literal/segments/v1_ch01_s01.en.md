Upon the Mediterranean, blue as glass, the sun was still pouring down its radiant light. The merchant ship Vesta had just passed between Sparta and the island of Crete and was entering the Aegean Sea, drawing a white wake behind her, and her lifted sails were filled with the swelling scent of spring. It was a noon so clear that the wings of the seabirds that now and then cut through the air seemed ready to carry away the deep blue of the sky.

"It is fine weather, dominus."

The young man whom the powerfully built man addressed as dominus, master, glanced once at the cloudless sky and then lowered his head again, sinking into thought.

Ever since the ship had entered the Aegean, a fair number of vessels with white sails had come into view. The Aegean Sea, where the peninsula of Asia Minor and the Peloponnesus of Hellas stretched out their fingers as though to clasp one another, was the route and crossroads of trade between East and West, and for that reason the coming and going of merchant ships there never ceased.

"...So six years have already passed."

The large-framed man, stealing a look at his young master's profile, was mischievously tugging at the first thread of conversation. Sure enough, the young master heard the words and lifted his head.

"That is true, Hesthenes. It has already been six years since we left Ephesus. At that time I was a foolish boy of seventeen."

Suddenly the young man's eyes began to shine with life. Surely he was tracing back some glittering memory from the time when he had been seventeen. Yet this time the man named Hesthenes, rather than following his young master's interest, deliberately turned aside.

"Indeed. It was exactly six years ago that you bought me in the slave market at the harbor of Ephesus, my lord...!"

As though he knew Hesthenes's teasing habit, the young man reached again toward the thread of memory he had found, unwilling to let it slip away.

"How might Ephesus have changed by now...?"

Though he knew that his master's concern was not really Ephesus itself, Hesthenes muttered with an untroubled air.

"It will be much the same. At the great theater on the slope of Mount Pion, tragedies of Euripides will be performed, and in the Tetragonos market merchants gathered from every nation of the world will be too busy buying and selling every kind of goods, from foodstuffs, spices, and cloth to gold, silver, jewels, and pottery."

As he said, Ephesus was the center of world trade. Roads coming over from Asia were descending the peninsula of Asia Minor along the Caicus, Hermus, and Meander rivers into the Aegean Sea, and among them especially the Persian Royal Road and the Sardis highway had Ephesus, situated at the mouth of the Cayster River, as their terminus.

For that reason, not only goods but many people from every region were pouring into Ephesus. Merchants, money changers, warehouse keepers, and carriers came there, of course, but so did lawyers who settled disputes, scribes, scholars, and artists. Countless people were streaming into Ephesus.

Thus in Ephesus, this wealthy trading port, there was nothing lacking. There were theaters, stadiums, music halls, gymnasia, and vast bathhouses for the citizens' amusement; there were also libraries and schools for the educated classes; and there was everything, even inns and brothels for travelers.

Therefore all the women of the world wished to visit Ephesus at least once. Even a woman like Cleopatra, queen of Egypt, who married Antonius after the death of Caesar, the Roman consul, is said to have stopped often in Ephesus with her husband Antonius to purchase jewels and cosmetics.

Because Ephesus was an object of longing for women, it naturally became an object to be seized whenever war broke out. This land, said to have once belonged to the nation of Amazon women, became a city that changed masters again and again, from Athens to Persia, from Persia to Alexander, and then to Rome, after it was occupied by Androcles, prince of Athens.

For that reason Ephesus was still even now a city of women. The goddess who ruled Ephesus was Artemis, the goddess of abundance with twenty-four breasts, and it was no exaggeration to say that all the luxuries traded in Ephesus existed for women alone. Persia, and now even Rome, the greatest empire in history, had begun to fall into shadow amid the fragrance of women's perfume.

Hesthenes continued to mutter.

"In the great arena, gladiators and beasts are probably still bleeding and dying. In the great square of the Arcadiane, scholars will still be discussing life and philosophy... In the Prytaneion, the sacred fire of Artemis will still be burning... and from the girls' school on Curetes Street, braided maidens will come chattering out...."

At precisely that point the young man snatched at the tail end of Hesthenes's words.

"That is right. Drusiana was a little innocent attending that girls' school then."

Hesthenes, having at last placed the thread of memory into his master's hand, finally smiled faintly and gazed at the islands floating idly at ease. Yet his ears were still open, waiting for the next words from his master.

"How might Drusiana have changed by now...?"

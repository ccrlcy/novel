# Empire and Heaven (II)

## Chapter 9. The Gospel of John (2)

Philip and Nathanael were already there, and adding the brothers John and James, and the brothers Simon and Andrew, the disciples of Jesus had become six in all. But he added again Levi, a publicanus seated at the customs post in Capernaum, and Levi's younger brother James. Levi, originally descended from a priestly house, thought it shameful that in order to make a living he had to serve as a tax collector while receiving the pointing fingers of his own people. Upon entering the company of Jesus, he changed his name to Matthew, and later came to write the Gospel of Matthew.

At just about that time, John of Gischala, who had joined the Zealot party, sent a man. He too was a man called Simon, of that same Zealot party, and he came seeking John bringing with him a friend named Judas from Kerioth. According to what he said, John of Gischala held a fairly important place within the Zealot party. Hearing the rumor of Jesus who had appeared in Capernaum, he had dispatched the party member Simon in order to identify who he was. It was his intention to plant Simon among those close to Jesus and grasp his movements from time to time. John introduced Simon and his friend Judas together to Jesus, and they too entered his company. Then Simon brought still another friend, Thaddaeus. He intended to use him in time of need as a liaison with the Zealot party.

Once Simon from the Zealot party became part of the group, Simon the fisherman threw away that name and changed it to the name Peter, which Jesus had given him. And Peter, who loved to push himself forward, then brought still another fisherman. The man called Thomas seemed very solid, both in build and in temperament.

So it happened that before they knew it, Peter's influence had quietly grown within the company of Jesus.

Together with his own brother, and Philip, and Nathanael whom Philip had introduced, and Thomas as well, there were five in Peter's line; in Simon the Zealot's line there were three; then there were the brothers of Matthew and the brothers of John.

Originally John had regarded Peter, who worked on his father Zebedee's boat, as a man belonging on his own side. But day by day Peter was solidifying a singular position among the disciples of Jesus. Moreover, John had already been left vulnerable to Peter because when he first met Jesus he had misjudged him. And besides that, Peter was one who loved to put himself forward, and although because of that temperament he often made mistakes, he was by no means rarely praised by Jesus.

At last the full-scale miracles by Jesus began to appear from the time he healed Peter's mother-in-law of a fever. Countless people who had heard the rumor of Jesus came carrying the sick upon their backs and shoulders, and many among those patients experienced miracles of healing. Lepers were cleansed, and paralytics began to walk and leap. Jesus raised to life the dead daughter of Jairus, ruler of the synagogue at Capernaum; he opened the eyes of the blind, unstopped the ears of the deaf, and brought back to clear reason those who had been possessed by demons. When this great procession of miracles had reached its height, Peter won from Jesus a decisive recognition. When they were in the region of Caesarea Philippi, Jesus asked his disciples,

"Who do the people of the world say that I am?"

The disciples each repeated what they had heard from the people.

"They say you are like John the Baptizer."

"Some said Elijah."

"There are some who said Jeremiah."

"In any case, they all seem to think you are one of the prophets."

Then Jesus asked again,

"Then... who do you yourselves think that I am?"

The disciples were suddenly struck dumb. No good thought came to mind at once. It was because, like the people, they themselves too had been thinking that perhaps he was one of the prophets, like John the Baptizer or Elijah or Jeremiah. Then suddenly Peter, who loved to step forward, blurted out,

"You are the Messiah, the Son of the living God!"

It was truly a surprising and daring expression. John looked at Peter with inward displeasure, thinking that he had babbled a foolish thing. But unexpectedly, Jesus praised Peter with astonishing words.

"Son of Jonah, blessing shall be upon you. The one who taught you this is the Father who is in heaven. As I called you rock, so now upon that rock I will build my church."

John's face changed color at once, and the other disciples too were beside themselves at these tremendous words of praise. But Jesus made a declaration even more tremendous than that.

"I will give to you the keys of the kingdom of heaven. Whatever you bind on earth shall be bound in heaven also, and whatever you loose on earth shall be loosed in heaven also...."

At these words of Jesus, John felt the blood of his whole body grow cold. What in the world was Peter? Was not Peter the unlearned fisherman who worked on his father Zebedee's boat? And yet Jesus had bestowed astonishing praise and promise upon that Peter.

Truly a rival had appeared from a place he had never expected. At once the blood of jealousy began to boil in John's breast, and the same was true of his brother James. Because of jealousy toward Peter, John's words at times grew violent, and whenever that happened, John was instead rebuked by Jesus.

When he proudly reported that he had seen a man casting out demons in Jesus' name and had forbidden him to do so, Jesus rather reproved him. He pointed out that the one who was not against them was for them. And another time, when they were passing through a village in the region of Samaria and the people treated them coldly, John became angry and said,

"Teacher, if you permit it, we will command fire to fall from heaven and smash them to pieces."

and for that had once been sternly scolded.

Yet Jesus too recognized that the place of the brothers was important. Moreover, John among them maintained ties with both Peter's side and the Zealot side, so his role could not be ignored. Therefore, whenever Jesus went to an important place, he always took along those brothers together with Peter.

Even when he revived the daughter of Jairus, ruler of the synagogue at Capernaum, Jesus allowed only those three to be present; and even when he went up a mountain to pray an important prayer, he took only the three of them.

Yet to John, Peter was a rival, and his brother James too was a rival. He could not rest easy simply because Jesus was giving those three special treatment. John was not a man who could be second. When Jesus the Messiah should free the nation of Judea and ascend the throne, John was not a man who would merely gain some seat or other and let it be enough. He had to become first. He had to become the chief magistrate of the government and commander in chief of the army. Only then could he launch the great march of revenge against the Roman Empire, to which Vespasianus belonged.

After hearing Jesus' praise at Caesarea Philippi, Peter was day by day solidifying the place of leadership. He was not only the oldest among them in age, but because he had the temperament that loved to step forward, he had begun to act as the practical representative among the disciples.

But John could not face Peter in open opposition. Not only would his pride not allow him to confront Peter, who had worked on his father Zebedee's boat, but if things went wrong, he might lose all five who stood on Peter's side. Therefore, while outwardly remaining close to Peter, John was watching for an opportunity to make use of him.

At the same time, John began to keep close company with the three from the Zealot side in order to draw them over to his own side. The one who perceived this intention of John's and approached him was Judas from Kerioth.

Judas was a young man who seemed very careful and sparing in speech. Among the disciples who were always chattering, he belonged rather to the thinking sort, and his thoughtful attitude made people feel trust toward him. He seemed to know more than any of the disciples, and to have studied much as well. Whenever John was alone, Judas would quietly come near and speak with him about the future of the nation or the political system of the Roman Empire, and John came to think that this was exactly the man who must be kept in his own hand. From John's point of view, Judas's knowledge and character seemed the most suitable for a first-rate chief adviser. Thus when the offerings coming in from the followers of Jesus had increased to the point that they were difficult to manage, John recommended Judas as the man to control them.

No one was inclined to oppose the idea that Judas should manage the money. If it were a matter of financial management, naturally the task ought to have fallen to Matthew, who had once been a tax collector, and yet no one said it should be entrusted to Matthew. Though he had already become a disciple of Jesus, the record that he had once done shameful things in the past was still preventing him from receiving full trust.

In any case, Judas carried out the difficult task of financial management without trouble. Every day he recorded and calculated without omission the money that came in and went out, reported it to Jesus, and disclosed it to all the disciples. And all the disciples were thinking themselves fortunate to have a man such as Judas, to whom such troublesome work could be entrusted. Meanwhile, the presence of Judas of Kerioth was little by little beginning to stand out among the disciples.

Innumerable crowds followed Jesus, and the disciples too went about in excitement because of it. Moreover, because Jesus recognized even the disciples as having the authority to heal disease and administer baptism, it was as hard as plucking stars from heaven even for people merely to get a chance to meet the disciples. The sick and the demon-possessed were endlessly crowding in to meet them.

The moment when a sudden change came over Jesus' attitude was precisely when such things had reached their height. When the whole city of Capernaum was boiling over because of the matter of Jesus, he suddenly got into a boat and moved to the eastern shore of the Sea of Galilee. There again, by healing a madman possessed by demons, his rumor spread through all the region of Gadara; and by the time he reached the fields of Bethsaida, there were already seas of people because of those who had followed from Capernaum and those who had chased after him from all the borders of Gadara.

To the crowds gathered in this wilderness, Jesus again began to teach the things of the kingdom of heaven. Because of Jesus' teaching and healing of the sick, a whole day passed in an instant, and as evening drew on Jesus said to his disciples,

"How pitiful these people are. Are they not just like sheep without a shepherd? Now the day has grown dark and they must be hungry, so food should be given them."

The disciples, in confusion, looked toward Judas, who was in charge of the finances, and when Judas showed a troubled face, Philip stepped forward and said,

"To feed all these people is utterly impossible with our funds, and even if we had the money, this is a wilderness, so we could not buy food and bring it back. It would be best to send them away so that each may go and eat."

But Jesus, after thinking for a moment, said again to the disciples,

"See whether anyone among the people has some food."

The disciples went about inquiring what the people had. Andrew brought five barley loaves and two fish that he had obtained from a certain small boy. Then Jesus instructed the disciples to divide the people into groups of about fifty and have them sit down on the grass. They were to recline with their legs slanted out, leaning upon the ground, according to the posture in which the Jews sat when they took a meal. In an instant, one hundred round banquet groups were prepared in the wilderness of Bethsaida. And that was only the places for the men; if the children and the women too were counted, the number of the crowd was far more than ten thousand.

When everyone was seated round about in circles, Jesus took the barley loaves and the fish and offered a prayer of thanks toward heaven. Then he distributed the bread and the fish to the disciples and told them to carry them and set them among the people. The disciples, doing as Jesus had told them, went about among the groups carrying the very small amount of bread and fish. And then a strange thing began to happen. Whenever they had taken food to the people and came back to Jesus, Jesus was still holding the food just as before. The disciples delivered food to each of the more than one hundred round groups, yet still Jesus had food in his hands.

Not only that, but now something marvelous was happening in the groups where the people were seated. However much they took and ate, the bread and fish lying on the grass remained just as they were.

At last a great banquet took place in which more than ten thousand people ate in one place. The bread and the fish remained even after all had eaten their fill, so Jesus instructed the disciples to gather them all up. What remained filled twelve baskets.

Then suddenly a strange change began to arise among the crowds. The people, who had been murmuring among themselves, now began shouting in loud voices.

"How long will you leave the Messiah in the open field?"

"We must enthrone Jesus as king! The day has come for Judea to be restored!"

In an instant these cries began to spread through the whole field. When such a thing actually came to pass, the hearts of the disciples were pounding like hammers. At last the day had come for Jesus to sit upon the throne and rule the Gentile nations with a rod of iron. How long they had counted on their fingers and waited for that day. Since the time when Israel fell to Assyria and Judea fell to Babylon, they had waited for hundreds of years with tears. And now the Messiah had appeared before them bringing immense miracles. There was no longer any need to endure, nor any need to hesitate. Now the Jewish people, who had been asleep, could rise up. The Messiah holding the rod of iron was standing before them. And behind them there was the Zealot party, which had sharpened its swords waiting for this day; there were the leaders of the Pharisees, who had kept the Law at the cost of their lives and waited for today; and above all there was the Jewish people, who had recovered a new hope. Now, if only Jesus of Nazareth would lift high the banner of Jehovah and give the order to march, Judea, the kingdom of God, was at the point of revealing its splendid form.

Yet at that decisive moment, the one whose attitude suddenly turned upside down was Jesus of Nazareth himself. For some reason his face grew pale, and in haste he gathered the disciples together, slipped out from among the crowds, and headed for the seashore. Hastily he put the disciples into the boat and, telling them to meet him in Capernaum, urged them to leave at once.

The disciples, swept into the boat before they knew what was happening, were on the way toward Capernaum and sat bewildered because of Jesus' sudden change. And when the boat came out into the middle of the sea, they finally began to complain.

"What in the world came over the Teacher so suddenly?"

"When the whole thing was ready to happen, for him to drive us off like this?"

"Could it be that he means to shake us off and then ascend the throne by himself?"

Then among the disciples there arose again an argument, some saying they should go back to Bethsaida, and others saying that since the Teacher had told them to meet him in Capernaum, they ought to go on to Capernaum as they were. The hidden rivalries that until now had only been cautiously watching one another within, now that gain had begun to appear before their eyes, were finally bursting out into the open.

Peter, in Peter's own fashion, insisted that they should go to Capernaum as if naturally he were the one responsible; while Simon and those on the Zealot side argued that they should return to Bethsaida. James and John, and Matthew and his younger brother, likewise differed from one another in opinion, and Philip, who belonged to Peter's side, also belonged to those who said they should go back. But their quarrel did not last long.

Suddenly the wind began to blow in, and the waves began to surge, and the boat shook as if it would immediately overturn.

Peter, Andrew, Thomas, and the others, all famous at the handling of boats, came forward and strove to steady it, but it was useless. The boat was plunging wildly and drifting amid the waves. On the pitch-black sea they had now fallen into utter despair and were exhausted. It was then that John suddenly saw something pale appearing in the darkness and cried out,

"What is that?"

It was the shape of a person. Across the tossing waves of the dark night sea a man was walking toward them. And his voice was heard.

"Do not be afraid."

Astonishingly, the one who had walked upon the water was their Teacher Jesus himself. When Jesus came up into the boat, the waves were becoming calm as though by a lie. Once more the disciples witnessed an astonishing event and lost all power of speech. Only after a long time did they recover themselves and look for the form of Jesus. Already Jesus, looking weary, had fallen asleep against the gunwale.

Yet even after their company returned to Capernaum, the people would not leave them alone. Countless people kept following Jesus and urging him to build the new kingdom. At last Jesus took the disciples and withdrew to the region of Lebanon. But this time the leaders of Lebanon came to seek Jesus. They had already heard all the fame of Jesus that was spreading up from Galilee. They too, since the fall of the Syrian dynasty, had lived under the rule of the Roman Empire while dreaming of the day of their recovery. They came to Jesus and proposed that if he had no intention of raising a power in Judea, he should launch the undertaking in the region of Lebanon.

But Jesus coldly refused their proposal. And so, while passing through the region of Tyre, when a Gentile woman came seeking him and asking that he heal her daughter's derangement of mind, Jesus answered her with insulting words,

"Do you think there is one who throws to the dogs the bread that should be fed to the children?"

But the woman clung to him and begged, saying,

"Yet even the dogs pick up and eat the crumbs that fall from their master's table, do they not?"

and at last her daughter's affliction of mind was healed. Seeing that even in Gentile territory they would not leave him alone, Jesus said with a sound almost like a sigh,

"...Foxes have holes, and the birds of the air have nests to which they return, but I have nowhere to lay my head."

At last Jesus returned again to Capernaum. But this time his younger brothers too were pressing him. They kept urging him to go up to Jerusalem when this Feast of Booths came.

The Feast of Booths was the fifteen-day festival in which all the Jews came out from their houses and lived in tents while waiting for the Messiah. The three great festivals kept by the Jewish people were Passover, when they came out from Egypt; the Feast of Weeks, held forty-nine days after they had entered the land of Canaan and offered the first fruits; and the Feast of Booths, in which they remembered the miracles of the wilderness and waited for the Messiah. And among those festivals, the one the Jewish people awaited with the greatest dream and hope was the Feast of Booths. The reason Jesus' younger brothers kept urging him to go up to Jerusalem at the Feast of Booths was precisely that they wished him to proclaim his royal entry as the Messiah.

"Yet... however much his younger brothers urged him, Jesus did not yield to it. He said that the present was the time when he must suffer and the time when he must die. He said that he could never go to the Feast of Booths by leaping over Passover and the Feast of Weeks. Because Passover was the feast of sprinkling the blood of the lamb, and the Feast of Weeks was the feast of offering the first fruits, only by passing through those two feasts could the Messiah's Feast of Booths finally come. Thus Jesus' younger brothers alone went first up to Jerusalem, while we remained in Capernaum with him. But after several days had passed, Jesus secretly told us. He said that we were to go up to Jerusalem.... None of us could help but be tense...."

Polycarp and Papias, and all the others who were present there as well, began to grow tense. That part, missing even from the records of Mark and Matthew and Luke, was now flowing out from the mouth of the apostle. The story of the final six months of Jesus' life was now removing its veil.

"Because the rumor of Jesus had already spread even to Jerusalem... he warned us to be cautious and went up to Jerusalem secretly...."

Though it was indeed a secret infiltration, the disciples who had gone up to Jerusalem began to grow busy. Now they judged that the time of Jesus' rule was near, and each began actively to contact the supporting powers behind him.

First, Simon, who had been dispatched by the Zealot party, was keeping close communication with the headquarters of the party and waiting for the decisive moment. Naturally John too received contact from John of Gischala. They were even sending detailed plans of operation telling how they themselves would act when the time came for Jesus to rise. But John could not do so foolish a thing as hand the initiative over to the Zealot party. While keeping connections with the Zealots on the one hand, he was also staying in close communication with Annas in Jerusalem. In the end, he meant to balance the Zealot party and the Sanhedrin council evenly and become the central figure of the ruling power.

But the problem lay on the side of the Sanhedrin. However earnestly John explained the things he himself had witnessed, they would not believe him at all. Rather, the religious leaders, including Annas, were guessing that Jesus must surely be a magician or a disciple of demons, and were determined that some measure had to be taken before his influence grew further.

Their policy in this direction at last began in earnest when the incident at Siloam took place.

Jesus, who had been moving about hidden and not showing his true face, met a blind man within the city of Jerusalem. The disciples, irritated by the business of hiding and moving about, slyly asked a question in order to induce a miracle from Jesus.

"Teacher... this man, they say, was blind from birth. Because of whose sin is that? Because of his own sin? Or because of his parents' sin?"

Because their Scriptures said that all misfortune came from sin, the disciples asked in that way. But Jesus' answer was clear.

"It is not because of this man's sin, nor because of his parents' sin. It is only that the works of God might be manifested in him."

Jesus spat upon the ground, kneaded the earth into mud, put it upon the man's eyes, and said,

"Go and wash your eyes in the pool of Siloam."

That blind man, with the mud on his eyes and knowing nothing of the reason, went all the way to the pool of Siloam and washed his eyes. And for the first time in his life he came to see the world. He went all through the city of Jerusalem boasting of this fact. In an instant the rumor spread that Jesus had come into the city. The Sanhedrin council, stiff with tension, summoned the parents of the blind man and interrogated them, and it was confirmed that he had indeed been blind from birth. Perplexed, they brought in the man whose eyes had been opened and tried again to browbeat him. It was God who had opened your eyes, not the Jesus of whom you speak, they said. And they warned him not to go about talking idly of Jesus' deeds, because Jesus was a sinner. But hearing the words of these high men, he only tilted his head in puzzlement. He could not understand how it could be a sin to make a man's eyes see.

At last the Sanhedrin council began discussing concretely what should be done with Jesus. They were afraid of only one thing: that if the people followed Jesus and rebelled against Rome, they might be stripped of the freedom of religion they had only barely obtained from Rome.

Once the Sanhedrin took this course, Jesus' disciples, John among them, had no choice but to give up cooperation with them. Not only would they not acknowledge the miracles of Jesus that John had conveyed to them, they were rather preparing to check him; and John, sensing this, judged that there was nothing more to expect either from Annas or from the council. As a second-best course, John attempted contact with the Pharisees. In any case, the cooperation of the religious leaders was necessary for the restoration of Judea. But the Pharisees were men who, even under dreadful persecution, had kept the Law at the cost of their lives, and they despised the chief priests who had yielded to Rome and compromised. Thus John made contact with the Pharisees and introduced several of them to Jesus. But Jesus, after meeting several Pharisaic men and even sharing meals with them, saw that they were excessively dogmatic and loathed even to share a place with sinners such as harlots and tax collectors, and in burning anger rebuked them. Jesus denounced them as formalists filthy as whitewashed tombs.

When the Sanhedrin and the Pharisees alike had both failed, the disciples no longer had anything to hope for in Jerusalem. Now there was no course left but to undertake the rising centered on the Zealot forces in Galilee and the Jews of Galilee.

Even the thoughtful Judas approved of this Galilean line. In his judgment, the leaders of Jerusalem had already lost the confidence due to them as leaders of the nation. Therefore, when he said that the Galileans must seize the initiative of the new government, there was no one who opposed him. Jesus' disciples were all Galileans, and there was no reason to oppose the idea that Galileans should do it among themselves. It appeared something altogether natural. Yet in this Galilean line of the disciples there still remained one problem.

Jesus, once up in Jerusalem, stayed as usual in the house of Lazarus at Bethany outside the city, and the disciples likewise were lodging in that house. But just as when John first met Jesus, Jesus was keeping especially close company with Lazarus. He never even rebuked him as he did the other disciples from time to time, and whenever he came back to Bethany he remained attached to him. Jesus' attitude toward Lazarus was more than that of a friend, and Lazarus's sisters Martha and Mary too served Jesus almost as though they were of one household with him. To anyone's eyes it seemed obvious that if Jesus built the new kingdom, he would set Lazarus in the first place. Before long Lazarus was beginning to rise as a common enemy of the twelve disciples of Jesus.

It was then that the incident of the Feast of Dedication took place. The Feast of Dedication was the day commemorating the restoration of the temple of Jerusalem by Judah Maccabee, who had risen in revolt against the Antiochian dynasty. The temple of Jerusalem was the spiritual pillar and stronghold of faith for the Jewish people. To them this temple was more precious than life itself, so that whenever national disaster came they repaired the temple, and even when they returned from captivity in Babylon, the very first thing they did was rebuild the temple.

Yet on the day commemorating the restoration of that temple, Jesus spoke words in that very temple of Jerusalem that proved fatal. The people of Jerusalem who had gathered in the temple kept questioning him stubbornly.

"How long will you keep us in this suffocating uncertainty? If you are the Messiah, then say so."

Then Jesus, who had sunk into thought for a moment, lifted his eyes and said,

"Do you not hear though you have heard, and do you not see though you have seen? If you had seen the things I have done, ought you not already to have known? Do you think that God is in this temple?"

To the people who stood bewildered, Jesus said,

"...God is in me. He and I are one."

Hearing his words, the people who had been listening suddenly began to rage. They said that Jesus' words had blasphemed their temple. People began to pick up stones. As the atmosphere grew dangerous, the disciples gathered around Jesus and tried to withdraw from that place. Stones began to fly. In an instant the crowd that had sought the Messiah had turned into a crowd seeking to kill Jesus. Struck by stones, Jesus' company barely escaped through the crowd, and that very day they fled Jerusalem to hide themselves. In a single rush they ran down through the valley of Achor, passed Jericho, and fled all the way to Bethabara, where John had first seen Jesus.

Once they had run breathlessly down as far as Bethabara, Jesus' disciples at last began to pour out complaints against their Teacher. Because of Jesus' irresolution, they said, they were continually losing decisive opportunities.

The best opportunity had surely been at Bethsaida. They argued that if Jesus had accepted it when the excited people rose up and sought to enthrone him, the uprising would immediately have succeeded. But even then, the disciples had been able to endure it because they guessed that Jesus was waiting for the time when he would enter Jerusalem.

The second opportunity had been the Feast of Booths. The people gathered from the whole nation were looking for the Messiah. The Feast of Booths was the festival of waiting for the Messiah, and if Jesus were the Messiah, they had expected that he would certainly come to Jerusalem at the Feast of Booths. But at that good opportunity Jesus had gone about hiding himself without showing himself. And then Jesus had not even dreamed of launching any undertaking, and now once more he had let the opportunity of the Feast of Dedication slip away. When the people asked Jesus whether he was the Messiah, the disciples had expected a clear answer from him. But instead Jesus committed a decisive blunder.

The disciples thought that all these things were because of Lazarus. They believed that if one traced things back, the reason Jesus had delayed and not seized the decisive opportunity at Bethsaida was because he was waiting for the time to act together with Lazarus, and that the reason he was still delaying the undertaking in Jerusalem was because he was planning some separate design together with Lazarus.

Thus the disciples who had come down to Bethabara strongly urged Jesus to return to Galilee. But when Jesus, ignoring their words, lingered there at Bethabara, someone came from Bethany.

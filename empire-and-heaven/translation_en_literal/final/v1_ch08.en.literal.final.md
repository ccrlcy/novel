# Empire and Heaven (I)

## Chapter 8. The Final Cross

Together with the couple Marius Andronicus and Drusiana, the boy Gaius, his steward Lucius, the servant Hesthenes, their other attendants, and John with the men who had followed him, Tychicus and Prochorus, the whole company had landed at the port of Puteoli, passed through Capua, and was now heading straight for Rome.

This Appian Way leading from Rome to Capua was the great trunk road built more than four hundred years earlier by the consul Appius Claudius, who had expanded Rome's territory across Italy, in order to govern those lands. Broad, black paving stones were laid across the wide road, and along its sides oleanders grew here and there among olive trees and evergreens.

Andronicus tried to place John on horseback, but he stubbornly refused to ride. Not only that, insisting that he was a man under arrest and being escorted away, he himself asked to be bound. Unable to overcome the old apostle's firm demand, Andronicus ordered his men to bind him loosely.

The hearts of old Tychicus and Prochorus, who were walking after the bound apostle, were deeply troubled. Many had wished to go with the apostle, but after repeated and careful consultation, the elders of the Ephesian church had agreed to send old Tychicus, who knew the conditions of Rome, and the courageous Prochorus. The Apostle John whom they were accompanying was a treasure among all the churches. At a time when the churches were growing weak, he was the only living witness of Christ.

Once he entered Rome, what would happen to him was plain. Either he would be nailed to a cross, or burned, or else become fodder for wild beasts or a plaything for gladiators before the eyes of all.

Therefore Prochorus still held one plan in his heart. If some opportunity came, he meant to flee from Andronicus's company with the Apostle John thrown over his own back.

Yet regardless of their troubled hearts, John walked the Appian Way with a face full of deep feeling.

Traditionally, this Appian Way was the road by which triumphant generals, returning after conquering foreign enemies, entered Rome. At the end of the road outside the walls of Rome stood the villas of the nobles, and when a victorious general arrived there with the troops under his command, he would be entertained in those villas while awaiting the emperor's order to enter the city.

Yet the Appian Way was also a road of tragedy. When Pompey returned from Hispania after suppressing the slave revolt of Spartacus, he had six thousand crosses set up along this Appian Way under the pretext of preventing anything similar, and hung the rebel slaves upon them to die.

After that as well, many victorious generals passed along this road. Julius Caesar passed by, and Antony, returning in triumph from the Parthian frontier, passed by, and then Octavianus, who defeated Antony at Actium, passed by. And on that very road along which so many victorious generals had passed, there also passed Peter, the fisherman of Galilee, and Paul, who called himself a soldier of Christ. Peter, who had followed Jesus in high spirits knowing almost nothing, and Paul, who had turned his life upside down from student of the great scholar Gamaliel into a wandering leatherworker, had all walked this road to lay down their lives.

And again, this Appian Way was also the road by which the commander Flavius Vespasianus, appointed to crush the Jewish revolt, had wheeled his horse's head about and driven his elite troops upon Rome in order to seize the imperium that ruled the world; and it was also the road by which his son Titus, taking over his father's task, had triumphantly returned after annihilating Jerusalem.

John, who had suffered that terrible outrage on the shore at Tiberias, had all along watched Vespasianus's course, grinding the blade of revenge. Yet Vespasianus had run one road of success after another as though rising like the morning sun, while John himself had walked a road of disappointment and frustration.

At the time when Vespasianus, like other ambitious young men, had joined the cavalry at seventeen, completed his frontier posting at Tiberias, moved to Gaul, won brilliant military glory, and become quaestor at twenty-five, John was leaving Jerusalem, now made dangerous by Stephen's martyrdom, and walking a miserable road of flight with Mary.

Around the time when Vespasianus, at the extraordinary age of twenty-nine, had risen to become aedile, married Flavia Domitilla, a young lady of a noble house, and begotten his first son Titus, John was wandering in Samaria. And while Vespasianus, now at last praetor, was leading the Second Augustan Legion, conquering Germania and Britannia and building radiant military achievements, John was again fleeing the persecution of Herod Agrippa.

When famine had struck Jerusalem as well, and Mary and John were barely keeping themselves alive by means of the offering that Paul had brought from the church in Antioch, Vespasianus had marched in triumph by this very Appian Way and received the Ornamenta Triumphalia, the decorations of victory.

Amid those harsh sufferings, John next heard the news that Domitianus, Vespasianus's second son, had been born. It was a strange birth, coming twelve years after Titus. Having traced Vespasianus's course even thus far, John at last had to gnash his teeth and break off his obsession with revenge. Moving together with Mary to Antioch, he was pastoring the church there while swallowing down his burning anger.

During that time, news arrived that a great fire had broken out in Rome, that countless Christiani had been slaughtered under accusation of arson, and that Peter and Paul had met their end amid it all. News also arrived that the young worker Timothy had been martyred in Ephesus. As John wiped away tears with his clenched fist while hearing those grief upon grief, at last there came word that John of Gischala, leader of the Zealots, had raised an uprising in Jerusalem. The Zealots, enraged by the extortion and slaughter committed by the new governor Gessius Florus, had finally begun to act.

John was by then already a middle-aged man whose hair had begun to go gray, yet at last he had tried to run to John of Gischala. But the one who checked his exploding passion was none other than Mary, the mother of Jesus. She clung to John and urged him to go with her to Ephesus, where the church had been scattered to the winds. John felt as if he would go mad. Since Jesus had nicknamed him a Son of Thunder, his whole body shook under the weight of his emotion. Yet what held him fast with stronger power was the image of Jesus upon the cross at Golgotha, entrusting his mother to him.

In the end John broke off his decision to run to Jerusalem. Leaving behind Jerusalem, where the anger of the nation was blazing, he had to stagger toward Ephesus with the aged Mary on his back. Without even trying to wipe the tears that flowed, he cried out into the empty air:

(Ah, why have you entrusted to me a thing so difficult?)

But when Mary's sorrowful life came to an end in the olive grove of Ortygia, John again began to listen to the noise of struggle coming from Jerusalem. By then the Jewish uprising had altogether taken on the shape of rebellion, and the young priest Josephus had been given charge of the rebel forces in Galilee. At last John could no longer endure it and ran out of Ephesus toward Antioch. He meant to pass through Antioch, infiltrate Galilee, and join Josephus's rebel army. And there at last, in Antioch, he encountered Vespasianus, whom he had never forgotten even in dreams.

Appointed supreme commander of the army sent to crush the Jewish revolt, Vespasianus had come through Achaia and crossed the Hellespont by land, reached Antioch, and was making preparations for war while waiting for the legion of his son Titus coming up from Alexandria. He had made Antioch his base of operations.

The place where John met Vespasianus was on the great marble avenue built by Herod. Vespasianus had just come out from the residence of Licinius Mucianus, governor of Syria, and was on his way to inspect his troops making ready to march along the Orontes River. John had no wish to avoid the Roman commander and so stopped walking, staring at the man approaching on horseback. Seeing John stand his ground, Vespasianus halted his horse and ordered the officers following him to question the man. John recognized him at once, but Vespasianus did not recognize John. The officers surrounded him and asked his identity.

"What sort of fellow are you? You are a Jew, aren't you?"

Jews and Romans were easy to tell apart, since Jews did not shave like Romans, but wore beards. John answered none of their questions and only glared at Vespasianus. At length Vespasianus, finding this strange, dismounted and came near.

"Stand aside. It seems this Jew has business with me."

Coming forward with a cold smile, he looked into John's face and said,

"Speak, you rustic Jew. What do you want with me?"

John thought that at last the time he had waited for had come. He drew out the blade he had kept hidden in his breast and, glaring at Vespasianus, said,

"Vespasianus, I challenge you to a duel. A commander of Rome who boasts gentlemanly courage will not cowardly turn tail, will he?"

Vespasianus looked over John's appearance with a cold smile.

"Who in the world are you? What sort of man dares level a blade against me, Vespasianus, whom all the world fears?"

From John's eyes there now began once more to blaze out the blue fire of his youth.

"Vespasianus, you surely remember that forty-one years ago, while serving with the cavalry at Tiberias, you abducted a Greek maiden from the seashore?"

"Tiberias...?"

He searched his memory for a moment, then nodded.

"Hm... now I think I understand. That was when you twisted my arm, was it not? You are a fellow who remembers truly useless things. It seems that memory is bringing poison upon your own head today."

"Vespasianus, what did you do with the girl you abducted then?"

Again he smiled coldly.

"Fool. Did you think I remembered broken toys like you do? If you wanted her so badly, you should have waited until I had played with her and thrown her away, then picked her up. Why come asking now, you dull Jew? By now she will either be an old crone or a dead skeleton, so if you must, go and look for a new woman."

"Draw your sword, Vespasianus. I will teach you how precious a human life is."

Now there was no way for him to avoid it. Before his subordinate officers he could not show himself cowardly. Slowly he drew the sword at his waist. The officers, already understanding their superior's intention, began stepping back one by one. The two grown men, blades leveled, glared at one another with terrible eyes. Tight tension filled the marble road.

John's sword first lured the other in. He feinted as though attacking, then drew his own sword back in reverse. As the other man's blade unknowingly followed inward, John shifted his legs and struck down against the other's blade. Vespasianus's posture broke and he staggered badly. Without giving him room, John pressed in. From childhood John had trained in swordsmanship in preparation for the day when he might join his country's war of independence. While Vespasianus shrank back in surprise at his opponent's fierce edge, John quickly wound around and snatched away the other's blade. Before Vespasianus had time even to respond, John's sword had gone around beneath his arm and was darting like an arrow toward his throat. The surrounding officers all seized their hilts together, but there was nothing they could do. The next instant a hole should have opened in the neck of Vespasianus, the most feared man in Rome.

At that very moment John suddenly heard a voice in his ears like a thunderbolt.

"Put up your sword again into its place: for all they that take the sword shall perish with the sword!"

It was the very voice of Jesus, the carpenter of Nazareth. John did not know from where that voice had come. His sword, rushing in like lightning, froze in the air. His entire body hardened in that place like fossil stone.

Vespasianus, who had thought himself finished, barely recovered his stance, and only then did the officers around him draw their swords and surround John.

Standing there as though petrified, John at last let his sword fall to the ground. Then he collapsed where he stood and burst into loud weeping.

"Lord! Why did you stop me?"

Looking at John, who had halted his blade at that absolute and final moment, Vespasianus wore a deeply bewildered expression. He could not bring himself to order his men to arrest John. After looking down for a long while at John as he beat the earth and sobbed, he signaled to his men and quietly withdrew. And John remained sitting there upon the marble road, weeping and weeping for a long time.

In that way John returned once more to Ephesus. And in the hut at Ortygia where Mary had lived, day and night he pondered the things concerning Jesus. Always he cried out toward heaven:

"Where are you now? The friends who ran to be your witnesses to the ends of the earth according to your command have now all fallen beneath the blade of the Roman Empire. But by that, what in this world has been made any better?"

Then to John in Ephesus came the news of Vespasianus's accession. He had at last become Caesar Vespasianus Augustus, lord emperor holding the whole world in his hand. Then came news that in Judea his son Titus had surrounded Jerusalem with four legions. And finally Jerusalem suffered its terrible destruction. On the day he heard it, John again knelt in the olive grove of Ortygia. The tragic figure of John of Gischala, who had once said he would join the Zealots, kept rising before him.

"When are you coming? John of Gischala has gone, and the people of Jerusalem have all gone. Are you satisfied now? For whom are you coming to bring salvation? Those who nailed you up have perished, and all the prophecies have been fulfilled, so come now. Come as the glorious Messiah over those miserable ruins. Corpses and maggots will welcome you. There will be no colt for you to ride and no branches to spread on the road. If you are going to come again to the Mount of Olives in Jerusalem, full of the stench of rotting flesh, then come. If this was your plan, was not everything indeed accomplished splendidly?"

Jesus of Nazareth had always spoken not of the kingdoms of this world but of the kingdom of God, and to disciples who wanted revenge he had taught forgiveness. So, on the night when he was arrested, when at last the enraged Peter struck with the sword and cut off the ear of Malchus, servant of the high priest, he had cried out:

"Put up your sword again into its place: for all they that take the sword shall perish with the sword!"

From that moment began the swordless march of the Christiani. And the result had been the destruction of Jerusalem, the deaths of the apostles, the enthronement of Vespasianus, and the triumph of Titus.

Thus everything John had hoped for had collapsed without leaving a single thing behind. Nothing remained to him. There was no reason left to live in this world.

"I too will now go to your side. You know well that I have nothing further to see in this world."

At last John gave up on continuing to live and began fasting. His body grew gaunt and his consciousness dim, but his eyes burned like flame. He was like a lump of living fire.

When John's life flickered like a dying wick, once again in that disastrous darkness he met Jesus of Nazareth. What appeared in his memory was the shore at Tiberias.

They had put out upon the night sea and cast the net all night long, but had caught not one fish. In that boat were Peter, Thomas, Nathanael, and also James, John's elder brother. Upon that fruitless night sea only the wind blew cold, and they were all silent. They were men who had lost their dreams in a single morning. They had thought that when Jesus of Nazareth came to rule the world, at the very least they would obtain some office like aedile or magistrate, but now they had returned once more to being fishermen of Galilee.

At dawn, when the darkness began to lift like a net being torn open in the sky, John suddenly saw a man standing on the shore. And he was shouting toward the boat:

"Cast the net on the right side of the ship!"

The men cast the net just as he said. Astonishingly, the net was filled with so many fish that it was hard to haul it up. John cried to Peter,

"It is the Lord!"

Peter plunged into the water and began swimming toward the shore. When the rest brought the boat in and came up out of the water, Jesus of Nazareth was standing there. He had kindled a charcoal fire on the shore at dawn. Cold sweat began to run across Peter's brow. On that dawn too, in the court of the house of Caiaphas the high priest, when he denied his teacher three times, a charcoal fire had been burning. Jesus of Nazareth asked the trembling Peter:

"Lovest thou me?"

"Yes, I love you."

"Lovest thou me?"

"Lord, thou knowest that I love thee."

"Lovest thou me?"

Peter's face was growing pale. In a voice choked with tears he gave his third answer.

"Lord, do you not know? Thou knowest that I love thee."

Only then did Jesus speak to him. Jesus of Nazareth was speaking of how Peter would die with both arms outstretched. Good Peter, as though his own death mattered not at all, only clung to the Teacher.

"Feed my sheep."

"Lord, and what shall this man do?"

But Jesus looked with cold eyes toward John, who was standing behind Peter, and opened his mouth.

"If I will that he tarry till I come, what is that to thee?"

That cold gaze of Jesus of Nazareth pierced John's whole body with pain. Half in a daze, John lifted both hands and cried out:

"Till I come... till I come...."

At last John gave up on dying and crawled out of the hut. He yanked up grass roots and chewed them wildly. Tears again streamed down from his eyes.

From then on John's life began to change beyond recognition. By day he cultivated vegetables in the vegetable patch at Ortygia and took them into the city of Ephesus to sell, and with the money left over he went around seeking out the poor of Ephesus and helping them. By night he knelt in his hut or in the olive grove and prayed for long hours. He became noticeably sparing of words. Gradually throughout the city of Ephesus he became known as a good old grandfather. Whenever he went into town, children gathered around him, and he would seat them down, buy them food, or pick lice from their heads. Many Christiani fed the poor, helped the sick, and brought food into the prisons for those confined there. While he lived thus, the figure of Jesus of Nazareth, whom he had always found so mysterious, was gradually taking firm shape in his heart as certain love.

At last Vespasianus too, that capable statesman who had ruled the whole world, died, and Titus as well, who had laid Jerusalem waste so miserably, died. But John still had not died. At times he would walk alone through the bustling streets of Ephesus and murmur to himself:

(Till I come... till I come...)

And thus John was now entering Rome along the Appian Way by which Vespasianus had once marched in triumph. John was thinking of the countless crosses borne by Jesus of Nazareth and by his beloved friends. And now there remained only one final cross.

Though one could arrive in a single day by riding, their company took more than a week to walk the Appian Way. Concerned for old John's strength, Andronicus had deliberately slowed the pace of the march.

"Only a little farther now, and we shall be in Rome."

Riding ahead on horseback, Andronicus turned and spoke. John silently nodded. He was remembering Paul's letter, written by the man who had so long wanted to go to Rome.

"I am sure that when I come unto you, I shall come in the fullness of the blessing of Christ. Brethren, I beseech you for the Lord Jesus Christ's sake, and for the love of the Spirit, that ye strive together with me in your prayers to God for me...."

In the end Paul had been arrested in Jerusalem, crossed the Mediterranean with the escorting officer Julius, and walked this Appian Way.

The horse Drusiana was riding stopped, and she dismounted. Along the journey she had already done this several times, unable to bear the thought of the Apostle John walking. The boy Gaius came trotting after her and took hold of the edge of her garment.

"Apostle, must you truly go like this? Even now..."

"Drusiana, you must not hinder the road of glory given to this old man.... This is the road Peter walked, and Paul walked."

At that moment the boy Gaius tugged at Drusiana's garment and said,

"Over there... who are those people?"

They looked toward the place Gaius indicated. At the point where the Appian Way and the Ardeatina Way divided, three women and two men were standing and looking this way.

When Andronicus, who was riding ahead, halted his horse, all the others behind him also stopped walking. Looking down at the two young men standing with the three women, Andronicus started in surprise and said,

"Why... are you not the brothers Nereus and Achilleus, who once served in the cavalry?"

He dismounted, grasped the hands of the two old friends gladly, and they too clasped his hands with tears standing in their eyes.

"I had heard that you resigned from the cavalry and became chamberlains to His Excellency Consul Clemens...."

Then they pointed to one of the three women, the one wearing a veil, and introduced her to him.

"It is Lady Flavia Domitilla."

Startled, Andronicus stepped toward the woman and greeted her.

"It is an honor to meet you like this. I have heard recent news regarding the state of His Excellency the consul... and I am deeply sorry."

From within the veil, however, there came the quiet voice of a woman.

"Thank you. You must be weary from so long a journey. Would it not be well for you and your company to stop a little while at my villa and rest?"

Since Lady Domitilla, daughter of Titus and niece of Emperor Domitian, had come out this far to wait for them on the road, it meant there was surely some deep matter behind it. Andronicus gladly accepted her invitation.

"Thank you. As it happens, I had been wishing to rest somewhere before entering the city."

At once he directed his steward Lucius to turn toward the Ardeatina Way and went ahead with Domitilla's company. As they walked, Domitilla introduced the two women coming behind her, Leodora and Euphrosyna, and Andronicus called Drusiana forward, who had been walking with John, and told them she was the bride he had married in Ephesus.

Originally, outside the walls of Rome at the end of the Appian Way there stood many villas belonging to the nobles. Thus victorious generals returning from war were accustomed to station their troops outside the city while awaiting the emperor's command to enter, and in the meantime to rest as guests at the villas of the nobles. Therefore it was nothing strange that Andronicus's company, coming in by the Appian Way, should be invited to Domitilla's villa. The only unusual thing was that John too, who followed behind them bound, was being invited.

When they reached the beautiful consular villa, Domitilla again said to Andronicus,

"Quaestor, is that old man there John, the disciple of Jesus of Nazareth?"

"He is. And the two standing beside him are the Christiani Tychicus and Prochorus, who followed him here."

Lady Domitilla turned her head toward the women standing nearby.

"Leodora, and Euphrosyna, please guide the quaestor's servants and attendants to the outer house. I will take these people with me into the inner house."

Thus the couple Andronicus and Drusiana, the boy Gaius, and John's company were led into the inner quarters. When they arrived there, the brothers Nereus and Achilleus did not enter, but stopped outside the door. It seemed they meant to stand watch.

As soon as they entered the drawing room of the inner house, Andronicus loosened John's bonds, and Lady Domitilla removed her veil and offered seats to the guests.

"If one invites guests who have come from afar, it is proper first to guide them to the baths, but there is something I wish to discuss for a moment...."

Yet Andronicus nodded as though he understood, while gazing with admiring eyes at Lady Domitilla's graceful bearing. It seemed she had inherited in full the dignity of her grandmother Domitilla. In the Flavian house there had originally been three Domitillas: Domitilla the wife of Vespasianus, Domitilla the wife of Domitian, and now this Domitilla standing before Andronicus, daughter of Titus and wife of Consul Clemens. Andronicus spoke in a respectful tone.

"From the moment you were waiting at the crossroads, I already thought there must be some matter upon your heart. In truth... is it not rather to see this Lord John that you had business, more than with me?"

Indeed, Domitilla started at Andronicus's observation and drew one hand to her breast.

"So far as I know... His Excellency Consul Clemens was arrested under suspicion of being a Christianus. And are you not perhaps yourself connected in some way with that matter?"

Pressed sharply by the young quaestor, Domitilla bit her lip as though already resolved, thought a moment, and then answered in a firm voice,

"That is so, quaestor. I too am a Christiana."

Andronicus smiled and looked at her.

"You speak so dangerously and yet without fear, my lady. In truth, I too am on my way from Ephesus after receiving baptism from the Apostle John."

"What?"

"It is true. And my wife Drusiana here is my senior in the faith."

"Oh, God!"

So deeply moved was Domitilla that she joined her hands and offered a prayer of thanks, then knelt before white-haired John and said,

"This sinner Domitilla greets the apostle."

Only then did John take both her hands and raise her up.

"Rise, sister. Seeing your bold bearing now, this old man rather feels ashamed. May the grace of the Lord be with you."

Domitilla rose, sat down, and looked at John with a face wet with tears.

"My husband Clemens had been receiving treatment for illness from Luke, the physician who was here in Rome, and so came to hear the story of Jesus of Nazareth. As my husband began to show more and more curiosity about that story, Luke kept writing out and sending him the stories of Jesus, and while reading those letters, my husband at last became a Christianus. My husband received baptism from Clement, bishop of the church here in Rome. Afterward Luke left Rome, entrusting to my husband another scroll recording the acts of the apostles."

John nodded, picturing in his mind the physician Luke who had followed Paul's perilous road like a shadow.

"Then... that name Theophilus appearing at the beginning of the Acts of the Apostles was in fact Consul Clemens?"

"It was. Luke used a false name because he feared that if this record leaked out, my husband's person would be placed in danger. But... some time ago my husband must already have sensed danger to his life, for he sent the later scroll to the church of Ephesus. Yet he wished to keep the earlier record, the one concerning the acts of Jesus, to the very end, and in the end he was arrested like this. My husband asked that record be delivered to the Apostle John in Ephesus."

"Where... is Luke's writing now?"

"I will lead you there at once. The rest of you also, please come."

Domitilla rose and led the company into a side room. In that room stood a great table; when it was pushed aside and the carpet rolled back, there appeared a passage leading below ground. All followed Domitilla down into the tunnel. She lit a lamp hanging in one corner of the passage and led them onward.

"Along the Appian Way and the Ardeatina Way there are many underground tombs. Among them are several cemeteries in which martyrs of the Christiani are buried, and whenever persecution comes from the emperors they have been used as hiding places. This underground passage beneath our villa is not yet being used as a cemetery, but we intend to dedicate it to the church in future and let it be used as a church burial place."

When they had gone farther in, there appeared at the end of the passage a broad chamber, and there they could see three men standing like shadows. The one in the middle was a white-haired old man much like John. Another seemed to be about the same age as Tychicus, and the remaining one was middle-aged. Lady Domitilla stepped aside and said to the old man in the center,

"Bishop Clement, I have brought the Apostle John of Ephesus."

Bishop Clement had already recognized John as the oldest among the company. Stretching out both arms, his whole body trembling with joy, he cried,

"Oh..."

Having let out that one cry, Clement came almost stumbling forward before the apostle and knelt.

"I have lived long enough to behold the apostle...."

"I am glad to meet you. The faith of the Roman church and the labor of brother Clement are praised by the churches of the whole world. The courage of the saints who are carrying out works of relief at the risk of their lives is giving hope to all the Christiani."

It was truly a meeting of two old men accomplished in deep emotion. One was the last remaining disciple of Jesus of Nazareth; the other, the last remaining successor of Paul. One was the last standard-bearer guarding Jesus Christ; the other, the final commander holding the suffering church of Rome. John released the embrace and once more looked closely into Bishop Clement's aged face.

As he raised Clement to his feet, John embraced him again.

"Truly the loyalty of the Roman church is astonishing. You have preached the gospel even to Caesar's household...."

"It is all the power of God. Already from Paul's time the gospel had been preached not only to Caesar's house but to many noble families. Because Paul was concerned for their rank and danger, even when he wrote to the churches he never revealed their names at all."

"Recently I heard that the former consul Glabrio and Consul Clemens, the husband of Lady Domitilla here, were both arrested. Just how many of our brothers are there in this Rome?"

"Even I cannot guess, but among the poor and among the noble, everywhere brothers are hidden. I think that a good portion of Rome's citizens have already become citizens of the heavenly kingdom."

"Wonderful. This is solely the result of the Roman church devoting itself to good works at the risk of life. Even in Ephesus I heard that you relieve orphans and the poor, tend the sick, and carry food into the prisons despite the danger. It feels now as though Rome is becoming the capital of heaven. Bishop Clement, this poor apostle feels ashamed."

"You honor me beyond measure. I hardly know where to place myself."

Listening to their conversation, Quaestor Andronicus was being seized by a strange emotion. One of them was a man who had now been arrested and escorted away by special order of the Roman emperor, and the other was the very head of the Roman church, whom the authorities were desperately seeking. Both men had walked harsh thorny roads, knowing nothing of the pleasures of life. Yet these two men met in a dark tunnel under Rome, gave thanks to God for the emotion of that meeting, and were filled with joy. That was precisely what Andronicus found so difficult to understand.

Clement recognized old Tychicus standing behind John and exchanged glad greetings with him. The two had known each other already from thirty years before, when both had been among Paul's circle.

Tychicus briefly explained to Clement how he had been seized while on his way back to the Ephesian church and had served thirty years in the mines of Hispania, and Clement comforted him in his suffering and marveled at his faithfulness. Then Clement introduced to them the two men standing by his side.

"This brother here is Rufus, son of Simon of Cyrene... and this one is brother Evaristus, who is helping me."

At once John's eyes flashed, and he looked at the old man called Rufus.

"Simon of Cyrene..."

"That is right. The man who bore the cross in the Lord's place as he went up to Golgotha...."

"...I know."

Tears filled John's eyes. His heart again ached as if torn apart. When Jesus was seized through the betrayal of Judas his disciple, abandoned by all Jerusalem, examined by the religious leaders, judged by Gentiles, and then mocked on every side as he walked that road of suffering... where had John been when Jesus walked that path of utterly lonely sorrow?

That grief fixed in the bone had tormented and pained John throughout his long lifetime. John had remained on the scene until the moment when Jesus received his final judgment. But when Pilate's sentence fell, John judged that there was no more to be expected from Jesus. So he had been walking in the direction opposite from the Lord going to Golgotha. Then suddenly a strange thought came to him: perhaps Jesus would proclaim the independence of Judea at Golgotha and become king. John had run quickly toward the hill of Golgotha. By then Jesus was already hanging on the cross.

That blank interval between Pilate's court and Golgotha was John's own secret. But in the end that secret had been exposed by Simon of Cyrene, the man who had borne Jesus' cross in his place. And so when John saw Rufus, Simon's son, tears burst forth. Taking his hand, John said,

"I met your father and mother in Jerusalem. They were together among the one hundred twenty who prayed in Mark's upper room."

"At that time my elder brother and I were snot-nosed little children. Afterward our family went to Antioch in the persecution of Herod Agrippa, and there we met Paul."

Paul had lived with their family and had received the care of their mother. Then, when Paul set out on his third journey longing to go to Rome, the household of Simon had gone on ahead to Rome, and there they met Paul and Mark once more.

Clement spoke again.

"Lady Domitilla brought the apostle here in order to show him Luke's record. I heard that the church in Ephesus is now the place gathering the greatest number of records concerning Jesus."

"The young men are striving to guard Christ from the heretics. Already a copy of the letter you sent to the church at Corinth, Bishop Clement, has also been collected."

"My poor letter..."

Clement blushed with embarrassment and turned toward the middle-aged man.

"Evaristus, bring out Luke's Gospel."

He opened the door of a secret store set in the wall of the tunnel, entered, and soon came back untying the cord of a thick papyrus scroll as he spoke.

"It seems likely that Luke recorded this Gospel on the basis of materials he heard from Paul and the testimony of all the people of Jerusalem whom he met. Also, since one of the Lord's brothers, Judas, was here in Rome, testimony from that side too will have been consulted. The reason Luke specially sent these records to Consul Clemens, of course, was partly the need to preach to the consul himself; but it also seems he wished them to be safely preserved."

"A strange thing indeed. Paul's letter brought the conversion of Consul Glabrio, and now Luke's letter has brought salvation to Consul Clemens."

John lowered his eyes to the scroll that Clement had spread open before him.

"This scroll is a copy made by arranging Luke's letters together. At the beginning of these letters Luke uses the false name Theophilus so that the consul's identity will not be exposed."

John began reading through Luke's Gospel.

"...Most honorable Theophilus, many have taken in hand to set forth in order a declaration of those things which have been fulfilled among us, even as they delivered them unto us, who from the beginning were eyewitnesses and ministers of the word. It seemed good to me also, having traced all things accurately from the first, to write them in order unto thee...."

The beauty of Luke's Greek style was captivating John. As he read with complete concentration, Clement carefully said,

"There is one thing, apostle, I would like to ask of you... that you would examine whether this record is factual. If you approve it, we too in the Roman church will use this record as one of the rightful Scriptures."

Within Luke's letters John could see the development of Consul Clemens's faith. At the beginning of this Gospel, which seemed to have been written first, Luke addressed the recipient as "most excellent Theophilus," but in the later Acts of the Apostles he began simply, like one addressing a close brother, with "O Theophilus...."

Clement, following John's gaze down the scroll, spoke again when they reached a fitting place.

"Apostle, already from the beginning this record displays an important difference."

Without yet taking his eyes from the scroll, John answered,

"What difference is that?"

"For example... in the genealogy of Jesus' ancestors that appears here. In the genealogy recorded by Matthew, Joseph's line descends through Solomon, son of King David. But here it comes through Nathan, who was Solomon's brother, though still a son of David. What is more strange is..."

Clement closed his mouth for a moment and looked at John before continuing.

"What is more strange is that... even if one might know the distant forefathers wrongly, the name of the very father of Joseph the carpenter, that is, Joseph the husband of Mary, is different in the two records."

Only then did John lift his eyes from the papyrus and look up with a faint smile.

"Bishop Clement, that very point is what matters."

"?"

"Often when we read such records, we begin by reading with suspicious eyes, and so it is easy to misunderstand even a simple fact. Think on it. Luke never met Jesus directly, but he met all of the Lord's brothers. In Jerusalem he met James and Simon, and here in Rome he met Judas. Would such a Luke ever record Joseph's father's name incorrectly?"

"Then...?"

The question meant, of course, whether Matthew's record was then incorrect. Yet Matthew too had met the Lord's brothers. John, still smiling, began to explain.

"As Bishop Clement knows, our Jewish nation has a certain peculiar custom. If a man takes a wife and dies without leaving a child, one of his nearest kin takes the widow and begets a son in order to continue that line. Heli, a descendant of Nathan, died early without children, and therefore Jacob, from the descendants of Solomon who were his nearest family, lay with Heli's wife and begot Joseph. Then whose son is Joseph?"

"..."

"Needless to say, Joseph is Heli's son. Yet Matthew wrote the genealogy this way: Abraham begat Isaac... and so he came down and then wrote, Jacob begat Joseph, the husband of Mary. That is because Joseph was indeed begotten by Jacob. But what of Luke's record? He writes that Joseph was the son of Heli, Heli of Matthat, and so on, going back until Adam. Therefore, is not Matthew's record that Jacob begat Joseph accurate, and is not Luke's record that Joseph is the son of Heli equally true?"

Only then did Bishop Clement suddenly understand, and he nodded. John smiled again and resumed reading the record. The others did not dare even to cough, but held their breath and watched John's face. No one could tell how much time had passed. Reading on, nodding several times as he did so, John at last came to one passage where he wiped away tears. It was the very scene in which Jesus answered the Pharisees who asked when the kingdom of God should come.

"...The kingdom of God cometh not with observation: neither shall they say, Lo here! or, lo there! for, behold, the kingdom of God is within you."

At that passage John remained for a long time choked with tears, unable to read any further. He joined his hands, offered a silent prayer of meditation for a while, and only then began again to read other portions.

"Luke's record has given a great light to the path I now go to the end."

There could be no doubt that the voice left to him by Luke had become yet another stepping-stone of faith. He lifted his head, looked around, and then called to Tychicus, who was standing at the back.

"Brother Tychicus, you have labored much to come with me this far, but now I would ask one more thing of you."

"Only speak, apostle."

"How delighted the young brothers in Ephesus will be when they receive Luke's record. What if brother Tychicus himself were to carry them that joy?"

"I understand. I will leave for Ephesus at once with the Gospel."

"Excellent. May Gabriel the archangel guard you."

John had just been reading in Luke's record the beautiful story of the angel Gabriel, God's messenger, announcing to the Virgin Mary the conception of Christ.

Just as Tychicus was about to depart with the scroll wrapped up, there suddenly came the sound of a bell from somewhere in the tunnel. The light ringing came twice in succession.

"Since it rings twice, it does not seem to be danger. I will go and see."

While Lady Domitilla went outside, John and Clement each exchanged farewells with those gathered there. John first introduced his own companions to Bishop Clement.

"The Quaestor Andronicus here came to Ephesus to arrest me and there received baptism. And the lady beside him is Drusiana, the bride who was married this time...."

To Andronicus it seemed truly that the Christiani were strange people. Even now he had not the slightest intention of becoming a Christianus in earnest, and yet trusting only the one fact that he had received baptism, they were allowing him without hesitation to take part in all their secret gatherings.

"This young man is the grandson of Prochorus, one of the seven deacons of the Jerusalem church... and this child Gaius is the only surviving blood of the house of Gaius, martyred in Ephesus. While the whole family was being martyred, only the youngest son survived, and later his mother too, after giving birth to this boy, was seized and in the end also martyred."

As Clement took the boy Gaius by the hand and comforted him, Lady Domitilla returned. Her expression was composed, yet she spoke in a voice that trembled slightly.

"The chief chamberlain Stephanos of Empress Domitilla has come with a message from the empress."

Empress Domitilla was, of course, Domitilla's aunt.

"My husband Clemens was executed this morning, they say. And after that, an order for my arrest will also be issued."

A heavy silence flowed through the tunnel. Old Tychicus opened his mouth.

"My lady... I must leave for Ephesus now, but if you are willing, would it not be good for you to hide yourself a while by going with me?"

Lady Domitilla slowly shook her head and looked toward Bishop Clement.

"After I came to know Christ, I have lived not for my own life but for the kingdom of God. I have always lived asking what role it was that I, and I alone, had to fulfill in that kingdom. Until now I lived as the wife of the consul, the rank next below the emperor in this country, in order to help and shelter the church. Now that role is ended. Of course there are still many things I can do for the church. Yet at this hour, the noble role that cannot be done unless by me, the role that I alone can fulfill... is to declare before all people that the wife of Consul Clemens was a daughter who remained fully faithful to the Lord, and by doing so to give courage to the brothers who are suffering. God will surely desire that of me...."

It was a truly resolute judgment. John murmured like one groaning:

"The kingdom of God is within you.... Hallelujah!"

Everyone gathered there followed John's lead and offered prayers of thanksgiving. Then Domitilla spoke again in a bright voice.

"At any rate, apostle, today is the day I must receive you. More than anyone else, you have walked the Appian Way as the most radiant of victorious generals, and a victorious general entering Rome must be entertained at a nobleman's villa. Today this house of Clemens has at last received its most glorious guest."

The gallant bearing of Lady Domitilla moved them all deeply. All attended the dinner prepared by Domitilla and her household, and at this dinner John told them the story of Jesus' last supper.

"...It was indeed an astonishing thing. Jesus rose from the table, laid aside his outer garment, girded himself with a towel, filled a basin with water, and began to wash the disciples' feet...."

Gasps and deep feeling spread throughout those present. John himself, who was standing before them now, had had his feet washed by that same Jesus.

"All the disciples were at a loss. Peter above all drew back, saying such a thing must never be. Then Jesus said to him: 'If I wash thee not, thou hast no part with me.' In the end, Peter, quite unlike his usual self, was deeply ashamed and could do nothing but stretch out his feet before the Lord."

It was a truly beautiful story. They had survived countless crises and still had many perilous roads before them, yet it seemed no threat could draw them away from such beautiful emotion.

While their supper was full of memories of Jesus, the centurion Tullius arrived at the villa of Consul Clemens leading soldiers of the emperor's guard. He had come to arrest Lady Flavia Domitilla and the chamberlain-brothers Nereus and Achilleus.

Andronicus first hid Bishop Clement, Rufus, and Evaristus in the adjoining room, then went out to meet Centurion Tullius, who was entering to execute the warrants.

"Centurion Tullius, is His Majesty the Emperor in good health?"

"Ah, Quaestor Andronicus. We meet in an unexpected place. Did the matter for which you went to Ephesus go well?"

"Have you ever seen me fail in what I undertake? Ah... I should introduce my wife to you."

He had Drusiana step forward and introduced her to the centurion.

"This is my wife Drusiana. It had been so long since I went home that I found myself wanting to marry."

Tullius looked at Drusiana's beautiful face and clear eyes and said with an expression almost dazed,

"The quaestor's eye is indeed remarkable. I think I understand now why the maidens of Rome did not satisfy you."

"Ah... I..."

"But, Tullius... have you come to welcome Quaestor Andronicus, or is there some other business?"

Tullius stammered when he noticed Lady Domitilla and the brothers Nereus and Achilleus seated among the company.

"In truth... by the emperor's command I have come to arrest Lady Domitilla and the brothers Nereus and Achilleus."

"Hm... do you mean to bind this beautiful lady?"

Andronicus placed a hand on Tullius's shoulder and said,

"Tullius, to bind so beautiful a lady would not be gentlemanly by Roman standards, nor would it be proper respect toward His Excellency Consul Clemens. And as for the brothers Nereus and Achilleus here, they are my comrades from cavalry days. I will personally escort these people with every courtesy as far as the Mamertine Prison, so you need only follow behind and keep watch well."

Centurion Tullius, unable to do otherwise before the quaestor's words, nodded. Domitilla said farewell to Leodora and Euphrosyna and entrusted the later matters to them, while John and Prochorus exchanged farewells with Tychicus, who was leaving for Ephesus.

At last John's entrance into Rome began. Andronicus on horseback and his chief steward Lucius went in front. Behind them walked white-haired John holding the hand of the boy Gaius, together with Drusiana and Lady Domitilla. Their steps were solemn and peaceful, like pilgrims going on pilgrimage to a holy place. Behind them came Prochorus, Nereus, and Achilleus, three young men, walking firmly; after them came Hesthenes, the rest of Andronicus's attendants, and the escorting soldiers; and at the very end came Centurion Tullius leading the emperor's guard.

In that final procession Prochorus made one more plea that John change his mind and escape, but again it was not accepted. Prochorus could feel that matters had already passed beyond recall.

At the crossroads where the Ardeatina Way met the Appian Way again, Domitilla said to John,

"This is the very place where the Apostle Peter, as he was leaving Rome, met the Lord."

When Nero's persecution had broken out because of the great fire of Rome, Peter was leaving the city to save his life. Then Peter saw a streak of light going toward Rome. Astonished, Peter asked into the air:

"Domine, quo vadis?"

Then into Peter's ears came the voice of Jesus: because Peter was fleeing, he was going into Rome again to be crucified once more. And at that very place Peter's steps turned completely around.

John was now walking along that road. The image of Peter, always a little rash but ever bluntly faithful, kept rising before him, and John again wiped the corners of his eyes with the back of his hand. Peter had originally been a fisherman who worked on the boat of John's father Zebedee and received his share of the catch.

At last John's company passed through the Appian Gate, the southern gate of Rome. The soldiers guarding the gate recognized Marius Andronicus, the rising young star of Rome, and rendered military honors. With the Aventine Hill and the Tiber River to their left, they walked straight toward the Palatine Hill. Before their eyes there now appeared the enormous chariot-racing ground, the very place where Andronicus had won victory in chariot racing. Domitilla spoke again.

"That is the Circus Maximus, where countless brothers were burned to death and devoured by beasts."

"Was Peter too martyred there?"

"The place where Peter was martyred was the amphitheater on the Vatican Hill, beyond the Tiber."

After walking along a street lined with tall buildings and passing beneath the arches of an elevated aqueduct, there appeared a gigantic cylindrical building. Around its three tiers of arches stood columns: those of the first story were the sturdy Doric order, those of the second the graceful Ionic, and those of the third the splendid Corinthian. Bearing within itself at once the morning, noon, and sunset of the Mediterranean, this vast structure was the Colosseum, where bloody battles of gladiators were fought every other day. Beside the Colosseum stood a golden colossal image nearly of the same height. Originally Nero's statue had stood there, but when Vespasianus ascended he had removed it and erected in its place a statue of Apollo, whom he made the guardian god of the Flavian house.

John frankly marveled at the gigantic structures of Rome and their magnificent scale. He himself, and John of Gischala who had died in Jerusalem, had sought to fight one great battle against this enormous Rome.

Yet now in this city of power it was instead the shabby carpenter of Nazareth who had begun to open his mouth. He had appeared in this city of darkness carrying a very small lamp. The Colosseum and the statue of Apollo, which had seemed distant, now became more overwhelming the nearer they came. It was along this vast street that Peter and Paul had walked into Rome. The company turned left before the statue of Apollo. There stood a beautiful triumphal arch. Domitilla explained:

"It is the arch dedicated to General Titus after his triumph over Jerusalem."

Again John remembered mercilessly shattered Jerusalem and the homeland that had vanished, and he ground his teeth. Jesus of Nazareth had taken everything from him. Love, hatred, friends, enemies, and finally even his country had all vanished away. Jesus of Nazareth had taken everything from John utterly. Thus John was now an empty man. Bare and empty, he was now standing face to face with Jesus of Nazareth. He had emptied John so completely and yet had not even granted him death. At that time in Antioch when he turned away unable to pierce Vespasianus's throat, John had cried out to Jesus of Nazareth, begging him to take even his life. But that had not been permitted. On the cross he himself had cried only that he thirsted.

Andronicus and the mounted soldiers all dismounted, for they were entering the sacred precinct where the shrines of the successive emperors stood. Passing the Arch of Titus and the Temple of Vesta and the shrine of Julius Caesar, there opened before their eyes the splendid Roman Forum. On the Palatine Hill to the left they could see the temple of Augustus, and before it stood the temple of Castor. On one side was the Senate house, and on the Capitoline Hill opposite stood the great temple of Jupiter rebuilt by Vespasianus. And just below that temple of Jupiter stood another temple facing John as though mocking him.

"What building is that, Sister Domitilla?"

Then Andronicus, walking ahead, turned and answered,

"It is the temple of Emperor Vespasian himself. Since Augustus he was Rome's greatest ruler and patriot. While alive he received from the Senate the title Pater Patriae, that is, Father of the Country, and after his death he was raised to the gods and that temple was dedicated to him."

John stared silently at the marble statue of Vespasianus standing before the temple. Again he heard Andronicus's voice in his ears.

"Josephus, the historian from Judea, is said to have confessed that Emperor Vespasian was the very Messiah the Jews had been waiting for."

John drew one deep breath and then slowly began to move his steps again. At length they wound once more among the buildings and came before a gloomy structure set on the western slope of the Capitoline Hill. It was a place that seemed hardly a building at all, but rather a cave.

"This is the Mamertine Prison."

At last Drusiana burst into tears, and the boy Gaius clutched the apostle's hand tight, while tears also stood in Prochorus's eyes. Lady Domitilla said to John as though comforting him:

"It is said that thirty years ago both the Apostle Peter and Paul were imprisoned in this prison."

After taking each person's hand in turn, including that of Andronicus, and blessing them all, John turned to the approaching Tullius and said,

"Come then, centurion. It seems the time has come for you and me to become acquainted."

# Empire and Heaven (I)

## Chapter 6. Lost Coordinates

Along the ridge of Mount Coressus, which embraced the city of Ephesus from the south, a stone wall spread out like a folding screen and ran all the way to the watchtower on the hill of Astyages overlooking the harbor. It was the wall built by Lysimachus, who ruled Ephesus after Alexander. The original Ephesus had stood on the slopes of Mount Coressus, but after forcing the inhabitants to move to the region near Mount Pion, Lysimachus built this wall. It is said that he constructed the new city so that he might give it the name of his wife Arsinoe. At any rate, traces of the old city still remained along the mountainside, with its ruins, heaps of stone, and caves.

Two men rode their horses along a narrow path thick with weeds. Lifting their hands now and then to push aside branches that blocked the way, they made their way up a mountain road where no people passed.

"Hesthenes, how did you learn that their secret meeting place was in such a place?"

"Be careful of the branches, dominus."

"You only returned to this Ephesus last night...."

"Since my lord is the rising star of Rome, should not this Hesthenes become just as capable a servant?"

"Hesthenes, understand this well. An informant who is too capable leaves his own life dripping behind him...."

"But if I am to protect my lord from the emperor's delatores, the servant too cannot help but be capable."

"But... will that old man named John still be there?"

Andronicus said this while looking at the sun standing high overhead.

"He will still be there. Their eucharisteo, that is, their communion service, ends at dawn, but today there will be a somewhat special gathering."

"A special gathering?"

"...There, I have misspoken again."

He still had not revealed that he had followed old Tychicus the night before.

"What do you mean?"

"At any rate, today what my lord must do is meet that old gentleman named John and ask him to officiate at the wedding. Beyond that, you must not become greedy."

"I know that well."

"For example... even if there are many others there besides John, you must not decide to arrest them all and win merit for yourself."

"You are forever talking as if you know nothing at all of Roman gentlemanly conduct."

"...."

"And besides?"

"On a day as fine as this, my lord would surely wish to see Miss Drusiana happy."

"Of course."

"If you are to make Miss Drusiana glad and happy..."

"What are you trying to say?"

"If you are to make Miss Drusiana happy, many guests must be invited."

"Naturally."

"What sort of guests will you invite? Nobles of Ephesus? Soldiers? Scholars?"

Only then did Andronicus realize what his servant was trying to say.

"You are talking about the Christiani."

"Yes. If you are to make Miss Drusiana happy, many Christiani must be invited to the wedding. But if they are invited to the wedding of Marius Andronicus, the capable official and quaestor of mighty Rome, how many of them will come? Who would do something as foolish as a fish crawling into a net?"

"That is true...."

It was only too obvious. The great persecution had already begun in Rome, and every day hundreds were dying as sacrifices in the arena. Ephesus was no exception. Though the governor's soldiers were not hunting them down everywhere, the governor too had to keep arresting Christiani if he wished to maintain the emperor's favor, and he could hardly avoid reporting how many of them he had executed. True, the Christiani were strange people who counted it an honor to be caught and killed in persecution; but none of them would willingly walk into a wedding feast to receive the cord with their own feet. Andronicus frowned at the sun standing over his head.

"What should be done?"

"That is simple."

"Simple?"

"First, my lord must abandon any desire to arrest them all and win merit."

"I have already said as much."

"And the second thing is this: meet John, invite all his companions, and promise them safety."

"Will that work?"

"The Jews, and especially the Christiani, are people who regard a promise as life itself. If my lord gives his word, they will believe it."

"But even if I guarantee it, their identity will be revealed, and once I am gone they will be in danger again, will they not?"

"There is another way."

"Another way?"

"Yes. There is a way to do it neatly. Hold the wedding twice. That is, first celebrate it at the school of Tyrannus with Governor Lycomedes, the nobles, and the scholars invited; then let the bride and bridegroom go to your family house above the great theater."

Ordinarily Roman weddings were held in the house of the bride. Guests were invited there, the wedding was celebrated, a banquet was served, and then the bride and bridegroom went to the groom's ancestral house. All the guests followed them there, blessed them, and only then departed.

So what Hesthenes was proposing was to finish the wedding ceremony early and then return to the groom's house and hold a second wedding feast there. The Christian guests would gather at the groom's house and could thus be fully separated from the other guests.

"I have never heard of holding a wedding twice...."

"Please do not say such things. If it were for marrying Miss Drusiana, it seems you would do it a hundred times."

"Everything is already hurried enough with this sudden marriage. Must we really do it twice?"

"Do not worry. The meticulous steward Lucius has been preparing for a month already, and when we left the house this morning I had already told them beforehand that there would be feasts in both places."

Indeed, Hesthenes was a servant who outmatched his master. Andronicus looked sidelong at him and said,

"It has become a more complicated marriage than I expected."

"There is no need to feel too burdened. The harder a woman is to win, the more substance there is to her. And once she is safely in your arms, my lord will be able to recover the dignity of a husband again...."

There was a mischievous jeer mixed into Hesthenes's words. To say he would recover his dignity implied that at present he had not done so. But it was no time to quarrel over words with Hesthenes, so Andronicus simply turned the subject.

"But what did you mean by saying that today there is a special gathering of the Christiani?"

"Because my lord pledged his Roman gentleman's honor, I will let you in on it...."

"Important figures in their movement have gathered in Ephesus from many places."

"For what reason? Are they plotting rebellion?"

"No. Trouble has arisen among themselves."

"Trouble?"

"Yes."

"What kind of trouble?"

"The doctrine that gives them strength is the resurrection of the man called Jesus. They say that after he rose, he remained in this world for forty days and then ascended. But he promised that he would come again before this generation passed away."

"And still he has not come...."

"That is right. So divisions have begun to arise among them."

"Divisions...?"

"Almost all who met Jesus directly are dead. And now the later generations, who did not meet him with their own eyes, have each begun to interpret his meaning in their own way. So at last the meaning of the savior they had worshiped has become distorted, and from within them the danger of collapse has arisen."

"Then...."

Andronicus pushed aside a branch touching his forehead and sank into thought. If what Hesthenes said was true, then Domitian had struck the heart of the matter when he ordered John to be brought in. At the moment when faith in Jesus was beginning to distort, the only center able to keep them from scattering and hold them together would be John, the last surviving disciple. If so, Domitian's order to seize John was nothing less than a fundamental measure to uproot them.

"I still do not know what crimes they have committed... but there is certainly something excessive in their faith. How can they take a young man who worked as a carpenter and exalt him as a god?"

"In that, Rome is no different."

"What do you mean?"

"To carve an image from stone and serve it as a god, or to serve a man as a god...."

"But Hesthenes, even if the image is carved from stone, within it are represented the pride and ideal of the peoples of the Roman Empire. What is a god? Is it not an ideal and a claim which gathers the hearts of the people and is served by them? But what became of that carpenter from Judea? How can a mere carpenter become a god? What ideal or goal is there in that?"

"That is precisely the mysterious power of the man named Jesus. No great man of Rome or Hellas was ever loved like that."

"In the end, perhaps they fall so easily into his way because it is the philosophy of the weak. The Jews, endlessly afflicted... and among them a pitiful man who had been a carpenter and was executed without cause.... Perhaps the poor and the powerless comfort themselves by thinking of miserable Jesus. That is, perhaps their way is not the religion of the strong but of the weak... not the religion of victory but of defeat. It may even be a kind of self-tormenting spiritual sickness.... If so, then not only is it unfit to be the religion of mighty Rome, it could become a harmful poison. Perhaps that is why Emperor Domitian persecutes them...."

Hesthenes looked about him, as though they were nearly upon the place. Along the mountainside several cave mouths could be seen.

"Is it near here?"

"I came when it was dark... wait a moment, and I will go confirm it."

He dismounted, looked around briefly, and then ran quickly toward the caves. There were six cave mouths of various sizes opening blackly in the air. Approaching the second entrance from the right, Hesthenes turned back and signaled. He raised his right hand, drew it downward, then made a gesture pulling toward himself. It meant that Andronicus should dismount and come to him. Having accompanied one another even on the battlefields of Gaul, they had grown skilled at reading one another's mind and signals.

When Andronicus came up to the front of the cave, Hesthenes said in a low voice,

"This is it. If you go inside, the Christiani will be holding an important meeting. Their worship will have ended long ago, so the ordinary believers will have gone home, and only the important persons will remain."

"Then what do we do now?"

"We cannot wait forever, so we must call the old man out. Stay here for a moment. I will go in and come back out."

"How is the cave inside?"

"It is used as a burial place by the people of Ephesus. Strangely, the Christiani have a habit of gathering in cemeteries. I have even heard in Rome that they meet among tombs. So stay here and do not move."

Hesthenes disappeared into the darkness of the cave. Left alone outside, Andronicus folded his arms and fell into thought. As Hesthenes had said, his marriage to Drusiana was an adventure. In a world where even innocent people were accused of being Christiani and arrested, to marry a Christiana, and to hold the wedding according to their rite, was exceedingly dangerous.

Moreover, Andronicus was a man already attracting much attention. The moment he came under the emperor's suspicion, the delatores would swarm like hornets and tear apart every weakness he possessed. He looked down upon the city of Ephesus shining beneath the sun. He could see Mount Pion rising softly like a mother's breast, the Cayster River flowing down behind it, and the harbor of Ephesus spreading its arms toward the glittering Mediterranean.

(Would it not have been better to spend a peaceful life here in my hometown with Drusiana rather than to pursue the savage ladder of advancement in Rome?)

Six years before, when he left Ephesus, Andronicus had despised such softness above all things. But the desire and tension of Rome had wearied him beyond measure.

(Am I now being lured through Drusiana into the superstitious world of the Christiani?)

From the first they were people who did not pursue the desires of this world. They chose the voice of Nazareth instead of the emperor of Rome; the tomb within the cave rather than the marble mansion; the path of wretched sacrifice rather than a rich and comfortable life. Yet they were not defeated. He remembered the Christiani dying calmly in the arena. So many were dying, and yet their line continued stubbornly on. They risked themselves to take in orphans, treat the sick, and bring food to prisoners in jail.

Suddenly Andronicus felt a cold murderous presence on the back of his neck and instinctively bent his body. It was the self-protective instinct learned on countless battlefields. Something passed by his neck with a sharp rush of wind, struck the rock face, and fell to the ground. It glittered in the sunlight. He went over, picked it up, and saw that it was a dagger with a blue-shining edge.

Holding the dagger, Andronicus swiftly looked around. Suddenly the desolate hill of ruins seemed filled with taut murderous force. Nothing moved in the silence gathering among the weeds and trees. The enemy was aiming at his life from somewhere unseen. Compared with that, Andronicus himself stood fully exposed and defenseless.

He flung himself at once into the cave Hesthenes had entered. Catching his breath, he watched again for movement outside. There was no sign of anything.

(Who was it that aimed at me?)

He began to recall, one by one, the faces of the Roman nobles who might count as his enemies. Because his rise had been so swift, there were too many who envied him. He felt all those glances passing through his mind and trembled for a moment alone. Among them, of course, was even the gaze of Emperor Domitian.

(Has the murderous breath of Rome followed me even to Ephesus...?)

As his tension eased somewhat, the damp smell of earth began to seep into his nose. He slowly turned his eyes and examined his surroundings. The wall of the cave was made of a somewhat crumbly tufa, easy enough to carve into an artificial cavern. He looked outside once more, then began taking slow steps inward. At first he blinked and groped in the darkness, but little by little the walls around him began to emerge faintly. Somewhere, a dim light was seeping into the cave. Feeling the wall with one hand, he kept walking deeper inside.

Somewhere a soft song could be heard. It would be heard and then cease, then begin again, and its melody was bathing his heart in a tone unlike anything he had ever heard in this world.

Now and then the passage bent or branched.

(?)

The earthen wall along which his hand had been moving came to an end. Startled, he swept his hand sideways and touched again something cold like a slab of stone. If, as Hesthenes had said, this cave was a cemetery, then it must have been a stone coffin. His hair stood on end, and he quickened his steps a little. Indeed, coffins were set into the walls here and there.

How far had he gone? Suddenly it seemed to him that the space before his eyes was growing brighter, and he walked toward it. Gradually the interior of the passage appeared in a dim haze, and at last he found the source of the light. It came from a part of the passage where one side widened slightly. On the right side of the wall was a recess hollowed in like a room, and along its three walls lay six coffins in all, two to a side. Before the coffin straight ahead stood a small stone table, and an olive-oil lamp rested upon it. It seemed to be the tomb of a single family. Andronicus picked up the lamp and examined the coffin in front of him.

Greek letters were carved into it.

In the eleventh year of Emperor Nero, the Macedonian Gaius and his wife, their three sons, and the slave Scopas fell asleep here under persecution from the governor of Ephesus.

The eleventh year of Nero was the year just before the great fire in Rome. Gaius... The name sounded strangely familiar to him. He looked at the images carved beside the letters. There was a scene of men bound to crosses and burned in flame. Apparently this household of Gaius had been burned alive in the persecution of the Christiani. Andronicus frowned, as though he could still smell burning flesh in the tomb. While in Rome he too had more than once seen such human roastings in the amphitheater. On each coffin were carved not only such scenes of burning but also doves and fish.

(This is....)

There was a picture of a radiant-faced man seated in the middle while others sat around him at a meal. It seemed to be the last supper that Jesus the carpenter of Nazareth was said to have shared with his disciples before his arrest. Beside it was carved the Greek phrase agapate allelous. Love one another.

Andronicus ground his teeth, feeling pity for the vain dreams of these Christiani. For what were they suffering so? If the God they believed in truly existed, why had He sent no hand of rescue when this family of six was roasting like pigs in flame? No, setting these six aside, why had He allowed that carpenter from Nazareth, called the Son of God, to expire on the cross? 

Then suddenly he realized that the tragedy concerned not only those lying in the coffins but had come very near to him. Drusiana, the woman he was to marry that day, also believed in such a God.

The moment he thought of Drusiana, his mind cleared. He was a man who was supposed to marry that very day. It was not his place to be idly visiting ghosts in coffins. He picked up the lamp again to make his way back outside. Suddenly a terrible thought struck him. It had not occurred to him while he walked in the dark, but now that the light revealed the cave passages, he no longer felt sure that he would be able to retrace the way he had come.

(If I had known it would come to this, I should have remembered the path as I entered....)

But regret came too late. He could remember the first few turns, but as he went farther inward he had become so intent on observing his surroundings that he had failed to prepare himself for getting back out. Before long that failure became reality. The passage split into two. He had no confidence at all in which way he had entered. Had he kept to the wall the entire time, he might have remembered, but he had not. Closing his eyes for a moment, he searched his memory and then took the left-hand passage. After making such choices several more times, Andronicus finally realized that he was completely trapped in a labyrinth.

He could go no farther. The more he went on, the more deeply he seemed to be descending into the interior. The mysterious melody he had heard when first entering the cave had now ceased. No goal and no direction remained to him. He looked at the lamp in his hand. Judging by the weight, there could not be much oil left.

(Where is Hesthenes now, and what in the world is he doing?)

But by now he could no longer place any hope in Hesthenes. It was entirely possible that he had turned into a completely different branch of the cavern. The dagger in his other hand could no longer protect him from the darkness. He threw it down upon the cave floor. Strength left his legs, and he simply sat down on the damp earth. Anxiety began to rise in him once more.

(Ah, Drusiana....)

Never in all his life had Andronicus felt himself so helpless. Marius Andronicus, hero of the battlefields of Gaul and rising quaestor of the Roman Empire, was trapped in this absurd cave in a state of utter helplessness.

Staring at the flickering lamp, he thought of the story of the labyrinth of Minos. Minos, ruler of Crete, had built the labyrinth to imprison the monster begotten by his wife and the bull, and Ariadne, daughter of Minos, having fallen in love with Theseus, prince of Athens, showed him how to escape when he entered to explore it. She gave him a great ball of thread and told him to follow it back out afterward.

Andronicus even considered taking off his tunic and unraveling it into threads. But it did not seem likely that he could make enough thread for the length of the cave, and even if he began from where he was now, at most he would only return again to the same spot.

How much time passed? Andronicus's heart was being consumed along with the wick of the lamp. Now even its light was weakening more and more, as though the olive oil were giving out. Confusion began to come over the clear mind of the young hero, the rising star Andronicus.

(Perhaps I must remain here until someone appears who knows the way through the cave. But when will that be...? It would be when someone dies and is brought here to be laid away, or when the family comes to offer prayers of remembrance. When would that be? A month from now? Three months?)

There was one other possibility. Hesthenes had said that the Christiani gathered in such places for secret meetings. But it seemed that the place of their assembly was elsewhere than where Andronicus now sat, for he had not encountered Hesthenes, who had entered the cave before him.

At this point, the wedding no longer mattered. It was altogether possible that Andronicus's life would end in this cave. There was nothing in the darkness for him to eat. He could hardly open the coffins and gnaw the dead.

Then he began to feel hunger. He could no longer tell how much time had passed. Rank and dignity no longer mattered. He shouted into the void without restraint.

"Is anyone there?"

The voice of a single man split and traveled through the dark cave like the voices of many.

"Help! I have lost my way!"

But there was no answer. It felt to him as though he were rolling down again into a cold darkness, and he seized his head with both hands. The lamp went out at last. Like a madman he cried out over and over, "Is anyone there?" He kept shouting until his throat grew hoarse. His body's strength ebbed away even further, and at last, exhausted, he leaned against the earthen wall.

(Marius Andronicus... is this truly how it ends?)

There was no one at all who could help him. Neither his mother, nor Apollonius, nor Governor Lycomedes, nor even the emperor himself had any concern with him now. Even Drusiana, whom he had longed for without cease, was now a woman who could do nothing for him.

(Is there truly no one... no one at all to help me?)

He thought of the many gods served by the Roman Empire. Vesta, goddess of fire. Athena, goddess of wisdom. Artemis, goddess of abundance who ruled Ephesus. Where now was their fame? Everywhere he had gone he had paid them respect and offered gifts upon their altars, yet now they too were in another country.

The world in which Andronicus had thought, lived, and acted was vanishing all at once. Everything had returned completely to a blank page. Nothing remained to him now except the dark cave in which Andronicus, hungry and powerless, had collapsed in a damp and chill space. Suddenly he discovered the piercing loneliness at the bottom of himself and shuddered in terror. Until now he had been a hero in the busiest and most active crowds. He had been among many men, in many organizations. But now he saw that he had been the loneliest and most pitiful of creatures.

Then he remembered the Christiani suffering in desperate situations. In the center of the broad arena, among flames or among wild beasts, there had been no one who could help them. Yet they had comforted one another and died one by one. He had often seen some among them even singing aloud.

(At the moment of dying, were they never lonely?)

He thought of the carpenter of Nazareth whom the Christiani served with such desperate devotion. They said He had come to give His flesh and blood to men. And He had been executed by Pontius Pilate, governor of Judea. Now Andronicus trembled before death, while this man was said to have died for others.

Suddenly Andronicus cried out into the empty air.

"Jesus, carpenter of Nazareth, did You also give flesh and blood to me?"

Again his hoarse voice split and flew toward the black void.

"If You truly rose again, then come to me. I am lonely now!"

Then Andronicus gave a little laugh, thinking how pitiful he himself looked, crying out in that way.

(It is all the same. Men find life in this world too lonely to endure unless they lean upon something. Vesta, Athena, Artemis... and that Jesus of Nazareth as well... all of them are such things....)

Just then it seemed to him that he heard a thin sound from very far away. It was like the sound of wind, or perhaps like a wall giving way. Because he had been so long in silence, even the slightest noise struck him like a clamor.

(?)

It was coming closer. He thought that because he had been subjected to such extremity for so long, he must be beginning to hear things. But the sound kept growing. It seemed as though several people were walking this way. And within those noises he heard one voice distinctly.

Marius!

Andronicus doubted his own ears. The voice was plainly calling his name. He was so startled that he struggled to his feet, reeled, and collapsed again. Having tried to rise after so long, he had been struck with dizziness. The voice continued.

Marius!

It seemed perhaps like his mother's voice, perhaps like the voice of Drusiana. Or perhaps it was the answer of Jesus of Nazareth, whom he had mocked. Lying where he had fallen, he did not move. His memories were scattering away, and like a fossil he seemed to be hardening slowly.

As he felt himself falling into an endless abyss, he thought of the words that would be carved upon his coffin.

Marius Andronicus, quaestor, in the fifteenth year of Emperor Domitian, in the month of April, wandered lost in the cemetery of the Christiani and fell asleep peacefully.

He held a faint smile at the corner of his mouth and let his face fall to the ground. Suddenly his whole body was growing very comfortable. The damp earth felt as warm as wool, and all his limbs grew languid. From the darkness, the soft wings of death floated downward like drifting down feathers. And with those feathers, Andronicus too was falling endlessly down into the darkness.

That warm and gentle darkness continued for a long while. Then, in the silence where he could not move at all, Andronicus became aware that someone was gently shaking his body.

(?)

Though his eyes were still closed, he could somehow sense brightness. Slowly he opened his heavy eyelids.

First the light entered his eyes. Andronicus frowned again in the middle of that brightness and slowly gathered his focus. Something was before him. As light tangled with light, an image gradually began to form before his eyes. It was the figure of an old man. His hair and beard were like snow, but his gaze shone like flame. He opened his mouth.

"Are you somewhat restored to yourself?"

Then from behind the old man's back a woman sprang forward.

"Marius, it is I. How you have suffered!"

It was Drusiana. At last Andronicus understood what had happened. Behind Drusiana stood Hesthenes, and beyond him he could see his mother, Drusiana's parents, and many other people. When Hesthenes came out of the cave with the Apostle John, he must have realized that his master was missing and looked for him everywhere. At last he must have understood that perhaps he had entered the cave and lost his way there.

As he raised his upper body, he seized Drusiana, who rushed toward him, and embraced her fiercely. The thought that he had lived restored life to him again.

(Foolish Hesthenes... to come so late....)

With the support of Drusiana and Hesthenes he rose to his feet. He still looked at the old man standing before him. Drusiana whispered into his ear,

"It is the Apostle John."

Before Andronicus could greet him, John spoke first.

"It is a blessing that we found you. Many people were worried...."

"I am ashamed. I have caused concern for nothing."

"Come now. Let us go outside."

Andronicus began to walk. It felt as though he were beginning a new life. He turned back once more and looked over the cave in which he had been trapped.

He remembered also how he had argued in the darkness with the unseen Jesus of Nazareth. He said to Hesthenes,

"Hesthenes, how much time passed after I was separated from you?"

"It must seem to you that about ten years have passed."

Hesthenes, who always knew how to mock without regard for another's state of mind, had begun his teasing again.

"It seems to have been about ten days."

"There now, I knew you would say that. But in fact only ten hours have passed. We parted before the cave this morning, and now the day is beginning to grow dark. You will not scold me now for coming too late, will you?"

Then not even a full day had passed. He turned to Drusiana.

"Drusiana... if nothing had happened, this is the hour when we should now be celebrating our wedding. Could the eucharisteo you spoke of be held here in this cave?"

In the lamplight Drusiana's eyes were shining.

"It can. Our secret meeting place is inside."

Andronicus called to the white-haired old man walking ahead.

"Sir... Apostle."

"...?"

The old man stopped walking and turned around.

"I have heard that the Christiani practice a rite called eucharisteo. Could you perform that rite at this hour?"

The old man stared at Andronicus's face, not understanding what he meant.

"In truth, I came here this morning because there was something I wished to ask of you. While my servant had gone in to see you, I entered out of curiosity, lost my way, and met with this disaster. Perhaps you have already heard it from my servant, but this evening I was to be married to Miss Drusiana of the house of Tyrannus, who is here. But Drusiana, who was to be my bride, wished very much to be married according to the rite of the Christiani, and so I had meant to ask you to officiate the ceremony."

The old man nodded and asked the young man beside him,

"Polycarp, is there still any bread left for the communion?"

"Yes. There should still be some left from this morning."

The old man looked again at Andronicus.

"Andronicus... can you receive as Savior the Jesus of Nazareth whom we believe in?"

John's gaze as he asked it was so intense that Andronicus could not help but falter.

"I... in truth, I do not know much about this Jesus of Nazareth.... Only, I love Drusiana...."

Suddenly he remembered crying out to Jesus of Nazareth in the darkness, asking whether He too had given flesh and blood even to him.

"I do not know very well... but I will seek to understand."

John turned again to Drusiana.

"Drusiana... if you marry this man, can you pray for your husband and lead him to the way of salvation?"

"Yes, Apostle. I will do so with all my strength."

Then John looked at Andronicus's mother standing behind him.

"Do you also consent that your son should become a Christianus?"

Andronicus turned to his mother. He had not yet spoken with her about this matter. Fearing that the wrong answer might come from her lips, he spoke first.

"Mother, forgive me for not speaking of it to you beforehand...."

Andronicus's mother looked at her son with a troubled face.

"Marius... a man decides his own affairs, and that is the tradition of the house of Andronicus. Only this I ask: remember that you are the sole heir of the house, and judge all things wisely."

"Thank you, Mother. I will remember your words."

John raised his voice slightly and looked behind him.

"Master Drusianus of the house of Tyrannus is here also, is he not? What say you? Do you have any objection to your daughter's marriage?"

Then a deep voice answered from behind.

"No, Apostle. We already consented to the marriage last night."

John nodded and then said again to Polycarp,

"Let us go."

They began to follow after John and Polycarp. After walking for a long while and turning several times, they came at last to a place that suddenly widened. In that broad space stood a heavy stone table, and upon it was a candlestick holding seven candles. When Polycarp lit the seven candles, the chamber suddenly brightened.

While Polycarp set out bread and wine and prepared the communion, John called Andronicus and Drusiana forward and made them kneel. Then he asked Andronicus,

"Andronicus, do you know that before the rites of marriage and communion, baptism must also be received?"

"I know."

When he had investigated the activities of the Christiani in Rome, he had already studied all their rites, and so he knew well what baptism was. Originally it was performed in running water, as a rite of repentance for past wrongs and resolve for a new life. Where there was no running water, it was done in standing water, and where even that was lacking, water was poured three times upon the head. Today, apparently, the third method would be used, for the young man named Polycarp was bringing out a jar of water.

John told the young man to bring the Gospel of Matthew. The young man laid before John a papyrus scroll, which John opened and began to read.

"...Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, teaching them to observe all that I have commanded you. And behold, I am with you always, even to the end of the world...."

He laid down the scroll and spoke to those assembled.

"Brethren, today, according to the command of Jesus Christ, I am about to baptize Marius Andronicus, who is here. Marius Andronicus, answer my questions."

Andronicus, bewildered, turned toward Drusiana, and she whispered softly,

"Just answer, 'Yes.'"

John began his questions.

"Marius Andronicus, do you believe that God created heaven and earth, and that His only Son Jesus Christ was conceived by the Holy Spirit and born of the Virgin Mary?"

"...."

"Marius Andronicus, do you believe that Jesus Christ suffered under Pontius Pilate, was crucified, died, and was buried, and on the third day rose again from the dead?"

"...."

"Marius Andronicus, do you believe that the risen Jesus ascended into heaven, sits at the right hand of Almighty God, and will come to judge the living and the dead?"

"...."

"Marius Andronicus, do you believe in the Holy Spirit, the holy assembly, and the communion of saints?"

"...Yes."

"Marius Andronicus, do you believe that the Lord forgives sins, that the body shall rise again, and that there is life everlasting?"

"...."

When the questions ended, John took up the jar of water.

"Marius Andronicus, I baptize you in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit."

Then all those gathered answered,

"Amen."

John tilted the jar over Andronicus's head and poured the water three times. Then the responses of apostle and congregation continued.

"May the Lord be with you."

"And also with the Apostle."

"Lift up your hearts."

"We have come before the Lord."

"Let us give thanks to the Lord."

"It is right to do so."

"Almighty Father, we thank You that today You have chosen this son of Yours, Marius Andronicus, by Your own hand and caused him to be born anew. Now that he has become Your son, grant that he may be married to Your daughter Drusiana. We pray in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ."

"Amen."

"Marius Andronicus, do you take Drusiana as your wife, and will you cherish, care for, and love her in joy and in sorrow, in pain and in difficulty?"

"...Yes."

"God made man male and female and said that a man shall leave his father and mother and be joined to his wife, and the two shall become one flesh. Therefore these two are now one flesh, and what God has joined together, no man shall separate."

Then John's question turned to Drusiana.

"Drusiana, do you take Marius Andronicus as your husband, and will you in the Lord support and love your husband in joy and in sorrow, in pain and in difficulty?"

"Yes."

"Amen."

John commanded Polycarp to bring the letter Paul had sent to the church of Ephesus, and read a passage from it.

"Wives, submit to your husbands as to the Lord. For the husband is the head of the wife, just as Christ, the Savior of His body the church, is its head. As the church submits to Christ, so wives should submit to their husbands in all things. Husbands, love your wives just as Christ loved the church and gave His body for her."

"Amen."

"May the Lord be with you."

"And also with the Apostle."

"The Lord broke the bread and gave it to His disciples, saying, Receive the flesh of Christ."

John took the bread Polycarp handed him, broke it, and lifted a prayer of thanksgiving upward.

"Almighty Father, You sent Your only Son into the world and broke His body, giving it to us and commanding us to receive it. We thank You that even today You give us Your flesh for our salvation."

"Amen."

He took the broken bread and came before bride and bridegroom. Again Drusiana whispered,

"Open your mouth."

When Andronicus opened his mouth, John put the piece of bread into it and said,

"The flesh of Jesus Christ."

For some reason Andronicus could not bring himself to chew it. He could not understand how the flesh broken by that carpenter from Nazareth in Judea had now entered his own mouth. Again he remembered crying out to Jesus, asking whether He had given flesh and blood even to him.

John came forward once more. Again Drusiana's voice was heard.

"Open your mouth."

This time it was wine. John poured the wine into his mouth and said,

"The blood of Jesus Christ."

The fragment of bread still remaining in his mouth slipped down his throat together with the wine. Suddenly his chest began to burn as though scorched by fire.

(How did I ever come to this?)

In truth, Andronicus himself could no longer make sense of anything. Only the night before, when he had entered Ephesus aboard the Vesta, he had been a young official of the Roman Empire sent with the emperor's special command to arrest and escort John. Yet now that same Andronicus had received baptism from John, had been married by him, and had even received and eaten the eucharisteo said to be the flesh and blood of the carpenter of Nazareth. It was exactly the sort of thing Hesthenes would later mock him about.

John's voice continued.

"May the Lord be with you."

"And also with the Apostle."

"Let us give thanks to the Lord."

"Our Father who art in heaven, hallowed be Thy name. Thy kingdom come. Thy will be done on earth as it is in heaven. Give us this day our daily bread. And forgive us our sins as we forgive those who sin against us. Lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from evil."

"For Thine is the kingdom and the power and the glory forever."

"And forever with Jesus Christ."

"Amen."

All the rites were ended. The whole congregation came forward and kissed the bride and bridegroom as a sign of blessing. Even while receiving their congratulations, Andronicus did not think of himself as a Christianus. He was still a young official of the Roman Empire, an ambitious rising star. He was by no means such a romantic young man that he would throw away everything merely to obtain the woman he loved. His future still stretched before him, and the expectations of Rome rested upon him. Yet whatever else was true, that day had undeniably left him with a shadow of unease.

To take a Christiana as his wife was clearly disadvantageous, and that he himself had received the baptism of the Christiani and eaten their communion would be exceedingly dangerous if it became a subject of gossip among the emperor's delatores. To cast off this unease, he smiled faintly to himself and asked inwardly,

(Marius Andronicus, are you truly prepared to lose everything in order to gain Drusiana?)

He slowly shook his head and looked around at the congregation.

"I understand that when your rite is ended, you usually hold a meal called an agape. This place seems somewhat inconvenient for such a gathering, so I invite you all to my house. Drusiana and I will receive the ordinary guests first at the bride's house and then come. I would be grateful if you would move beforehand to my house."

At once the faces of the Christiani who had attended the ceremony grew stiff. A young man stepped forward and cried,

"We thank you for the invitation, but our circumstances...."

Andronicus at once understood what he meant and replied,

"I will station layers of guards around my house. There is also an underground passage prepared there for emergency escape. If you receive me as a brother, then please do not refuse my invitation."

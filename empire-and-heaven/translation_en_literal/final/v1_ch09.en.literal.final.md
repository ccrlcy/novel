# Empire and Heaven (I)

## Chapter 9. The Emperor and the Fisherman

Emperor Domitian, tormented all night by nightmares, was sleeping late, long after the morning sun had risen high. At first glance he appeared healthy, with his somewhat heavy body, thick neck, and ruddy complexion, yet cold sweat was running down his brow. Empress Domitilla, standing at the window and looking out toward the Circus Maximus, wiped the emperor's forehead with her handkerchief.

For a moment it seemed that the emperor's breathing stopped, and then his right hand slipped beneath the pillow. Empress Domitilla quickly said,

"Your Majesty, be at ease. It is Domitilla."

Only then did the emperor open his eyes and stare piercingly into his wife's face. Already in his right hand was the dagger he had drawn from beneath the pillow.

"You were sweating on your brow... I merely wiped it with a cloth."

"Why were you not sleeping... and watching me?"

"My dear lord... it is already late morning. At this hour, in this palace of yours, the only one who still had his eyes closed was Your Majesty alone."

"Is that so?"

He turned his scowling face toward the window, then put the dagger back under the pillow and said,

"It is an unpleasant thing that I alone should sleep while everyone else is awake. From now on, when the emperor sleeps, everyone should sleep with him."

"Your Majesty... with Your Majesty alone excepted, if those below sleep too much... will they not merely grow lazy and produce more children?"

"This is no time for jokes."

The empress clapped her hands, summoning the attendants who were waiting nearby.

"The emperor has coughed."

Male and female attendants came in carrying various implements and began to prepare the emperor's toilette. They washed his face and shaved him, then applied all manner of cosmetics; when the trimming of fingernails and toenails was finished, male attendants kneaded his arms and legs in massage.

Soon afterward a wheeled dining table was brought in. Domitian, being anxious about obesity, took only a light breakfast, and so on the table there was only a glass of fig juice and a dish of seasoned asparagus.

While the emperor drank the juice, Empress Domitilla sat beside him and looked on with pity.

"Your Majesty, did you not sleep well last night either?"

"Domitilla, I am the man who rules the whole world. Do you think there can be even a moment when I am at ease?"

"Your Majesty, the whole world is in Rome's hand, and there is not a single province in rebellion. Every Roman citizen follows you and obeys you. It seems that now Your Majesty may perhaps allow your heart a little ease."

"That is precisely the problem, Domitilla. A man who stands in the place of ruling others, the moment he begins to be intoxicated even briefly with comfort, his heart grows slack and corrupt."

Just then a chamberlain entered and reported that a visitor sought audience with the emperor.

"Musicius, liaison officer of the Third District, requests to see Your Majesty."

"Send him in."

The Third District meant Ionia, that is, the region of Asia Minor. Musicius was the man in charge of the delatores active in the Asia Minor region.

When the chamberlain withdrew, a middle-aged man wearing an ordinary white toga came in and bowed deeply.

"You have come early, Musicius."

"I arrived some time ago and was waiting outside."

"Is that so? How do matters stand there these days?"

"For the moment, they remain peaceful."

"And Governor Lycomedes?"

"No fault has yet appeared on the surface. However, lately..."

"What is it?"

"Greek philosophers have from time to time been entering the governor's residence. Apollonius among them, for example...."

"Apollonius? According to your report, is he not a scholar with a rather rebellious disposition?"

"It is precisely that point that causes some concern."

"Organize delatores closer around Governor Lycomedes and draw out the central information."

Musicius looked at the emperor with a shadowy smile.

"Measures have already been taken."

"Well done. And... what became of Andronicus?"

"Yes. Quaestor Andronicus, as Your Majesty commanded, arrested the old man called John of Ephesus and arrived in Rome yesterday, and the prisoner was confined in the Mamertine Prison."

"And his movements?"

"There were no movements that might be thought suspicious. In Ephesus Andronicus spent most of his time searching for John's whereabouts, and there was nothing such as meeting subversive persons."

"And when John was arrested, how did the Christiani move?"

"In arresting John, Andronicus displayed his exceptional political skill. On the day of his own wedding he invited the orphans of Ephesus to his house and entertained them, thereby winning the favor of the Christiani, and then persuaded John and made him willingly accompany him to Rome."

"A wedding?"

"That is right. The day after arriving in Ephesus, Andronicus took as his bride a maiden named Drusiana from the house of Tyrannus in Ephesus and celebrated a wedding. Governor Lycomedes and the other leading persons of Ephesus were invited to the feast, though it was concluded rather simply."

"A maiden from Ephesus...."

The emperor turned his head and looked toward Empress Domitilla.

"It seems Andronicus... has no intention of advancing."

"So it seems. He is a pity, for if one is to advance in Rome, he should at least know that a bride must be sought from a noble Roman house."

"Perhaps he is more foolish than I thought."

"I do not think that is so, Your Majesty."

Domitian's eyes flashed as he looked at Musicius.

"Not so?"

"Political climbers have long, for the safety of their own persons, deliberately kept their faces from appearing at the center. Even Your Majesty's honored father Vespasian... in order to avoid Agrippina and Emperor Nero, continually remained only at the far edges of the frontier."

"Even so, he did not marry a country girl. A man with ambition does not do that. And is there anything else to report concerning Andronicus?"

"There is one thing more. Before entering the city of Rome by way of the Appian Way, he was invited to the villa of former Consul Clemens."

"And for what reason was that? Are you saying my niece Domitilla invited him herself?"

"That is so. We do not know what was discussed inside the villa, but considerable time passed."

"Why were you unable to penetrate as far as the inside?"

"Because the brothers Nereus and Achilleus, former chamberlains of former Consul Clemens, were keeping such close watch on the surroundings, it was very difficult for us, who were not there on official business, to approach in secret. However, I do not think it is cause for great anxiety."

"Why is that?"

"Even if Quaestor Andronicus had some connection with the household of Clemens, now that the principal man has already been executed, it will no longer become a serious problem. In our analysis, because Lady Domitilla herself was a Christiana, she most likely invited the company in order to meet the old man named John."

"Then... might not the Christiani be plotting some conspiracy?"

"On that point as well there seems to be nothing to worry about. John and Lady Domitilla, and also the brothers Nereus and Achilleus, have all already been confined in the Mamertine Prison."

"You have done well. Then are you departing again for Ephesus?"

"I shall do so."

"Keep strict watch on Governor Lycomedes's movements, and hand over the matter concerning Andronicus in full detail to the chief of the First District."

"I understand perfectly. Farewell, Flavius Domitian, Emperor our master and our god."

When Musicius withdrew, the empress studied Domitian's face with an anxious expression.

"Your Majesty, is it truly necessary to pay such attention to the Christiani?"

"Domitilla, an emperor's rule must be perfect. If even a small problem is left alone, it always becomes the seed of trouble."

"And yet... are the Christiani causing some problem?"

"There is a danger that they may bewilder the minds of the people and damage the patriotism of Roman citizens."

"Your Majesty, if one wishes to steady the minds of the people, would it not be better to gather Roman citizens beneath the wings of Pax Romana? If too many people are arrested, among the citizens there will be unease and fear...."

"That is exactly it. Peace is not maintained through weakness, but guaranteed by the power and fervor of the ruler. The people always carry dissatisfaction in their hearts. No amount of benefit can entirely quiet the discontent of the people. The ruler must show them majesty and provide them a way to discharge their dissatisfaction."

"Do you mean such things as gladiatorial combat or the burning of Christiani?"

"Exactly. That is why my father Emperor Vespasian completed the construction of the Colosseum as the work of his life. Public entertainments have three great advantages. First, by concentrating the people's attention in one place, they prevent the division of public sentiment. Second, they guide people to release their dissatisfaction. And while they delight in cruel spectacles, they gradually and without realizing it come to possess fear and powerlessness, and thus the people are tamed."

"But if all the people become powerless, will not the strength of Rome be weakened?"

"Rome is not defended by the people, but by the emperor and the emperor's strong heavy infantry. What Rome needs is a strong army, a cooperative Senate, and a cooperative popular assembly."

"Then why not abolish the Senate and the popular assembly altogether?"

"The Senate and the popular assembly are necessary because, by being present at what the emperor does, they share the responsibility. Institutions that speak for the people become troublesome when they talk as they please, but when they become cooperative witnesses to the process, they are very convenient."

"Then... the Christiani do not speak as they please, nor are they plotting conspiracies, so why are they feared?"

"It is because they believe in something more fearful than silence. They believe that this Roman Empire is not their country."

"If not...?"

"They believe their country is not this Roman Empire, but a heavenly kingdom somewhere, no one knows where. That can only be called a denial of the Roman Empire and rebellion."

"And yet they are not displaying rebellious action toward Rome."

"That is a conspiracy more terrible than rebellion. Already too many Roman citizens have been bewitched by their faith, and the number of those who refuse to bow before the emperor's image is increasing. If this continues, the Roman Empire may be overturned from top to bottom. I feel it is my mission to crush them. We must drive this cultic group out from our Roman Empire."

Empress Domitilla tilted her head as though unable to understand.

"If their strength is truly so great, why do they not make rebellion appear in action?"

"That is the crafty thing about them. They are scheming to bewitch all Roman citizens and overturn the empire from its very foundations. Already most of the urban poor and country peasants are in their grasp, and even in the popular assembly and in the Senate a considerable number have fallen into them. And... as you know, in the end even former Consul Glabrio and Clemens were drawn in, and now even my own niece Domitilla has fallen into their hands. Then who is to protect this Roman Empire?"

Domitian looked at Domitilla with a troubled expression. Rubbing his broad forehead with his fingers, he sank into thought.

"But, Your Majesty, Domitilla is...."

"What is it you are trying to say to me?"

"Domitilla may simply have thought wrongly for a time, because she is still a woman who does not yet understand the world."

Domitian struck the table with his fist and sprang to his feet.

"You heard what Musicius reported just now, did you not? That girl invited into her own house John, the ringleader of the cult that was being brought from Ephesus under escort. Domitilla chose that old man John over Domitian, the emperor of Rome and her own uncle. And you...."

He shot a sharp look at his wife Domitilla and said,

"I ask you. Your husband Domitian is now fighting a lonely battle against a great power that seeks to overturn the Roman Empire. Therefore... I want at least my wife, you alone, to remain on my side."

"Your Majesty, I...."

"There is no need to say more. I know very well what you are going to say. What troubles me is simply that of late you have spoken more and more in a tone that defends the Christiani. And... your chief chamberlain Stephanos seems to go out far too often. If it is not too inconvenient, for the safety of your own person it would be better to replace the chief chamberlain."

For a moment Domitilla's face seemed to grow pale, but then again a bright smile rose upon it.

"Your Majesty, surely... you are not jealous of the broad-shouldered Stephanos?"

"What nonsense!"

Then Domitilla approached the emperor and kissed him on the cheek.

"So you still take an interest in me. It seems I have not yet... grown too old for you?"

When Domitilla kissed him once more, a chamberlain entered.

"Quaestor Marius Andronicus waits in the audience chamber to report to Your Majesty."

"Very well. At once send word to the Senate and have the senators stand ready, and in the square before the Senate have an oil cauldron set up. And send word that Lady Flavia Domitilla and her accomplices, together with the man named John brought from Ephesus, are to be held ready in the square."

"Yes, Your Majesty."

The empress's face was growing pale again. After the chamberlain withdrew, the emperor said to Domitilla,

"I must change clothes. Have my imperial robes brought."

After Empress Domitilla had gone out to summon the maids, Domitian took off his tunic and stood before the mirror looking over his own body.

(That broad-shouldered Stephanos....)

From the banquets that continued day after day and his lack of exercise, he was becoming fat. As he looked down at the belly where thick layers of fat had gathered, his eyes came to rest upon the shell hanging from his neck. He laid a hand on it and fingered it gently. It was small, yet it shone brilliantly with an iridescent glow.

"When will you give me that shell?"

Domitilla, who had come in with the maids, said so.

"You have jewels gathered from all over the world. Why do you covet this thing?"

"Because it is something Your Majesty cherishes. You have never once taken off that shell necklace."

"It seems that you, too... are jealous of this shell."

"A splendid counterattack."

When the emperor had finished changing his clothes and was about to go out to the audience chamber, Domitilla caught hold of his sleeve and whispered in a low voice,

"Your Majesty, surely... you are not intending to fry your niece in oil?"

"Again you are defending Christiani."

"Your Majesty, you changed my name to Domitilla. And do not forget that the niece you are about to judge bears the same name as Your Majesty's mother, and the same name as your wife, me. If she enters the oil cauldron, remember that at one stroke your mother and your wife will both be fried."

"Domitilla, I tell you again... do not leave me lonely."

The emperor went out to the audience chamber with the hem of his purple robe fluttering. Marius Andronicus, who had been standing there waiting in a proper posture, gave a crisp salute.

"Your Majesty, I have returned after carrying out the tasks you commanded."

"Oh, Andronicus... you do not look weary at all."

"Not at all, Your Majesty. I was greatly weary, but when I behold Your Majesty's radiant countenance, it seems new strength rises in me."

"Andronicus... your way with words is as smooth as that of the Greek philosophers."

In the emperor's words a snare was always laid. The Roman military spirit looked down upon the speculative culture of Hellas.

"Thank you. I have one Greek servant, and as he is so worthless, I suppose I am beginning to resemble my servant."

Domitian kept staring into the eyes of Andronicus as he said this. Whenever he fixed that stare upon another, there were two intentions behind it. One was the suspicious gaze asking whether there was any sign of rebellion; the other was the look by which he determined whether the person before him could be safely trusted and used. Under Domitian's gaze, the fate of those who met his eyes divided into two roads: either they were branded as useless and cast down into ruin, or they kept their lives only to be used as the emperor's inner instruments.

Andronicus, who had become quaestor at the age of twenty-three, knew at least this much beforehand. He knew well that at such a moment one must never give the emperor the impression that he was a man more capable than the emperor himself. Still staring hard at him, the emperor asked again,

"So... how was Ephesus?"

"It was my hometown, which I had not visited in a long time, and so everything felt new, and everything felt dear. Therefore...."

"Therefore..."

"I married the woman I had marked out in my heart before ever leaving home."

"What?"

Raising his voice a little, Domitian said as though in anger,

"In the end, then, you have refused my offer to arrange the match for you."

"I beg your pardon, Your Majesty."

"Andronicus, I had thought that, as a young talent in whom our fatherland Rome places its expectation, you would possess a sense of mission. Yet are you saying that, led away by private affection, you married a provincial maiden?"

"Your Majesty... in truth, this marriage was undertaken in order to carry out Your Majesty's command in perfect fashion."

At that, once again the emperor's expression changed abruptly.

(This fellow is not an easy man.... One moment he seems a fool, and the next he says something wholly unexpected....)

The emperor still kept the look of anger upon his face. After glancing at that face, Andronicus went on in a more unhurried tone.

"The woman I married was a young lady of the house of Tyrannus, a scholarly family of Ephesus. And it was known that at the lecture hall run by the house of Tyrannus the assemblies of the Christiani were often held."

"...?"

"Just as I had anticipated, Drusiana, the young lady of the house of Tyrannus, took a strong interest in the Christiani. Therefore, when I asked her to marry me, I said that I wished to receive the officiation of John, leader of the Christiani."

"What? Then are you saying you were married according to their rite?"

"I thought that was the best way to bring out the hidden John."

Domitian stood with his mouth open, unable to close it.

"What, then... you...?"

"Of course I held two weddings. Once I held a proper ceremony with His Excellency Governor Lycomedes and the other dignitaries present; and then, in secret, I invited John, the leader of the Christiani, and celebrated yet another wedding according to the Christiani rite."

"Man, do you know that at this very moment you are speaking before the emperor who has judged that his own nephew-in-law, Consul Clemens, should be executed under the charge of being a Christianus?"

Andronicus looked at the emperor with a grave expression.

"Your Majesty, for the sake of my fatherland and Your Majesty, I stand prepared gladly to accept death, mockery, or anything even worse. Your Majesty, not only at the wedding, but from John I also received baptism, their rite of admission, and I received the rite called eucharisteo and ate bread and wine. Now my life rests entirely in Your Majesty's hands. Whenever Your Majesty commands it, I may be executed. This Marius Andronicus is a man who lives by entrusting his life to Your Majesty's hand."

In Domitian's gaze there was beginning to appear a light of deep emotion. Into his harsh yet always lonely expression, Andronicus had leapt with truly exquisite timing. At last Domitian could not suppress that emotion, and he seized the shoulders of the young quaestor and shook them vigorously.

"Marius Andronicus. You are indeed an extraordinary young man. So long as Rome possesses men of your sort, anxiety for her future will be needless."

"Your words are more than I deserve, Your Majesty. I merely acted according to my conviction so as to carry out Your Majesty's command without enraging the Christiani of Ephesus. I followed their rites, I married according to their rite, and I invited the orphans of the city into my house and gained their favor. John himself resolved to come to Rome, and thus we arrived safely."

Andronicus already knew that matters of this sort would all have been reported by the emperor's organization of delatores.

Still holding the quaestor by the shoulder, Domitian opened his mouth in order to confirm one thing more.

"When did you arrive in Rome?"

Andronicus likewise knew why the emperor was asking.

"I arrived yesterday. When I reached outside the city by the Appian Way, I was received by Your Majesty's niece, Lady Domitilla, and ate supper at her villa."

Once again Andronicus's answer struck directly at the emperor's expectation. He was indeed a man who hid nothing before the emperor.

"Why did Domitilla invite you?"

With a faint smile on his face, Andronicus answered,

"It is very regrettable, but the beautiful Lady Domitilla invited not me, but John."

"And what did Domitilla speak of with that old man?"

"To speak honestly, I could not well understand the substance of their conversation. Only this: Lady Domitilla asked many things about that carpenter of Nazareth, and John told various stories about the carpenter whom he had followed."

Now the emperor had finished all that he wished to verify with Andronicus. Removing his hand from the quaestor's shoulder, he looked at the trustworthy profile of the young man.

"You have done well, Quaestor Andronicus. Now it is time to go and meet the old man John whom you have brought."

The emperor and Andronicus left the audience chamber side by side and boarded the imperial carriage waiting in the courtyard of the palace. Following behind the guards, the emperor's carriage took the lead, and that of Empress Domitilla followed after. The long imperial procession left the palace, passed the Palatine Hill, and entered the Roman Forum.

Already the senators, wearing togas with broad purple stripes, had come out and stood upon the steps before the Senate house, and in the middle of the square a great oil cauldron had been set up. The emperor's carriage stopped before the cauldron. Beneath it a fierce red fire was already blazing. Above the cauldron hung a tall tripod made of logs, and from it hung a chain with a hook attached to a pulley. The device was for hanging the bound prisoner upon the hook, raising him into the air, and then lowering him little by little into the boiling oil, beginning with his ankles.

The emperor dismounted, inspected every detail of the preparations, and then walked toward John, who had been brought out and was standing there bound. John looked at the emperor with a perfectly calm gaze. Lady Domitilla, standing beside John, bowed lightly in greeting to her uncle the emperor. The emperor's expression suddenly turned cold. He glanced over the brothers Nereus and Achilleus standing behind Lady Domitilla, then turned his feet and strode up toward the chair set upon the Senate steps. When emperor and empress had seated themselves, all became quiet as though water had been poured over them. Now the emperor's personal inquest was about to begin.

"Are you John, once a disciple of Jesus of Nazareth?"

The emperor asked toward the prisoner John standing below the steps.

John lifted his eyes to the emperor.

"...I am."

Domitian was feeling something strange. The question sent from this side had been full of dagger-like hostility, whereas the other man's bearing was suffocatingly calm. It was a feeling he had commonly felt from all the Christiani he had interrogated. The emperor drew his brows together and began to tense. For now the decisive confrontation was taking place: Domitian, emperor ruling the Roman Empire, was examining John, supreme leader of the Christiani who awaited the heavenly kingdom.

"When did you first come to know Jesus of Nazareth?"

"I was eighteen years old, so it was sixty-seven years ago."

Domitian calculated John's age in his head. If he had been eighteen sixty-seven years ago, then he had been born in the thirty-sixth year of Augustus, and so he would be the same age as Domitian's father Vespasian.

"And at that time, what were you doing?"

"I was fishing at Capernaum."

"You mean you were a fisherman?"

"That is so."

At once a light of contempt passed through the emperor's eyes. For a moment there came the sound of whispering among the senators.

"So then... what did Jesus of Nazareth teach you?"

John looked up briefly toward the sky, as though sinking into thought, and then opened his mouth.

"...He taught about the kingdom of God."

"And this kingdom of God of which you speak... is it the same as the heavenly kingdom?"

"It is."

"Then are you saying that your God is in heaven?"

"Heaven... is a way of speaking of that which is not this world."

Truly Domitian, like a ruler of empire, was driving John with sharp interrogation.

"And what is this world? Do you mean the Roman Empire?"

"The Roman Empire also may be said to belong to this world."

Only then did Domitian let a cold smile rest upon his thin lips.

"Then the heavenly kingdom that Jesus of Nazareth taught means, after all, a kingdom that is not the Roman Empire?"

"It does."

"You...."

Domitian paused a moment, glared at John, and then asked sharply:

"You know well, do you not, that to teach and stir up another kingdom different from the Roman Empire is a denial of the Roman Empire and rebellion against it?"

"Your Majesty...."

John quietly lifted his eyes to the emperor.

"Your Majesty, denial and rebellion are possible only when the two belong to the same realm. It is just as heaven and earth cannot deny one another or rebel against one another. Jesus of Nazareth never denied or resisted the powers of this world."

For a moment Domitian was at a loss for words and looked around him. Marius Andronicus was standing quite near.

"Andronicus, what do you think of what that man says?"

Again Andronicus had been placed in a difficult situation. To ask a man's opinion was practically the same as telling him to accept responsibility for his answer. Moreover, the senators who were witnessing the emperor's decision were present in this place. What Andronicus thought was something he must not now speak.

"Most honored Flavius Domitian, Emperor our lord, and senators: in investigating the criminal charges of the Christiani, I have examined the report of Governor Pontius Pilate, who executed Jesus of Nazareth."

At once the senators all looked toward Quaestor Marius Andronicus. So far as they knew, the young official who enjoyed the emperor's trust above all others was about to offer decisive testimony concerning the Christiani.

"According to the report of Pontius Pilate... Governor Pilate, who arrested Jesus of Nazareth, concentrated his examination precisely on this issue of rebellion. That was because the religious leaders of Judea who arrested him and handed him over to the governor accused him as a rebel."

At that moment an old man among the senators opened his mouth in a loud voice.

"And what was the material evidence by which they accused Jesus of Nazareth?"

All turned their heads toward the direction from which the voice had come. The one who had asked the question was Ovidius, the jurist, famous in the Senate for speaking straight. Looking toward that side, Andronicus answered:

"The evidence by which they accused Jesus of Nazareth was that he called himself king of the Jews, and that he told people not to pay taxes to Caesar."

Andronicus looked again at the emperor and spoke.

"Governor Pilate asked Jesus of Nazareth, 'Art thou the king of the Jews?' At that time the prisoner's reply was... that his kingdom was not of this world."

Again Domitian smiled coldly and asked Andronicus,

"In the end, then, the heavenly kingdom of Jesus of Nazareth is nothing more than a concept opposed to the Roman Empire."

"And yet... the strange thing is that in Jesus' answer no evidence of such rebellion appeared. He said that if his kingdom were of this world, his servants would have fought for him."

By this a question had been raised as to the certainty that the Christiani were plotting practical rebellion against Rome.

"Then what in the world is the heavenly kingdom of Jesus of Nazareth?"

"On that point too Governor Pilate pressed him again. The governor asked, 'Art thou a king then?' and Jesus replied again that he was a king. When the governor asked what kind of king it was that claimed to be king all alone, he answered that he was a king come into this world to bear witness unto the truth."

Again Domitian asked Andronicus,

"And what is this truth of which he spoke?"

"Governor Pilate asked exactly the same question as Your Majesty. The governor asked, 'What is truth?' Yet Jesus, it is reported, closed his mouth and answered no more."

Domitian again looked down at John, standing below the steps.

"John, you answer. What is truth?"

"Truth is Jesus Christ himself."

"Why is a man truth?"

"Because Jesus Christ is the Son of God."

"What? Then to whom was that God married, that he begot Jesus? And who is the mother of Jesus?"

"His mother according to the flesh was a virgin called Mary who lived in the village of Nazareth."

"Hm... then are you saying God married that woman?"

"No. Jesus was conceived in Mary by the Holy Spirit."

"And what is the Holy Spirit? Is the Holy Spirit also a male?"

"The Holy Spirit is the providence of God working for the salvation of men."

"Then are you saying Jesus is truth, but God and the Holy Spirit are not truth?"

"No. God, the Holy Spirit, and Jesus are all truth."

"Then you are saying there are three truths."

"Those three are one."

Domitian's face was full of anger.

"Behold, this old man is mocking the emperor of Rome. This fellow has made sport of my inquiry and answered as he pleased, and now at last he babbles that three are one. There is no need to hear more. Thread that man upon the chain and hang him up."

The guards rushed toward John and fastened the hook to the rope that bound him. Then once again Ovidius spoke loudly from the senators' side.

"There is no urgency in the execution. Let us first hear how matters turned out regarding the second piece of evidence against Jesus... namely, the refusal of taxation."

Looking toward the Senate, Andronicus said,

"When they went to Jesus and asked, 'Is it lawful to give tribute to Caesar, or not?' his reply was...."

Looking down at John bound there, he said,

"His answer was this: Render unto Caesar the things which are Caesar's; and unto God the things that are God's...."

Again Ovidius of the Senate raised his voice and asked:

"And what is Caesar's, and what is God's?"

"Caesar's, of course, means the tax paid to the Roman government... and what is God's means their love toward God."

Domitian cast a sidelong glance at Andronicus and smacked his lips for a moment. Andronicus was truly an impossible man to grasp. In the emperor's audience chamber he had just shown himself quite clever, and now in this important setting he had again given dull testimony and thrown confusion into the line of argument the emperor had been driving.

The emperor drew a deep breath for a moment, then threw a question toward John, who was just about to be hung upon the hook.

"John, is what Quaestor Andronicus has said correct?"

"It is. The kingdoms of this world are visible kingdoms, but the heavenly kingdom is an invisible kingdom. Therefore we give visible coins to the kingdoms of this world, but to the kingdom of God we offer the invisible heart."

There was nothing in John's answer that the emperor could lay hold of, and yet he felt a deep insult and fury. The answer meant that he could be given tax, but not the heart. And that heart was something that neither the power nor the laws of Rome could ever compel.

Domitian glared at John with a most displeased expression.

"John, how do you think this Roman Empire will one day become?"

He had now resolved no longer to circle round through Jesus of Nazareth, but to drive straight at John himself. John also seemed to perceive that intention, for after gazing steadily at the emperor's face, he began slowly to answer.

"Your Majesty, all ruling powers of this world are imperfect. That is because every power in the world turns its face away from truth and executes itself according to its own desire. Therefore the powers of this world may be called powers of darkness, for they must live covering over countless contradictions and corruptions."

Domitian's expression was becoming violently distorted. John's words were now like a sharp blade, piercing instead into Domitian's own heart.

"Your Majesty, but we disciples of Jesus of Nazareth live bearing the authority of the heavenly kingdom. We live in the truth, and we offer our hearts only to him."

"And did not that carpenter of Nazareth whom you call truth bleed and die?"

"We believe in him because he did not remain dead, but rose again."

"Again you are misleading men with monstrous words. Jesus of Nazareth was executed already sixty-five years ago. Where is he now? If he has risen again, can you bring him to me?"

"Your Majesty, Jesus of Nazareth is in the invisible kingdom. If Your Majesty gives up all visible things, you will be able to meet him."

Domitian could no longer remain seated, and sprang up from his chair.

"John, you are causing the good people of the Roman Empire to grow dissatisfied with reality and are setting the people and the government at odds with one another. Your true purpose is to overturn the Roman Empire. Speak plainly. How do you think this Roman Empire will become?"

"The Roman government is turning away from many truths. Beneath the banner of Pax Romana, of which Rome boasts, multitudes of people are dying of hunger, and they are becoming sick in spirit. The number of rich who do not work is increasing, and in the Tiber River the number of abandoned infants is swelling. Love is disappearing from among men, and only intrigue and greed are running wild. A kingdom of darkness like this does not endure long. However deep the darkness may be, when the kingdom of light enters, it cannot withstand it. Even if that light is only a weak little lamp, it will still illumine the darkness, and the darkness will feel pain. Even now, Your Majesty, you yourself are feeling that pain."

"Now all things are plain. This old man has called the emperor of Rome a beast of darkness, and has declared that the Roman Empire will perish. By this the true nature of the Christiani has become plain."

The emperor's tone was full of murderous force. As his sharp gaze swept across them, not one senator dared make a sound. Lifting his finger to point at John, the emperor said,

"I, Flavius Domitian Caesar, according to the laws of the Roman Empire and the judgment of the Senate, sentence the rebel John to death."

As the guards began drawing on the chain of the tripod, John's body started to rise into the air. Once he hung above the cauldron, his body would gradually be lowered into the boiling oil, beginning from his ankles.

"Wait."

With cold eyes fixed upon John, Domitian spoke to the soldiers.

"One cannot very well prepare fried food with the clothes still on. Strip that old man naked and fry him slowly until he is crisp."

The soldiers ran in and tore off John's garments. When the plane of his chest was exposed in the sunlight, Domitian noticed that something there was glittering.

"...?"

The emperor's face was reddening as though he had been drinking wine.

Turning toward the senators, he said,

"Senators loyal to Rome, if there is any objection to the charge of treason brought against the Christiani and their ringleader John, speak now."

Already John's body was rising, dangling in the air. Keeping his eyes fixed upon that glittering thing, Domitian descended the steps and walked almost as far as the cauldron.

What hung from John's neck was a small shell. Domitian stared hard at it, then ordered the soldiers to lower John's body again. When John's naked body was brought down, the emperor approached and looked closely at the shell hanging from his neck. At last he stretched out his hand and seized it.

"W-where did this shell come from?"

John kept silent, his fiery eyes fixed upon those of the emperor. Domitian drew the sword of the guard standing beside him and brought it to John's neck. For a moment there flowed a silence tight enough to stop breathing. At length the emperor cut with the sword the filthy cord by which the shell was strung. Holding the shell in his hand, he slowly climbed the steps of the Senate.

Then he turned once more and looked at John.

"...I, Flavius Domitian Caesar, revise the judgment. Until that ringleader of the rebels recovers his loyalty to the Roman Empire, I order that he be exiled to the island of Patmos and subjected to hard labor. And...."

For a moment Domitian had fallen into confusion. In the palm of his hand he was still holding that tiny shell.

It was the exact same shape as the shell he himself possessed. Only this: one hung on a gold chain, and the other had hung upon a filthy cord.

Domitian himself did not know why that shell had hung from his neck ever since childhood. Neither his father Vespasian, nor his mother Domitilla, nor anyone else of the Flavian house had ever told him its history.

Wiping the sweat from his brow, he looked again into the sun. The light was dazzling. Frowning, he now looked toward his niece Domitilla, who was still standing below the steps.

"Domitilla, lift your head."

Lady Domitilla's face had grown somewhat pale, yet she still looked calmly toward her uncle.

"Domitilla, as a citizen of Rome, as the granddaughter of the former Emperor Vespasian, the daughter of Titus, and the niece of this Domitian, and as the wife of the consul, the highest administrative officer of this nation, why did you consort with the Christiani?"

"...Because I loved Jesus of Nazareth."

"Do not speak such foolishness, Domitilla!"

Domitian shouted loudly in anger.

"What are you saying now? You are a married woman, and the mother of two sons who possess the right of succession to the imperial throne of this day. How can you speak so lightly of loving another man?"

"Your Majesty, it is a love different from what the people of Rome think of as love."

"I do not wish to hear it. If all women love him, Jesus of Nazareth is nothing but a lecher who corrupts public morals. Why is it that, when Rome has great gods like Jupiter, Bacchus, and Hermes, you insist on loving nothing more than a mere carpenter?"

"Because they are not gods, but only idols made by men."

"Silence! Do you dare blaspheme the gods in this holy square beneath the gaze of Jupiter's temple?"

As the emperor sprang up once more, Empress Domitilla rose with him and whispered urgently,

"Your Majesty, if you impose the extreme penalty upon her, you will become an unpopular man."

Domitian ground his teeth with a harsh rasp and let out a groan.

"Exile that obscene woman to the island of Pontia."

When the emperor wheeled about, about to go into the Senate house, a secretary ran up and said,

"Your Majesty, what shall be done with the brothers Nereus and Achilleus, who were arrested with Lady Domitilla?"

"Send them all together to the island of Pontia."

As though there was still business left, the secretary followed after the emperor and asked again,

"And under what charge shall we record the crimes of Lady Domitilla and those brothers, Your Majesty?"

As though annoyed, the emperor spat out his answer.

"Atheism."

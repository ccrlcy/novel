# Empire and Heaven (II)

## Chapter 4. Beyond the Years

There was no dramatic performance that day, nor was there any great assembly like the riot of Demetrius, and yet many people had gathered in the theater of Ephesus. But those who had gathered were not the wealthy citizens of Ephesus who came to watch plays. They were all beggars in rags or very old people, and then a few onlookers had climbed up merely to stare at them. One of them said,

"At this rate the great theater of Ephesus will turn into a den of beggars."

"Has it not already looked like this for more than a year?"

"Even if there is a play, I do not feel like coming to watch because of these beggars."

"That is why the actors are miserable too. They cannot simply stop performing, yet if they do perform, they have to put on a show for beggars..."

"At this point it seems they cannot drive the beggars out."

"They cannot drive them out. After all, this theater is a theater that gives free performances for the citizens of Ephesus, and these beggars are certainly citizens of Ephesus too...."

"But why are there so many beggars? Since when did Ephesus produce this many beggars?"

"It seems there are many people spreading the report that they were healed of sickness after receiving the prayer of that old man."

"It is something I heard from my elders too, but when a man named Paul, who was also one of the Christiani, came here some forty years ago, it seems there were events much like this then too. That is why the silversmiths who made a living producing souvenirs of Artemis stirred up a riot."

"If the rumors going around these days are true, the popularity of the goddess Artemis can hardly do anything but collapse."

There were still many people in Ephesus who lived by matters connected with Artemis. They were people who survived by making statues of the goddess Artemis or niches and by selling various tourist souvenirs themed after the goddess. Nor could the offerings that citizens and merchants brought to the temple of Artemis for the success of their business be dismissed as insignificant.

It was truly hard to believe. That a city as prosperous as Ephesus, the center of east-west trade and a flourishing city where every kind of commodity from all nations of the world came together, had so many beggars, was something that could not help but surprise every citizen. The theater of Ephesus had originally been a vast open-air theater designed to hold more than twenty-five thousand people. Yet now that enormous theater was filled with beggars and old people in wretched condition. And the beggars of this city were precisely the unavoidable blot upon the prosperous Roman Empire.

"Could it be that even beggars from other cities are hearing the rumor and swarming here?"

"You mean the rumor of that old man called John?"

"Do they not say he prays and heals diseases?"

"It seems there is some effect to it after all, does there not?"

That year too the festival of Artemis was drawing near. But the attention of the citizens was fixed more on John in the theater of Ephesus than on Artemis.

"That old man called John... they say he was even arrested and taken all the way to Rome and still came back alive?"

"I do not know whether it is true, but they say he did not die even when they put him into a cauldron of oil."

"A cauldron of oil?"

"They say Emperor Domitian himself interrogated him, and instead he prophesied that the emperor himself would soon die. So the enraged Domitian ordered John to be thrown into a cauldron of boiling oil, but that old man sat there calmly in the boiling oil as if he were in a bathhouse."

"Could that really be true...?"

"In any case, the Christiani seem to be a dreadful sort of people. Even in the present case, is it not so? Emperor Nerva has ascended the throne and the government has grown somewhat milder, but the controls on them have not been lifted, and yet without the least fear they are carrying on these things in the great theater of Ephesus...."

"Shh... it seems that old man over there is John."

Many people were rushing toward one old man and stretching out their hands. They were all sick people whose eyes had sunk in and whose faces were pale. Several young men were coming after the old man and distributing loaves from the baskets they were carrying.

"It must cost an enormous amount of money to feed this immense crowd...."

"If the Christiani heal sickness through prayer, ought they not also to be able to produce bread? The Jesus of Nazareth whom these beggars believe in is said actually to have fed five thousand men with five barley loaves and two fish."

"Do not talk nonsense. I have never heard that Artemis makes bread. Probably they all ended up eating by yielding to one another, and so it became as if everyone had eaten."

"I too thought so, but how do you explain that twelve baskets were left over after they had all eaten?"

John laid his hands one by one on the heads of the sick and prayed. At times he closed his eyes, and at times he looked up toward heaven, and without stopping he kept on praying. Many patients, old people, and beggars surged forward trying to touch John's hand. John said to them,

"My friends, my hands are human hands. Do not think these hands heal you. Entrust your anguish to God. God loves you. Because He loved you, He sent His own Son to you and let Him die. Because that Son died on this earth, all of you have become sons of God...."

Then a beggar from among the people asked,

"Does God marry too?"

"God loves."

"My friends... God is one who is alone. With whom would God speak? God made man, and God loved man, and because God loved man He spoke with man. What is truth? It is the promise of love that God gave to man...."

"Then... there is no great difference from gods like Zeus or Jupiter, is there?"

"God does not love only some particular object. He loves all of you."

"But they say that Jesus, who is called the Son of God, was born from one woman, do they not?"

"My friends, I will explain by using words you know well. Among the words you commonly use, there is the word Logos."

"That is a word philosophers use...."

"That is right. And the one who first used that word was Heraclitus, who was a citizen of this very Ephesus. He said that this Logos is the root of all laws governing the universe and all things, and the eternal spirit. Yet when you ordinarily use this Logos, you use it in the sense of the Word. Why is that? Because this Logos is eternal truth, that is, the Word of God."

When John explained the Logos, many people understood it. This Logos had been the proud discovery of Ephesian philosophy itself.

At last the people began nodding their heads and allowing themselves to be drawn into John's words. In the splendid streets of Ephesus there was no language to be found even if one washed out one's eyes to find it, that warm language called love, and the people were longing for it with burning hearts.

"God thought that He Himself must prove that promise of love to mankind. Therefore a woman named Mary was chosen. And the Word of God became flesh through Mary and was born into this world. That woman Mary returned to God here in Ephesus, yet she was not some particular object singled out as the recipient of God's love. She was only the representative of you all who received God's love."

John's voice was not loud, yet it overwhelmed the people. He was not speaking in the tone of an orator at all, and yet his words had more power than any eloquence, for they were a testimony rising out of his own inner conviction. Even the citizens who had been standing around the theater merely to watch were gradually drawing closer to him. John's calm voice was soaking into every corner of the great theater like water.

"In the beginning was the Word. This Word was with God, and the Word was God Himself. The Word was with God in the beginning, and all things received life from the Word, and that life was the light of men. The light shines in the darkness, and the darkness does not overcome the light...."

John's sermon in Greek was sufficient to persuade the people of Ephesus. Memories of Tiberias, where he had learned Greek together with John of Gischala, flashed through John's eyes like lightning.

"...There was a man sent by God, whose name was John. I feel so unworthy to bear the same name as that man that I always conceal my own name and do not use it, but he came to testify concerning the light. He came to bear witness to the light, and the Word of God was the true light, and that light came into this world and was shining on every man."

One of the young men standing behind John had spread out a sheet of papyrus and was eagerly taking down his sermon. It was Bishop Papias, who had come from Hierapolis to meet John. Polycarp and Prochorus, holding the baskets of bread, were showing him the passages he had failed to catch.

"...The Word was in the world, and the world came into being through the Word, yet the world did not recognize the Word...."

Prochorus nudged Polycarp's sleeve and whispered,

"Polycarp, do you recognize the people sitting there behind that cripple?"

Polycarp turned his eyes in the direction Prochorus indicated with his chin. There sat a husband and wife, beggars in rags.

"Ah, that is...."

"Hush. If you recognized them, that is enough. Be quiet."

Polycarp's face was mixed with amazement and joy. Those who were listening to John's sermon disguised in rags were none other than the governor and his wife. John's voice was rising somewhat.

"My friends... the birth of Jesus Christ was like this. His mother Mary was betrothed to Joseph, a carpenter of Nazareth, and before they lived together she became with child by the Holy Spirit. According to the law of the Jewish nation, if a woman conceived before marriage she was to be stoned to death, so kindhearted Joseph intended quietly to cancel the betrothal. But an angel of God appeared in a dream and told him that Mary's conception was of the Holy Spirit, so he was not to be afraid but to take her to himself. And he was told that she would bear a son and that he was to call His name Jesus. That meant that He would save His people from their sins...."

That the Savior would be born from the body of a virgin had already been prophesied by the prophet Isaiah more than seven hundred years before Jesus was born. Mary was chosen as that virgin, and she in turn came to Ephesus to become again a witness of the light, only to be struck with stones by the citizens of Ephesus.

"When Mary was full-term, Emperor Augustus issued the command for a registration of the whole world, and Joseph also went with Mary to Bethlehem, his hometown, for that registration. Bethlehem had long before been prophesied as the village where the Savior would be born. Yet as soon as they arrived in Bethlehem, Mary began to be in labor. Startled, Joseph hurried about looking for an inn, but he could not find a room. In the end Mary entered a stable, gave birth to the child, wrapped Him in swaddling cloths, and had no choice but to lay Him in a manger.... My friends, why do you think the Word of God was born in a stable? Even now, truth has no place to lie down in a rich man's house or in an inn room. The Word of God is born in a poor place. Among people like you, who have stripped off all the pleasures of the world and come forward weeping, truth breathes and is born.... Though your hearts may be shabby now, they are open. The Word of God will be born within you even now...."

The governor's wife, who was listening to the sermon among the beggars, secretly wiped away tears.

"My friends... what is truth? Pontius Pilate, the governor of Judea who ordered Jesus to be executed, asked that question, and Emperor Domitian, who interrogated me, also asked that question. Truth is the Logos, and it is the Word of God, and that Word came into this world. Truth was born in a stable, and truth breathed with us and lived with us. Truth loved the people, truth comforted the people, and truth healed the people. Yet truth was despised, truth bled, and truth closed His eyes upon the cross. The world became darkness again, and the earth shook. But truth did not die and rose again. I am a man who touched that truth, saw it with my eyes, and heard it with my ears. I always asked Him when His time would be. Only later did I realize that the question was foolish. For the time of God continues forever. Today also, at this very hour, the Word of God is being born, loving us, healing us, suffering, and dying countless times. The time of God comes to you beyond the years...."

The sound of weeping was being heard here and there. It was because words they had not heard for so very long were piercing their hearts like daggers.

"...The Word came to His own land, and no one received Him. But to those who received Him, that is, to those who believed in His name, He gave the right to become children of God. These are born not of blood, nor of the desire of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but only of God.... The Word became man and dwelt with us, and we saw His glory. It was the glory the only Son received from the Father, and He was full of grace and truth. Therefore John bore witness in this way. No one has ever seen God, but the only Son who is in the bosom of the Father has made God known to us."

Tears were flashing in John's eyes.

"...Even I myself, who followed Jesus in person, did not know who He was. He was one who possessed astonishing power, and yet He died nailed to the cross. And three days after He was buried, He rose again, and then departed from us leaving only the promise that He would come again. Foolish as I was, even into this old age I have waited for Him to come again and have thought and thought about who He was. And only now have I begun to meet Him. He was the way, the truth, and the life. He was the light, the Word, the vine, and the shepherd who keeps us...."

When he had finished speaking, John went about among the people, comforting them. It was certainly something strange to the people of Ephesus. There was no frenzy there such as the festival of Artemis had, and there was no endless greed for health or property. In John's words it seemed as though they were finding and drawing up from some deep well of memory something they had once lost, something that had been important.

Then another voice rose from among the people and flew toward John.

"Please teach us what we must do to be saved!"

John looked in the direction from which the voice had come. It was the husband from the couple who from earlier had been looking at John and listening eagerly to the sermon.

"God loved us and sent His only Son, so that everyone who believes in Him may not perish but obtain eternal life."

John spoke toward the place from which the question had come.

"Open your heart and receive Jesus Christ into yourself, and be baptized. And do what God desires."

Then countless people came crowding before John, saying they wanted to receive baptism. Polycarp and Prochorus hurriedly brought water jars.

John began administering baptism to them. There were too many to count. John's mouth was truly moving without pause.

"I baptize you in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit.... I baptize you in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit...."

Truly it was an enormous event. The edict restricting the Christiani had not yet disappeared, and the smell of blood in the arena had not yet faded, and yet here in the great theater of Ephesus a massive baptism was being conducted in broad daylight. John kept giving thanks to heaven again and again. The theater of Ephesus where Demetrius had stirred up a riot in order to overthrow Paul, that theater was now the place where such a thing was happening. In Ephesus, which had served Artemis with utmost devotion and taken proud delight in the name neokoros, that is, guardian of the goddess, and in that Ephesus where Mary the mother of Jesus had been struck with stones and had bled, a longing for salvation was now erupting.

Then someone else asked loudly,

"What is the work that God desires?"

"God desired that man should be a free being. Therefore He even left open for man the path by which he might betray Him. Though God knew well that if man went by that path, pain would one day come to God Himself, He valued man's freedom more highly than His own pain. Yet man made a mistake and walked that path of rebellion. Man grew distant from God, was shut up in sin, and little by little the shadow of death began to fall over mankind. At last the world grew dark. God sent light into that darkness, and that light has come also to you. Now God asks you to become witnesses of the light. Just as you have been comforted, healed, and forgiven by Him, you also comfort, heal, and forgive others. As God loved you, you also love people."

After continuing the baptismal rite a little longer, John turned again and said,

"In Jerusalem there once stood a beautiful temple built in the days of King Solomon. That temple was a magnificent and beautiful thing, built with one hundred thousand talents of gold, together with countless quantities of silver, bronze, iron, and cedar from Lebanon and stones. The Jewish people regarded this temple as their very life, because they believed that the ark of God's covenant was inside that temple and that God was there."

Again the people wanting baptism surged in, so John had no choice but to break off the story for a moment. He continued baptizing for quite some time and then resumed speaking.

"...Therefore, when the Jewish people returned after being released from the captivity in Babylon, the very first thing they did was repair the temple, and when they were delivered from the oppression of Antiochus Epiphanes, the first thing they did was cleanse the temple. And the Jewish people even made and kept a feast called the Feast of Dedication in remembrance of the renewal of the temple. On that very Feast of Dedication day, Jesus spoke to the people in the temple court. He declared that God was not in this temple but in Himself. It meant that a temple that does not do the work of God is already a temple that has been cast away."

Bishop Papias again hurriedly began taking down his words.

"Even three days before Jesus was arrested, He said the same thing in the temple at Jerusalem. Jesus watched people putting money into the temple treasury. The rich were putting in much, while one poor widow was putting in two small coins. The temple of Jerusalem, where many rich people and one poor widow coexisted, was already a temple that was not doing the work of God. No one cared for the widow, and the widow cast her coins into the temple treasury. The moment Jesus saw it, He said this: the temple of Jerusalem will be thrown down until not one stone is left upon another. And before long that temple was completely destroyed by General Titus until not one stone was left upon another. God does not dwell inside buildings made of stone. When you become children of the light... then God is right there among you. That is the kingdom of God."

John instructed the young men of the church in Ephesus who were following him to draw more water and prepare basins.

"My friends, where is God? Is He above the sky? Is He in the mountains? Is He inside a building? Because God is almighty, when you call on Him, He is right there. What is hell? It is when the Word of God has vanished from among you. What is the kingdom of God? It is when the Word of God grows and expands among you. The kingdom of God is not death but life. Even the person who is healed of disease today will someday die again. But if he walks with God, he receives eternal life. Comfort one another, and love one another."

John had the water the young men drew poured into the basins and began washing the feet of the beggars and old people. They did not dare stretch out their dirty feet before John, but each time they hesitated John spoke to them with the words Jesus had used to rebuke Peter.

"If I do not wash your feet, you have no part with me."

John seized the foot of one man who was trying to run away and looked at him. It was a familiar face.

"You are...?"

Then from behind him came the voice of old Tychicus.

"Please baptize him first, Apostle. He is Hesthenes, the servant of Quaestor Marius Andronicus."

John remembered him. It was he who had come to John's cave in Coresus and arranged his meeting with Andronicus, and while John was being escorted to Rome he had accompanied him the whole way. He had shown John various kindnesses as he went under arrest, and whether on shipboard or at the inns, whenever he found an opening he had come to John and asked questions about the teaching of Jesus.

John looked closely into Hesthenes's face. His eyes were badly bloodshot.

"Apostle... today, after hearing your words, I feel that my eyes have at last been opened."

John realized that the fact he had preached in Greek today had been fitting for Hesthenes.

"Hesthenes... do you desire to receive baptism today?"

Hesthenes was nodding his head. Then again from behind John old Tychicus said,

"I too ask you for that, Apostle. I speak as Hesthenes's father."

Then all those standing nearby were startled and looked at old Tychicus. But no one was more startled than Hesthenes himself. Old Tychicus spoke in a grave voice.

"Thirty-four years ago, as I was on my way back to Rome to report the affair of apostasy in Ephesus to Paul, I stopped in Corinth to learn what the situation in Rome was. And there I met a beautiful prostitute named Helene and earnestly explained the way of Christ for her salvation...."

Because of this sudden recollection from old Tychicus, who had lived his whole life unmarried, everyone was listening to the story with faces full of wonder.

"But that woman named Helene paid no attention to my preaching. Instead she kept thinking of me as a man and tempting me. At that time I was still in the full vigor of life, and the concern I had for her gradually deepened into pity. I came to think that true evangelism would be difficult if I treated a woman merely as a prostitute. At last I promised to marry Helene, spent one night with her, and then left for Rome...."

A beautiful memory was moving among the people. Hesthenes, his mouth hanging open, was staring at old Tychicus.

"But after I met Paul in Rome, I too was arrested and served thirty years in the mines of Hispania. During that time, while going in and out of the house of Quaestor Andronicus, I had chances to speak with Hesthenes, and I learned that he had been born in the brothel quarter of Corinth and that his mother's name had been Helene. Helene had waited for me, who never returned, and then fell sick and died, and Hesthenes grew up in the hands of a brothel keeper and was finally sold to a slave dealer...."

At last large tears began to fall from Hesthenes's eyes. He stood up and was walking toward old Tychicus.

"Why... why did you not tell me sooner...."

At last father and son embraced one another and burst into tears. Whenever he saw an old Greek living alone, and especially whenever he met old Christiani, Hesthenes had always stretched out his neck on the chance that perhaps this one might be the man. It was a thirst in which hatred and love were mixed together. His mother Helene had told him that the father who never came back was a Christianus. That Christianus had abandoned Helene and never returned, yet she had not resented him in the least. The only man who had truly loved her and cared for her, she said, was that wandering Christianus.

In fact, Hesthenes had been interested in old Tychicus from the very first time he met him aboard the ship Vesta. That was why, on the evening when they arrived in Ephesus, he had followed after him and trailed him all the way to the olive grove of Orga.

Patting his son's back, Tychicus said,

"To tell the truth, the moment I saw you... I recalled the old days in Corinth and thought perhaps it had been around the time you were born. I kept watching you carefully, and when I heard the name Helene I knew for certain. But I had determined to wait until you could fully understand the Christianus who did not come back...."

Tychicus released the son he had held in his arms and had him kneel. John poured the fresh water Polycarp had prepared over Hesthenes's head.

"Hesthenes, I baptize you in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit."

Leaving father and son behind, John once more began washing the feet of the beggars and old people.

Dirty feet, wounded feet, even feet from which pus was flowing, he washed them all. Each pair of feet carried within it countless pains and tears. They were feet that had borne grievous lives upon them and followed their owners through the world. To John they were all precious feet, as if he were looking upon the painful years of his own past. And these feet had now come walking toward the cross of Jesus.

Papias, attending him by pouring water into the basin, carefully opened his mouth.

"I too read the Gospel of Luke that you sent us, Apostle, and it was a truly excellent record."

John, washing the feet of the sick, nodded his head.

"Especially in this record, the many parables of Jesus not only have a literary structure of great excellence, they are also most valuable materials for supplementing the teaching of Jesus. The parable of the Good Samaritan, the parable of the fig tree, the parable of the wedding feast, the parable of the lost sheep, the parable of the prodigal son...."

"Jesus always spoke in parables using simple things close at hand. Trees and birds, farmers and widows, prostitutes and tax collectors.... Yet once those things came from the mouth of Jesus, they would all be changed into beautiful words."

"From this record of Luke's, it appears that all those moving parables were spoken in the period from after Jesus went up to Jerusalem for the Feast of Booths until the time He said not to keep the little children away, that is, in the interval when He met Bishop Ignatius. That is why those parables do not appear in the records of Mark or Matthew, who omitted what happened in that interval... and perhaps the incident of Lazarus took place in that interval as well...."

John still kept his mouth shut, so Papias once more steered the subject toward the parables.

"Among the parables I read newly this time, the parable of the rich man and Lazarus especially contained considerable religious mystery. The beggar Lazarus spent his whole life only begging at the rich man's gate and never did any good deed at all, so how could he enter paradise? And the rich man did no particular evil deed either, so why did he go to the place of torment...?"

John still said nothing as he washed the patients' feet. Papias poured new water into the basin again.

"And... Apostle."

"Another question?"

"When Jesus ate the Passover supper, why did He choose the house of Mark for the place? In Luke's record it says Jesus was entertained in the house of Lazarus in Bethany by the sisters Martha and Mary, and if He had raised their brother Lazarus again to life, should He not naturally have eaten the Passover supper in that house?"

"The other records do not say so, but in this new record of Luke's it is written that when Jesus sent men to find the place of the supper, He sent Peter and John. Why did Jesus send those two? And in Matthew's record it simply says briefly that they were to go to the house of a certain man in the city, but here He sent those two and said that in the city they would meet a man carrying a water jar, and they were to follow him inside and ask the master of the house where the supper room prepared for our teacher was. What was the reason Jesus especially chose Mark's house, why did He send those two men for it, and why did He create such a complicated procedure by putting forward a man carrying a water jar?"

"Moreover, Mark's house was a widow's house without a master, and Mark himself was still only a young boy. This also corresponds with the supper Jesus ate on the evening He arrived in Bethany. In the house of Simon the leper likewise, Simon the master of the house would not have been there because he was a leper, and only the rest of the family would have been there."

"Papias."

"Yes, Apostle."

"Papias has studied a great deal."

Papias, somewhat embarrassed, scratched the back of his head.

"No. I have only been comparing the records of the three men...."

"Papias, love is not something to analyze but something to understand, and truth is not something to dig out but something to guard."

Papias could ask no further. Silently he followed behind John with the water jar. It was then that from the entrance side of the theater one man shouted something, and the people were rising in a commotion. Many were running either toward the gymnasium or toward the civic square.

"...What could it be?"

When Papias said that, Polycarp too tilted his head.

"Could something have happened?"

Polycarp spoke while recalling the night when the news of Domitian's end had become known.

By nature the gymnasium and the civic square were places where many people gathered, so news from outside reached there quickly. That was why whenever something happened, people would run there.

Prochorus stood up and said,

"I will go and see."

With his solid frame springing up, Prochorus was already running down the theater steps.

Papias and Polycarp moved a little apart from John and whispered in low voices.

"Before the Apostle passes away, we must arrange all the Gospel records, and yet he will not open his mouth, so this is difficult."

"I too have asked him whenever I found an opening, and he always remains silent."

"In any case, whenever he speaks as he did today, we have no choice but to record it all. As I read it, Luke's record contained considerable questions. He did not state them directly, but by presenting countless indirect materials, he gives the impression of demanding answers from the Apostle John. I did not get to ask him all the way to the end, but concerning what happened at the Last Supper too, Luke recorded something distinctive."

"What do you mean?"

"At the supper table, Jesus seems to have pressed Peter very hard. That is to say... in the records of Mark and Matthew, Jesus says that tonight all of you will abandon Me, and when Peter says that even if all abandon the Lord, he will not, Jesus says again to him that before the rooster crows tonight, he will deny Me three times.... But in Luke's record...."

When John turned back, Papias quickly went and changed the water in the basin and then dropped back again.

"In Luke's record... the disciples were disputing even at the supper table over who among them was greatest, and after Jesus taught how authority in the kingdom of heaven differs from authority in this world, and said that the one who is great in the kingdom of heaven must become one who serves, then immediately He pressed Peter. He called Peter's name twice, then said that Satan had already set out to take them, but that Jesus Himself had prayed for Peter so that his faith would not fail. And afterward, when he returned, he was to strengthen his brothers.... And after that came Peter's boast and the prophecy that he would deny Him three times."

"I too... actually had a strange thought when I read that passage. It did not seem to me that it was merely a warning given because of expectations regarding the chief disciple."

Now whispering and murmuring could be seen even among the people inside the theater.

Papias and Polycarp paused their speech for a moment and looked down toward the lower part of the theater. They could see Prochorus. He was moving his feet quickly and running up the steps. As soon as he came near enough, Papias gave him no chance even to catch his breath and asked,

"What has happened?"

Though he was still at some distance, Prochorus's breathless voice flew to them.

"They say Emperor Nerva is dead. Emperor Nerva has died, and General Marcus Ulpius Trajanus, who had been princeps of the Senate, has become emperor as expected."

"Trajanus...."

"That is right. They say he is from Hispania, and before becoming princeps of the Senate he had been governor of Germania."

At Prochorus's report, troubled expressions began appearing on the faces of the Christiani. At last the persecution against the Christiani had somewhat softened, and they had rejoiced that a new age was opening because an emperor of senatorial background had come to the throne. Yet along with the news that Nerva's health was poor, word had come that Trajanus, a man from the military, had been named successor, and now at last he had indeed ascended the throne.

If what the military desired was strong rule over the provinces, and now an emperor had come from the military itself, then what the empire's policy toward the Christiani would become from here on was more obvious than fire before one's eyes.

As John heard the report from Prochorus behind him, he rested his hands for a moment as if sinking into thought, then at once resumed washing feet. The young men looked at one another and exchanged glances. It was as though their eyes were once again filling with a new resolve, as if they already sensed beforehand the wind that the change in Rome would drive down upon Ephesus.

As though confirming that premonition, Prochorus continued with his next report.

"Emperor Trajanus has ordered that the former temple of Domitian be changed into the temple of Vespasianus... and has commanded the construction opposite it of a fountain bearing his own name, the Fountain of Trajanus."

The hands of John, who was washing feet, paused again for a moment. It was because of the news that a temple venerating Vespasianus as a god would arise in this Ephesus. For a moment he clenched his jaw and then began drying with a cloth the feet of the old man whose washing he had finished.

Polycarp looked at Papias and whispered,

"When I saw Domitian die, I thought perhaps the return of Christ was drawing near.... It seems the return must be delayed again."

"It may still be too early to judge Trajanus."

"Did they not say he commanded a fountain to be built in his own name?"

"Still, a fountain is better than a temple...."

Polycarp nodded his head and murmured,

"As the Apostle said... the time of God leaps beyond the years, so we cannot measure the time of that return either. The Lord said all would be fulfilled before this generation passed away, but what time and season that generation points to is also something we do not know...."

Then Prochorus, who was listening beside them, cut in.

"In any case, if that is so... it seems there may also be a change for Quaestor Andronicus."

Hesthenes, who was sitting beside Tychicus listening to their words, was nodding his head. Polycarp looked at him and asked,

"Quaestor Andronicus is still submitting petitions for the dispatch of legionary forces, is he not?"

"That is so. Because the quaestor had liked Drusiana from his boyhood, he rejected proposals that came from all the nobles of Rome and married her. He will never renounce Drusiana. All the more because when the quaestor led a cavalry cohort into Cappadocia in the autumn of the year before last, he learned that the chief of those bandits was Callimachus, who had come from Ephesus, so he will never abandon the matter."

Looking at the bent back of John, who was washing the feet of an old grandmother, Polycarp said,

"Did Quaestor Andronicus say so?"

"He did. He said that the chief of the bandits who carried off Drusiana was none other than the orphan Callimachus raised in the house of Tyrannus, and that he too had loved Drusiana deeply even before her marriage. Therefore... because this affair is a struggle between two men over one woman, it seems that whenever the day comes, the clash will be inevitable. As the quaestor left for Rome, he told me... that he would never return empty-handed."

Tears were now gathering in John's eyes as he washed the old woman's feet. Struggling not to let them fall, John kept looking down at the small feet in his hands. They were terribly thin and shriveled feet. Then suddenly a tear fell upon the back of John's hand. John was startled when he saw the tear on the back of his hand, for he had been striving not to let his own tears fall. Perhaps he had become so old that his eyes had lost the power to hold back tears.

Another tear fell.

John was startled again and lifted his head. An old woman with a terribly shrunken face was there. Thick streams of tears were flowing down from her eyes, darkened as though seized by a deep sickness. The old woman's lips trembled, and then a thin voice came out like a groan.

"John...."

At that John's blazing eyes grew wider and wider. A taut tension swelled until it was enough to fill the whole theater, and at last John cried out in a loud voice,

"Sophia!"

So unexpected a thing it was. No one had imagined that there might be a woman whom the Apostle John would greet so gladly that he would run and embrace her. The old woman's body was skeletal as though only bones remained, and filthy rags hung upon her. It seemed that the only clean place on her body was that little foot which John had just washed.

John held the old woman in his arms and did not know what to do.

Yet within the changed and shrunken face of the old woman, John was struggling to find the beautiful figure he had once known. The figure he had met on the shore of Tiberias, the figure that had appeared at the wedding feast of Cana.... It was a fateful gesture that had set the whole life of one man ablaze with the obsession of revenge. Because of the sorrow of losing her, John of Gischala entered the Zealot party, while John himself left home seeking another John who had appeared in the wilderness of Judea, and so met Jesus of Nazareth. Even while following Jesus of Nazareth, John was always sharpening the knife of revenge and aiming at Vespasianus. Thus, after laying down Mary's burden, John again met and attacked Vespasianus at Antioch, when Vespasianus had marched out to suppress the Jewish rebels. By that time John was already fifty-eight years old.

Truly the life of the man called John had been a life directed toward Sophia. The fact that he had lived unmarried and celibate until old age was not because he had become a disciple of Jesus, nor because, like Paul, he had wished to devote his whole life to the work of Christ. He had lived only for Sophia, and he had lived for revenge, and so there had never been time for his eyes to turn toward any woman other than Sophia.

And now Sophia was in John's arms. She was in his arms now as a life all but destroyed, with only the shell remaining. He grasped and shook the branch-like body of the old woman in his arms with aching tenderness.

"Sophia, speak. What happened?"

"John...."

"Sophia...."

"Already... seventy years have passed."

"Yes. Since we met there on the shore of Tiberias, that many years have indeed passed. Sophia, what happened? How could it be that we met again? Since you were carried off by Vespasianus's cavalry, what became of you?"

The old woman's head fell weakly backward. She was gasping as though her breath might fail at any moment.

"I was dragged away by the cavalry... and suffered every indignity a woman can suffer. Then I was released, but with a body like that I could never return home. I wandered here and there weeping. After that I heard that John of Gischala had entered the Zealot party... and I heard that you had returned from Judea. I followed you in Capernaum and slipped into the wedding feast at Cana, but... for some reason, even though your eyes met mine, you pretended not to know me. Only then did I realize that Sophia's life had come to its end, and I left that place."

"Sophia...."

"Worse still, something strange began to happen in my body. A cursed seed was growing within me. And when my time was fulfilled, I bore a daughter. From that time my revenge began...."

"Revenge...."

"Yes. Against Vespasianus, who trampled me down, and against all the gods who cursed me, I resolved upon revenge."

The old woman's eyes suddenly seemed to be staring into the air and breathing out a blue flame.

"While I was sharpening the blade of revenge... Vespasianus was promoted from quaestor to aedile and then even to praetor, and he received medals of victory after conquering Germania and Britannia...."

The old woman kept having to stop speaking and steady her breathing, because it seemed her breath might fail at any moment.

"But I... waited until my daughter was twenty-three years old.... My daughter, honed by the blade of revenge, became a beauty unmatched beneath heaven... and I used my influence among Vespasianus's close attendants and sent the girl into that household as a maidservant.... As I had planned, Vespasianus fell into the snare of her beauty. Vespasianus, who at forty-two was enjoying the very height of life, violated my daughter like a beast and made her bear a child...."

Truly it was a terrible revenge. As she herself said, it was a horrifying revenge directed not only against Vespasianus but also against all the gods, the cursed daughter, and even herself.

"That son of shame was Domitian.... When my daughter gave birth to Domitian at Vespasianus's country villa, I immediately sent men out and began spreading the rumor that Vespasianus had lain with his own daughter and gotten a child by her. That rumor soon reached his wife Domitilla. When she confronted him with it... he ran to the villa and questioned my daughter about her origin. She answered that her mother was a woman named Sophia from Nicopolis... and that she had once lived in Tiberias. But more than anything else, what astonished Vespasianus was the shell necklace my daughter had hung around the infant's neck.... Vespasianus ran out of the villa like a madman, and when my daughter learned the circumstances, she too fled that place.... In the end Domitian was entered into the household as a child born of Domitilla, but... as the rumor kept spreading, Vespasianus had no choice but to surrender even the glorious office of consul suffectus that he had received that year and retire until the rumor died down...."

The terrible resolve of one woman was making John tremble. This pitiable woman had accomplished that revenge which John himself and John of Gischala had not been able to accomplish.

"But my revenge did not end there. In the year my daughter, who had been living as a prostitute in Corinth, died, Vespasianus rose to the throne of Rome.... I again began leaking rumors around Domitian.... Domitian was not the child of Domitilla... one day Vespasianus would remove that son.... Ten years later, that persistent curse succeeded. Domitian, at twenty-eight years of age, was at last deeply diseased with suspicion, and in the end he poisoned the father who had gone to recuperate at the Sabine villa.... The hand of Domitian reached again, two years later, even to poisoning his brother Emperor Titus... and I began stretching my hand once more toward his wife Domitilla. Domitian is a madman born from incest between father and daughter... he killed his father and his brother, and next he will kill his wife.... In the end that Domitian died by the hand of his own wife Domitilla. Thus my long, long revenge came to an end...."

John's whole body was trembling like the leaves of an aspen. A woman who had lived through such terrible years was there in his arms.

"What... what was the name of that daughter of yours?"

"I named her Helene... Helene, after Helen the beauty who destroyed Troy...."

John turned his head and looked behind him. Hesthenes had run forward toward him, and old Tychicus too had come nearer. But old Sophia's breath was already giving way.

"John... today... in the whole long and rough course of my life... this has been the happiest day.... With your own hands... you washed my feet.... For this one day... with this one day alone... my life has been worth living.... I give thanks to your God... John... pray for me...."

Holding the old woman's dry body in his arms, John opened his mouth.

"Lord... I entrust this woman into Your hands...."

# Empire and Heaven (II)

## Chapter 5. A Time to Lose and a Time to Repay

The steward Lucius entered the drawing room and cautiously looked at Andronicus, who was seated facing the wall. Because this mansion of Lateranus, having lost its mistress, had lain sunk in heavy silence for nearly two years already, he always had to deal carefully with his master.

During those two years, Quaestor Marius Andronicus had been like a man out of his mind. At times he prowled through the garden like an enraged lion; at other times he shut himself up for hours sitting in the drawing room facing the wall; then suddenly he would mount a horse and gallop to the imperial palace. Andronicus, who had been famed for being cool-headed beyond his years, had begun to grow unrecognizably rough, and even his speech had become violent, so that he would freely pour abuse upon the servants. Whenever he sat down or stood up, all the stewards and servants of the house fluttered about in helpless confusion.

Lucius composed himself for quite some time before at last opening his mouth carefully.

"...Quaestor."

Andronicus was still sitting facing the wall in silence. The steward, trained through long years in tact and patience, merely stood still and waited for a response from his master. Indeed, only after a long while, just as he had expected, a low voice came from that direction.

"What is it?"

"A message has come from His Majesty the Emperor."

"What?"

Andronicus sprang to his feet like a man scorched by fire.

"The message says you are to come at once to the imperial palace."

Flames leapt into Andronicus's eyes.

"Very well. Bring me my uniform."

Truly his movements were swift, as befitted one from the cavalry. He put on the splendid dress of a cavalry officer over a light tunic, leapt onto his horse, and before long had already left the mansion and was racing like an arrow toward the Palatine. It had been two years spent waiting for a single day as though for a thousand years. Those two years had truly been a living hell for Andronicus. Whenever he closed his eyes, the image of his wife held in Callimachus's arms rose before him, and whenever he opened them, the furniture his wife had touched and the vases his wife had touched stabbed his heart like daggers.

Andronicus was even afraid to sleep, because he was afraid of seeing in dreams the image of his wife held in Callimachus's arms. Night after night he had to be tormented by such dreadful dreams. Sometimes that image of his wife was weeping, and at other times it was smiling. And sometimes... she was naked and rolling together with Callimachus, panting.

But more than anything else, what pained Andronicus was his own pride, trampled so miserably underfoot. He was the rising star of the Roman Empire that ruled the whole world. All the nobles of Rome had envied him, and all the maidens of Rome had admired him. Yet now that Quaestor Marius Andronicus had had his wife stolen away by no more than a bandit. And when that Quaestor Andronicus had gone to recover his wife, he had lost one cavalry cohort, suffered total defeat, and been driven back.

It seemed to him that all the citizens of Rome were looking at him and laughing. Even if someone merely looked at him, he would flare up. Once he even struck mercilessly across the face a little child who had been looking at him in the street, and the child's mother protested. He had believed that the child was laughing at him.

It was not only the citizens of Rome. What enraged him even more was the thought that Callimachus must be laughing at him. The image of Callimachus roaring with laughter was always tormenting Andronicus. Worse still, over that laughing face he kept hearing even the laughter of Drusiana. Andronicus had become a complete fool before them.

There was something else that had tormented Andronicus all that time. It was what Callimachus had said to Drusiana in the garden of the house of Tyrannus.

-Drusiana, we have grown up together since we were children, and I have always been beside you. I know even what your sleeping habits are like, what foods you like, what flowers you like, and what music you love... I know it all.

Only now had those words become truly painful to Andronicus. After bringing Drusiana to Rome, Andronicus had in truth not been a faithful husband. Leaving his wife alone in a foreign place in the great mansion of Lateranus, Andronicus himself had been so overwhelmed by government work that came pouring in upon him that he had had no time at all to pay attention to her. In the end, Drusiana had found it so hard to endure the empty hours by herself that she had even had to attend medical school and study.

Only after losing Drusiana did Andronicus realize his own fault. In the loneliness of a foreign place, perhaps Drusiana had regretted her mistaken marriage. She may have thought of Callimachus, who had grown up with her from childhood and knew even what her sleeping habits were like, what foods she liked, what flowers, what music she loved, and may have wept alone.

If that was so, perhaps Drusiana had by now been reunited with Callimachus, returned to their old affection, and become happy again. Andronicus even remembered what Drusiana herself had answered to Callimachus then.

-Callicus, I know it all. I know how empty it would feel without Callimachus. I know it all. But, but....

But what did that "but" mean? Drusiana must surely have felt empty. Neither the power nor the wealth of Andronicus could have filled the emptiness in her heart. That was why his pride was always seething. He could only reproach himself, asking how a man who could not even fill the emptiness of one woman's heart could ever become a leader of the Roman Empire. Perhaps even now Drusiana was in Callimachus's arms, laughing at worthless Andronicus.

All those thoughts were driving Andronicus mad. At last he had begun to hate and despise everything in the world. That had even appeared in the form of dissatisfaction toward Emperor Nerva.

From Andronicus's point of view, Emperor Nerva's policy of liberalization was laughable. If oppression meant binding the people fast in a spider's web of information so that they could not move, then loosening everything merely to curry favor with the people was nothing but cowardice. At least such, to him, was Nerva. In everything he respected the opinion of the Senate, watched the faces of the nobles, and was easily swayed by the complaints of the people, all in order to be evaluated as a generous ruler.

Moreover, when his petition for troops to suppress the bandits was finally ignored, Andronicus criticized the emperor and openly expressed dissatisfaction. Because Andronicus was a core official of the Roman government and, moreover, from the military, he was in a position to exert considerable pressure upon the emperor. Behind him stood many high officials who had come from the cavalry.

At last Marius Andronicus, supported by the military force behind him, was elected to the Senate. Truly, only three years after becoming quaestor and nine years after entering the cavalry, he had stepped into the empire's highest deliberative organ of state as a quaestor of only twenty-six.

In the Senate, Andronicus in effect acted as the spokesman for the military interest. Whenever the assembly met, he criticized Emperor Nerva's weak policies and attacked him for slighting men of military background. He also possessed considerable information concerning rebellions and the threat of bandits arising in the provinces here and there, and using that as a basis he challenged the countermeasures of Nerva's government.

Thus Andronicus was crossing swords over every matter with Senator Ovidius, who defended Emperor Nerva. Senator Ovidius drove against Andronicus, saying that his insistence on harsh measures was merely reckless bravado scheming to revive oppressive rule, while Andronicus in turn shot back that his opponent was a theorist who knew nothing of the very foundations of provincial administration.

Meanwhile the years kept passing. Because of his burning impatience, even his complexion had darkened. Every night he dreamed of Drusiana being toyed with by Callimachus, and each time he writhed as if losing his mind. Perhaps by now Drusiana had already borne Callimachus's child.

Not long after Drusiana came to Rome, she had become pregnant. But one day, while she had begun attending medical school because she could no longer endure her emptiness, she was attacked on the way home with only one maid in attendance. Of course the ruffians did not know that she was the wife of the much-renowned Quaestor Andronicus, but in any case, because of that assault Drusiana miscarried their first child.

After that she had not conceived again, and then after returning to Ephesus she had been abducted. Therefore, as each day passed, the thought that she might have borne Callimachus's child became more unbearable to Andronicus.

The horse carrying Andronicus was already racing around between the Colosseum and the statue of Apollo and driving toward the Palatine Hill.

Now Emperor Trajanus, who had succeeded Nerva upon the throne as a man from the military, had sent for Andronicus. In one sense it could be said that Emperor Trajanus had been connected with Andronicus even before his accession. Nerva, because of the continuing pressure of the military, had designated General Trajanus, a man from the military, as his successor, and at the head of that military pressure had stood none other than Quaestor Marius Andronicus.

Yet once Trajanus, the representative of the military interest and princeps of the Senate, had ascended the throne, Ovidius, the leading figure of the reform party, began pressing Andronicus again by an entirely different method.

He argued that rather than dispatching troops to solve external problems, the more urgent task was to restore the spirit of the people, who had grown stagnant. Therefore he insisted that the noble class and high officials should be required to live austerely, that salary increases should be restrained, and that by cutting an enormous portion of the budget, better distribution should be made to the urban poor.

But the problem came next. The emperors had each increased the number of holidays in order to curry favor with the people, yet because the free entertainments of the arena were decreasing, the common people, unable to obtain money for amusement, were growing dissatisfied. After pointing out that the decline of arena spectacles was due to the fact that since Emperor Nerva the arrest of Christiani had markedly decreased, Ovidius then argued that gladiators should be trained within the army.

And Ovidius drove the nail home. The barbarous practice of arresting decent Roman citizens under suspicion of being Christiani and exhibiting them in the arena must never be allowed to return. He made this point by citing the cases of Consul Glabrio and Consul Clemens, who had been sacrificed again in the age of Domitian, and of the many senators besides them. He said there must never again be a reign of terror that arrests people on rumor alone. And there were still, he said, any number of high officials even now of whom rumors circulated that they themselves, or their wives, were Christiani.

That was hardly different from bringing up the very matter of Marius Andronicus himself. In truth Andronicus had, by a crafty method, explained the circumstances of his marriage to Domitian and secured his understanding beforehand, yet such rumors had continued to circulate among the nobles. It was also because his wife Drusiana had disliked attending the decadent banquets of the aristocracy, and so she had become a subject for their gossip.

In any case, by this Ovidius had achieved the effect of killing three birds with one stone. First, by bringing up the rumor concerning Andronicus, he struck at the spearhead of the military interest; at the same time he drove a wedge into the military, which longed for the formidable power of the age of information politics; and by counterattacking them, telling them rather to go and train gladiators, he sought to cut down the military's arrogance.

Trajanus himself, however, exercised outstanding political skill. He always respected the opinion of the Senate and maintained friendly relations even with Senator Ovidius. Therefore Andronicus's petition for a military expedition had still not received approval for more than five months.

Andronicus arrived at the imperial palace, dismounted, received the salute of the guards, and strode toward the emperor's office.

"Oh, Marius, thank you for coming."

Emperor Trajanus, who had been seated at his desk going through a mountain of documents, sprang to his feet and welcomed Andronicus. Because the emperor had called him by his praenomen, that is, his first name, which was used only between the close, Andronicus was deeply flustered. The emperor put an arm around Andronicus's shoulder as an elder brother might to a younger and urged him to sit.

When Andronicus sat down, the emperor also leaned back comfortably in the chair opposite him. It made a favorable contrast with Emperor Domitian, who had always tried not to show a relaxed posture before his subordinates.

"Marius... when I look at you, it seems to me I am looking at the fresh-rising sun of Rome. It truly seems that the uniform becomes you."

"You praise me too highly."

Though seated, Andronicus answered while straightening his posture. Unlike the days of Domitian, he judged that before a circuitous politician such as Trajanus, it was advantageous to appear ingenuous. Andronicus understood exactly that even if the other man displayed a relaxed posture, should he imitate it, he would at once fail.

When Andronicus set his expression in seriousness, Trajanus's face too returned to gravity.

"Marius... I have examined carefully from the beginning the petitions for troop deployment that you have submitted."

"Thank you, Your Majesty."

"What do you think of the Senate's opposition to the expedition?"

Andronicus's thoughts were racing swiftly through his mind. However displeasing the Senate's opposition was, he could not complain of it. The emperor before his eyes was also respecting their opinion.

Andronicus turned the subject away from the Senate and toward the emperor.

"I believe that Your Majesty's measure of postponing the expedition in order to avoid abrupt change was appropriate."

Andronicus used the word postponing, not restraining. And Andronicus did not miss the brief smile that crossed the emperor's face, as if that word had pleased him.

"What is your opinion regarding frontier defense?"

"Your Majesty, I believe that what matters above all in a dominant state such as Rome is the preservation of the present order. Yet the preservation of the present order is not something accomplished by remaining still, but by continual expansion. Territory, wealth, and national strength, if left as they are, gradually shrink. It is because all possessions undergo what is called natural attrition."

Again a brief smile crossed Trajanus's face. He nodded his head once or twice, then suddenly looked straight at Andronicus.

"Two years ago you advanced as far as Cappadocia leading one cavalry cohort. Why were you annihilated?"

This was truly a sudden strike from the emperor.

"It was my mistake to calculate that, because Cappadocia is a place where wandering frontier peoples gather, a disorderly rabble of bandits could easily be handled with one cavalry cohort. Moreover, the bandits of Korama also possessed mounted forces like the Roman cavalry, and they were not only numerous, but were hiding on a large scale within caves, so it was extremely difficult to carry out the suppression operation."

The fact that the bandits had been stationed inside caves had been the fatal cause of Andronicus's defeat. He already had the experience of once losing his way in the cave of Coresus in Ephesus. And after that, even hearing the word cave was dreadful to him, and the memory of that cave was something he hated even to think about.

Though he had rushed all the way to Cappadocia leading a cavalry cohort in fury over his stolen wife, the moment he saw the immense complex of caves where they were stationed, his spirit had already been broken. He tried the use of fire, and tried surprise attacks, to lure the bandits out from the caves, but all in vain. Rather, he was drawn into their trap, advanced into rough ground, suffered a crushing defeat, and barely escaped with his life.

Now Trajanus had already taken the upper hand in the conversation by first reminding him of the fact of his earlier defeat.

"Even if, as you say, we move legionary forces... does the fact that the enemy are stationed within caves not remain unchanged?"

"That is true. Therefore, if Your Majesty grants permission for the expedition this time, I intend to fight them on open ground."

"On open ground...? Do you mean to lure them out? Would they be so easily drawn out?"

"I have one method."

"So you have a way to draw them out?"

"Yes."

Without asking further, Trajanus turned the subject.

"The reason I called you today... was to speak with you of something that cannot be said in the Senate. As you yourself said just now, what the Roman Empire most needs is the preservation of the present order, and that preservation means continual expansion. And yet the stiff-necked pacifists of the Senate continue to demand restrictions on military action, and instead ask for greater power to the assembly and broader autonomy for the provinces...."

Andronicus was still listening to the emperor with erect posture.

Trajanus was indeed a politician of high cunning. He had not asked even once about Andronicus's abducted wife, nor tried to ensnare him with such matters. He had merely reminded him of the defeat of two years earlier as a warning, and now was calmly speaking of something altogether different from what he said in the Senate.

"The strengthening of provincial authority... that is, the strengthening of the power of provincial governors and magistrates, has in actual fact brought about corruption among provincial officials and, contrary to its original intention, aroused resentment among the provincial peoples."

"That is so, Your Majesty."

"That is why, while strengthening the authority of the provinces, I have also been dispatching curators, that is, overseers, who can help governors contribute more efficiently to provincial development."

"I am aware of it, Your Majesty. I understand that the curators are charged with overseeing construction projects for the expansion of livelihoods and the development of regional economies, low-interest loans to farmers, and works of relief for the poor."

"You understand it well. Not only that, but in the case of curators in the provinces, especially in frontier provinces, I am planning to give immediate authority over military action, so they may deal with provocations on the border. What is your opinion?"

"It is a righteous word. If one must come all the way to Rome and receive approval in order to decide military action on the frontier, then while that decision is being made the borders of Rome will be battered into ruin."

Trajanus sprang to his feet, clasped his hands behind his back, and began pacing around the room. Andronicus rose with him and assumed a motionless stance. As Trajanus walked about the room, he opened his mouth.

"Therefore I...."

Trajanus's face grew serious again.

"I have made one method by mixing the opinion of the Senate with my own thinking."

Trajanus closed his eyes for a moment as though in thought, then spoke.

"Marius... are you aware that Governor Lycomedes of Ephesus has petitioned to resign?"

"Governor Lycomedes, Your Majesty?"

Andronicus asked that back in great surprise. Lycomedes had been a general from the tribunes, a senator, and a magistrate. Now he stood in a position from which even the consulship might be within sight. Yet he had petitioned to resign. Whatever the cause might have been, Andronicus could not ask the emperor.

"So you did not know. Whatever reason he may have had for wanting to resign, I had no choice but to appoint a successor. This very morning I appointed Praetor Marcus Ovidius governor of Ephesus and ordered him to depart for the province."

That meant none other than Ovidius the senator, who time after time opposed Andronicus in the Senate, that very old man. But Andronicus at once understood the emperor's intention. Ovidius, who had been a key man supporting the former emperor Nerva and who still led opinion in the Senate by insisting only on liberalization, could not but be a deeply inconvenient presence for Trajanus, who sought to lead the political order under the banner of consensual rule. Moreover, to appoint that Ovidius to Ephesus under the pretext of giving him the chance to exercise the strengthening of provincial administration which he himself advocated was, in any man's eyes, a promotion with a proper justification. Andronicus did not hesitate to say,

"Your Majesty, it is a wise choice."

Trajanus nodded again two or three times, took a few steps, and turned back once more toward Andronicus.

"But Marius... if you were sending Marcus Ovidius to a province, would you send him with wings attached, telling him to spread his intentions to the fullest?"

"Marius Andronicus."

At last the emperor, fixing his eyes on Andronicus, called him with a stern face.

"Yes, Your Majesty."

"Today I appoint you, Marius Andronicus, as curator, and at the same time assign you under Governor Ovidius of Ephesus."

The face of Andronicus, who had been famed for coolness, flushed at once. Since the office of curator had been strengthened under Emperor Trajanus, it stood on the same rank as an aedile. Vespasianus himself, who had set a record of promotion, had won that office at the age of twenty-nine; now Andronicus had seized it at only twenty-six. Once he passed through the next stage, the praetorship, he would be looking straight toward the consulship, the highest governing office in the Roman Empire. Andronicus said in a trembling voice,

"Your Majesty, I will be loyal with my very life."

"Marius, I trust you have fully understood what work you must do when you go to Ephesus. As difficult as the work you must do is, I too give you authority with respect to the work you wish to do. As you know, there are three legions and three auxiliary cohort forces stationed in Asia Minor. I will place the authority to request the deployment of those three legions and three auxiliary cohort forces in the hands of the curator stationed at Ephesus."

"Thank you, Your Majesty."

"Tonight there will be a farewell banquet hosted by the Senate for Praetor Ovidius and Curator Andronicus. Attend the banquet, then leave for the province immediately tomorrow."

"I shall carry out Your command."

Andronicus rendered a spirited military salute, turned, and was about to leave the emperor's office. Suddenly the emperor's voice came from behind him.

"Marius."

He turned hurriedly and looked at the emperor.

"Yes, Your Majesty."

"What do you think... of the Christiani?"

"Yes...?"

"Of the Christiani...."

Suddenly darkness was rushing over Andronicus's eyes.

"I... I have not thought deeply about it...."

"Very well. Do not be late for the banquet."

"I understand, Your Majesty."

He rendered one more military salute and came out of the place. Cold sweat was running down his forehead.

Even while riding home, Andronicus kept thinking about that final question from the emperor. There was never any idle speech in the emperor's questions. He seemed to say whatever came, and yet he was a man who never wasted words. Such a man had deliberately called back Andronicus as he was leaving and asked what he thought of the Christiani.

Of course, at any other time, it would not have been a difficult question. The Andronicus of his ordinary days could have answered calmly enough.

-The Christiani are not committing any clear violation of the law, but because they do not acknowledge the gods of Rome and take a negative attitude toward the order of the existing world, there is a danger they may develop into a collegia, that is, secret societies, which deny the political system of Rome. Therefore I believe the Roman Empire must continue to watch and control the movements of the Christiani.

That was the model answer Andronicus was supposed to give before the emperor. Yet the moment he suddenly received the emperor's question, darkness had rushed over his eyes, and he had hesitated.

It had been the same when he debated with Praetor Ovidius in the Senate. When Ovidius said that even among current high officials there were many of whom rumor said that they themselves, or their wives, were Christiani, his tongue had suddenly failed him. Certainly there had been a barb in Ovidius's words. There had been no need, when saying such a thing, to mention both the man himself and his wife, and yet he seemed especially to emphasize the word wife. In any case, it was a remark that applied directly to Andronicus. His wife Drusiana had originally been a Christianus, and Andronicus himself not only had been baptized by John, a direct disciple of Jesus of Nazareth, but had also celebrated his wedding by their rite and even held a banquet in his house to which the Christiani were invited. Moreover, the Christiani who attended his wedding were all important leaders of the churches of Achaia and Asia Minor.

Yet even if all of that was true, Andronicus could have said, just as he had explained to Domitian, that his participation had been strategic, undertaken in order to learn their internal information or for some political negotiation with them. Yet this time he had not done so.

On the ship coming to Rome after the wedding in Ephesus, and on the Appian Way from the port of Puteoli to Rome, Drusiana had spoken continuously to Andronicus about Jesus of Nazareth. It had truly been a long and fascinating story, and because it had been the beginning of their marriage, everything had sounded to Andronicus simply like a beautiful tale.

Starting from the story of creation in the Jewish scriptures, Drusiana explained with great care the story of how the first human beings sinned, and how in order to save mankind, who must after all walk their own way, God had from the beginning planned the sending of the Savior.

Why the stories in the Jewish scriptures were not matters pertaining only to the Jews, she explained in detail; and she explained also why the Jews had been chosen and singled out by God, and why Jerusalem had become the center of the history of the world.

Like a daughter of the scholarly house of Tyrannus, she was very intelligent and possessed excellent powers of expression, so that by the time they came near Rome, Andronicus too had received knowledge scarcely inferior to that of any Christianus.

He heard explanations about the many prophets who had appeared in Jewish history, about the numerous prophecies concerning the Savior, and even about the end of mankind. Drusiana also told him almost everything about the mysterious story of how Jesus of Nazareth had come into this world, and about the miracles and sermons He had performed during His life.

Drusiana's fervent evangelism continued even after they arrived in Rome and set up their new household, and Andronicus would fall asleep listening to her stories of Jesus as a little child falls asleep listening to his mother's lullaby.

Strangely, there had been freshness in her stories. And it was all the more vivid because of several Christiani whom Andronicus himself had seen.

There was the apostle John, the supreme leader of them all, who had gone calmly along the road of escort with Andronicus; there was the way he had spoken quietly before Domitian as though instructing a child; there were John's disciples whom he had met; old Tychicus; Nereus and Achilleus, fellow cavalrymen of the old days; Leodora and Euphrosyna, who had been friends of Lady Domitilla; and the Christiani of the Roman church who performed good works at the risk of their lives. Seeing them, he had felt as though his eyes were being opened toward a new world he had not known.

But among them all, the one who impressed him more than anyone was Lady Domitilla, wife of Consul Clemens.

She had said that there were many things she might do for the church at Rome, but among them she wished to choose the work that only Domitilla herself could do. The work that only she could do was to make known before the whole world that even the wife of a consul was a Christianus, and by being martyred to give courage to the many brothers of the Roman church.

That declaration of hers, that whether it meant martyrdom or something else she wished to choose the work that only she could do, had been deeply shocking to Andronicus. He had thought of his own case. Marius Andronicus, twenty-six-year-old senator and quaestor, a high official from the cavalry. However record-breaking his promotion might have been, in the end the work he did was work that could be replaced by anyone at any time. The same was true of aediles and magistrates, and even of consuls and emperors. Vespasianus had died, Titus had died, and now Domitian had died, and Nerva had died, and yet Rome was being maintained without any change.

The fact that the present I could be replaced by anyone... this could not but be a shock to Andronicus.

And the shock of that time had continued until now. It had already been two years since Drusiana was abducted... and in that time nothing had changed. Certainly Andronicus was Drusiana's husband. Yet that was only a role anyone at all could perform. That was why perhaps no word had come from Drusiana.

That was Andronicus's loneliness. To Drusiana, and to the Roman government as well, Marius was no more than such a being as might at any time be replaced by someone else. Such things kept causing him to take interest in Jesus of Nazareth and those around Him. Jesus of Nazareth and His disciples had certainly been people who did works that had to be done by themselves, works that could not be done by any other.

On the Appian Way outside the walls of Rome, Peter had seen a vision of Jesus going toward Rome.

-Lord, where are You going?

When he heard Jesus answer that if Peter was leaving Rome, then He would go into Rome again and be crucified, Peter hastily turned back into the city and was hung upside down upon a cross. And it was precisely the thought of Peter that had caused Andronicus to hesitate before Senator Ovidius in the Senate, and before Emperor Trajanus.

When Jesus was eating the Last Supper together with His disciples, He said that the disciples would abandon Him. Then Peter answered that even if he lost his life he would not abandon his Teacher, but Jesus said to him again that before the rooster crowed that night, he would deny Him three times.

That night Jesus was arrested, and Peter slipped into the house of the high priest Caiaphas to see what would become of his Teacher, when he was questioned by a maid of that house. Was not he also one of the disciples who had followed Jesus? Peter feigned ignorance and answered that he did not know Him, but when others also kept asking the same question, he had no choice but to insist repeatedly that he did not know Him. It was said that when he denied Him three times, the rooster crowed, and he remembered his Teacher's words and wept bitterly.

That story of Peter was sealing Andronicus's lips. If, as they said, Jesus was the incarnation of truth, then to say that one did not know Him was no different from denying truth itself, and to live while denying truth was in the end no different from death.

Of course, Andronicus still had no faith in Jesus. But he had one experience. It was what had happened when he lost his way in the cave of Coresus. Wandering lost within the cave and feeling his own powerlessness for the first time in his life, he had cried out into the dark void.

-Jesus, carpenter of Nazareth, did You give flesh and blood even to me? If You truly rose again, come to me! I am lonely now!

Crying out thus, Andronicus had lost consciousness. And within that fading consciousness he had heard a voice.

-Marius... Marius!

Perhaps it had been the voices of the people who were searching for Andronicus. But in any case, it had been a very striking experience for him. Yet Andronicus's interest in Jesus of Nazareth and the Christiani would collapse whenever he thought of Callimachus. From what he had discovered, Callimachus too was a disciple of the Apostle John. Above all, Andronicus hated the very fact that Drusiana had been a Christianus before him, and that with her Callimachus too had been a Christianus. It made him feel that they were bound together by the string called Christiani, while he alone stood apart.

And if Callimachus's ungentlemanly way of resolving his need for a woman by a method such as outright abduction came from something Christ-like, then Andronicus could never agree with it.

In any case, Andronicus decided not to think about whether he had answered the emperor well or badly today. At last, after two years, he had obtained the longed-for expedition. Friction with the newly appointed Governor Marcus Ovidius was to be expected, but once he himself went out to the province, his thinking too would have to change, and Ovidius would know what the authority of a curator meant, even if Andronicus went under him.

When he saw his own mansion of Lateranus in the distance, Andronicus again could not help sinking into melancholy. Without Drusiana, the mansion was desolate like a haunted house, and he hated to go into it. Therefore he would usually go from banquet hall to banquet hall in the evenings, then return only late at night after getting drunk.

(If Drusiana were here now, how pleased she would be....)

It was a promotion three years faster even than Vespasianus, and on top of that he was being appointed not merely governor, but curator holding greater practical power, to his hometown Ephesus. It was a triumphant return in glory. If only Callimachus did not exist, and if only Drusiana had not been abducted, this posting itself could not but have been a blessed one.

(To Drusiana, and to John too, who has reportedly already been released and gone to Ephesus, this would have been good news....)

The new governor Ovidius was a moderate man opposed to oppressive rule, and Andronicus himself, however formal it may have been, was a man baptized by John and the husband of Drusiana the Christianus, so for the Christiani of Ephesus this could not but have been welcome news.

As he rode his horse toward the house, he looked up at the sky.

(Jesus of Nazareth... is this also something You plan? Why is it that You do not give peace to those who believe in You, but instead plan tribulation?)

Such things were precisely what made Andronicus doubtful of their faith. Though they suffered and were persecuted so much, surely they must always have prayed to their God whom they said was almighty. And yet their sufferings never came to an end. Just as gods like Rome's Vesta or Jupiter were no more than handsome stone images to the eye, perhaps their God too was one who gave nothing more than psychological comfort.

Lightning flashed in the clear sky, and thunder roared loudly. Rome had unusually many thunderclaps and bolts of lightning. That was why Jupiter, whom Rome worshiped, was the god of thunder.

By the time he had almost reached his house, the steward Lucius came running out and bent his waist.

"Congratulations on your promotion, Curator Marius Andronicus."

Receiving Lucius's greeting, Andronicus still could not hide the lonely cast of his face. Formerly Drusiana would have run out first. Even as he entered the mansion, his heart kept pounding as though Drusiana might come running out through the pines of the garden.

The steward Lucius walked behind him and said,

"One officer who is to accompany you to Asia Minor this time has been waiting here since earlier. From him I learned of your promotion."

"What did he say his name was?"

But before Lucius could answer, from among the pines there appeared a man in military uniform.

"Curator Marius Andronicus, congratulations on your promotion."

Andronicus looked at his face. It seemed like a face he had seen somewhere before. As though he understood his curiosity, the other man introduced himself with a somewhat sinister smile.

"I am Musicus, appointed to command the velites, that is, the scout force, of the legion stationed at Ephesus."

"Musicus...?"

The velites were a lightly armed elite scouting unit of twelve hundred men within the legionary forces.

"Then... are you a tribune?"

Tribune meant the commander of a thousand, the officer who led a body of a thousand men in the Roman army.

"Yes. I too received my appointment only today."

However closely he looked, this man called Musicus did not appear to be from the cavalry. At any rate, if a legionary tribune belonged to the higher officers and was from neither the cavalry nor the tribunes of the civil side, then his background was suspect.

"Before this appointment... where had you been serving?"

As though seeing through Andronicus's thoughts, he smiled broadly.

"Curator... do you mean to send me away here in the garden just like this?"

"Ah... I have been rude. Lucius, conduct our guest to the reception room."

Only then did Lucius go before them and lead them into the reception room. When the two men entered the reception room and sat down, Lucius withdrew outside. Andronicus began to study Musicus's face slowly. However strong the Roman army might be, however much it possessed twenty-eight legions throughout the world, it was strange that an ambitious man like Andronicus should fail to remember the face of a high officer of tribune rank or above.

"You asked me, Curator... but as for my career before I was appointed tribune, it is hardly the sort of thing worth speaking about."

Only then could Andronicus guess this man's background. If it was not the sort of career worth speaking about, that meant he had worked in positions that did not appear in the open; then beyond doubt he must have performed duties such as those of a delator. Thinking of it, Andronicus now felt that perhaps he had encountered this man named Musicus three years ago at the entrance of the imperial palace, when Andronicus had returned from his official trip to Ephesus and gone to report to Emperor Domitian.

Looking at the face of this tribune named Musicus, Andronicus began to think that the expedition in Ephesus would not be easy. If Musicus really came from the delatores, then one could know all too well the intention of Emperor Trajanus, who had appointed him commander of the scouting force of the legion stationed in Asia. Once again Andronicus could only marvel at the emperor's stratagem. While sending off Senator Ovidius, a thorn in his eye, under the pretext of promotion, and attaching Andronicus to him as a curator who would watch him, at the same time he was sending along Musicus, a former informer, to keep Andronicus himself in check. If so, one could not know who might stand behind Musicus either.

"I regard it as good fortune that I will be serving beside such an excellent man as the Curator this time."

"What is there to look forward to in an official who is sent out to an external post?"

"As expected, even the very tone in which an ambitious man speaks is different. The caution not to show one's inner heart easily is essential in one who will later become great."

"I do not know what you are talking about."

"Curator, you cannot deceive the eyes of this Musicus. The ambitious men of the Roman Empire in every age all waited for their time while serving in the frontier regions. Emperor Vespasianus did so, and His Majesty Emperor Trajanus now upon the throne did so as well. And now Rome of the next generation will give opportunity precisely to a hero like the Curator."

"You... speak more carelessly than I expected."

"Only before the Curator in particular. When later you stand in a good place, I ask that you remember this Musicus."

In every age it was the same. Andronicus smiled bitterly, remembering the story Drusiana had told him about the youth of the Apostle John. When Jesus of Nazareth had foreseen His end and been sunk in anguish, John's brothers had put forward their mother to ask that when Jesus later sat in the highest place, He should give them the very best seats.

In the end, it seemed that the disciples of Jesus of Nazareth and this tribune from the informers were all alike, for a man was a man.

Seeing that Andronicus was smiling, Musicus perhaps thought his smooth talk had loosened the other man, and he stepped one pace further in.

"In the meantime... it must have been very inconvenient for the Curator to live alone."

"I heard that you suffered an unhappy event two years ago."

His talk was now entering its real point. By making a show of knowing even the affairs of Andronicus's household, he meant, Do not ignore me, for I know as much as needs to be known about you.

"Tribune Musicus... you are rather attentive."

It was an openly mocking remark. This man was surely a line from the emperor. The fact that he had come and been waiting before Andronicus himself had returned proved that he had received his appointment earlier, and furthermore that he had known of Andronicus's promotion before Andronicus did. In other words, in matters of information, he was undoubtedly one step above Andronicus.

"As expected, Curator, you recognize people well. As you say, I am the sort who lives by paying attention to too many things. Curator, I am always a man loyal to one who recognizes me."

"A good thought."

"Curator, when do you intend to launch the expedition?"

"...What do you mean?"

Musicus was smiling broadly and looking straight into Andronicus's eyes.

"Two years ago, did you not march to suppress the bandits of Cappadocia, only to withdraw?"

"And so?"

"As I know it... men of the Roman Empire who come from the cavalry never leave a debt unpaid. Now that the Curator too has let it sit for two years, it seems the time has come to repay it."

"But, Musicus... Rome now wants peace, from His Majesty the Emperor down to the citizens."

"That is true. At least on the surface."

"On the surface?"

"His Majesty will never directly command the expedition himself."

"His Majesty needs both to respect the opinion of the Senate and the citizens, and to expand the territory. But those two things are now in mutual contradiction."

That was precisely it. Emperor Trajanus had no need to order an expedition with his own mouth. He was exercising the curious stratagem of sending a pacifist like Ovidius into the province and having him be the one who launched the expedition. Trajanus was that kind of man. He was a man who never verbally ordered what he wanted.

"Curator Andronicus."

Musicus said it while leaning the upper part of his body forward.

"Now you have become a figure with the praetorship before you. You should recognize that the eyes of those around you are always upon you, and take care concerning matters close to your person."

"In that sense, it seems I may need your help."

"Do you know why Governor Lycomedes of Ephesus petitioned to resign?"

"No?"

"Rumors had begun to circulate that Governor Lycomedes had become a Christianus. However much His Majesty may refrain from making arrests recklessly, he will not like an official who allows such rumors to circulate about himself."

Andronicus started in surprise. That Governor Lycomedes, who had seemed so noble and prudent, had become a Christianus? It was truly hard to believe. More than even Musicus, who had seized all kinds of information in his grasp and was approaching him threateningly, Andronicus felt an indescribable fear of the terrible power of the Christiani, who could draw even a man like Lycomedes over to their side.

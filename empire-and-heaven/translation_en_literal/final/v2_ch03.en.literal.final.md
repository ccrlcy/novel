# Empire and Heaven (II)

## Chapter 3. The Garden of a Wounded Heart

People were walking through a passage in the darkness. The women going in front carried lamps, and the men following behind also carried lamps in their hands. The passage was broad enough that one need not bend at the waist to walk through it, and the floor had been dressed so smoothly that one might have walked it with eyes closed.

"Gaius, who is carrying the box of tools?"

Drusiana, dressed in a white stola, asked the boy walking beside her. Since all the others moving through the passage wore black garments, her figure in white stood out distinctly.

"The opifex walking behind us is carrying it."

The woman walking in front with the lamp said,

"Let us go down the stairs, domina."

They began descending the stairs. They called Drusiana domina, that is, mistress. Since the title given to Callimachus, the chief who ruled this underground city, was dominus, that is, master, to call Drusiana domina meant that they recognized her as the wife of the dominus.

When she had first been kidnapped and brought into this underground kingdom, Drusiana understood that there was no command of Callimachus she could truly resist. Any stubbornness, in fact, was useless. Therefore, when Callimachus announced before his subordinate officers that he had married Drusiana and ordered them to call her domina, she knew that resistance would accomplish nothing, and so she had no choice but to remain silent.

This underground city in the valley of Korama was truly a kingdom in the shadows. Seen from outside, all that appeared were grotesquely shaped rock-mountains pocked with holes here and there, with a few houses clinging to them. But that appearance was only outward; in truth, caves spreading like a spider's web had begun within those houses and extended underground. This underground city was a three-dimensional city that descended seven levels below the earth, and within it were perfectly provided not only houses but work facilities such as mills and breweries, and cultural facilities such as hospital, school, public hall, and bathhouse. A single unit of this underground city could hold nearly ten thousand people, and there were several such underground cities connected to one another by subterranean passages.

Even Drusiana, who because of her experience going in and out of the catacombs of Ephesus had some prior knowledge of underground life, could not help being astonished by the enormous scale of this city of Korama. She was thinking of the meticulous and careful character of Callimachus from his youth. For she had heard that more than anything, it was thanks to the thorough reorganization of their leader Callimachus that this dark underground city had been changed into a place fit for human life.

Of course many underground caves had existed in Korama even before Callimachus joined the bandits and came here. Wanderers who had drifted in from every place had easily prepared dwellings for themselves by hollowing out this valley of tuff, which was well suited for excavation. But it was Callimachus who reshaped this disorderly underground city into a place where people could truly live.

He furnished this underground world with all kinds of production facilities and cultural institutions, piped water to each installation, and connected air shafts to the surface in order to remove dampness and odors.

Yet more than anything, the reason young Callimachus became the chief of this immense organization in less than three years was his thoroughgoing ideology and power of leadership.

There were among the opifices here refugees from frontier lands such as Pontus and Parthia, but most were people driven in from the competitive world: people who had left the countryside because grain prices had fallen and entered the cities, yet had failed to adapt even there and been pushed back out again; people who could not endure in the merit-based bureaucratic society and had dropped away; slaves and criminals.... Such were the citizens of this underground city. They were all people whose hearts had been wounded and who had drifted in from the outer world, abandoned lives in whom no one took any interest. Korama was the final settlement such people had discovered on the frontier of the empire.

Therefore their lives had been empty, and their organization disorderly. It was Callimachus who had encouraged and roused these wounded lives. He stripped away the disguised masks of the outer world and criticized it mercilessly. Against the noble society that bound, used, and exploited human beings through clever systems and intrigues, he rained down arrows of attack. Against the Roman Empire, which deceived the masses with thin tricks such as bureaucratic systems in which only cunning men could survive, public entertainments staged to soothe the dissatisfied, doles to the unemployed, and the increase of holidays, Callimachus cried out that they must raise the sword of revenge.

He taught the bandits of Korama the ideology of revenge and unfolded the logic of instinct. He demanded that they smash the masks of deception and bow before strength. He taught the bandits of Korama that they were the real, while the outer world was false. Thus he argued that what had been occupied by the false must be taken away, and defined that act not as taking, but as taking back.

To the lives of Korama, dry as kindling, the ideology of revenge poured out by Callimachus was a marvelous fascination. His arguments were enough to move and revive the wounded multitude of Korama. He awakened the sleeping souls of the bandits and set fire to their fading hearts. In the blink of an eye Callimachus became the savior of Korama and completely captured the bandits.

At some point the bandits had begun calling themselves opifices, that is, workers, and had bestowed upon Callimachus the title dominus, that is, master. And once raised up as leader, Callimachus never disappointed his followers. He reorganized the scattered bandit force into a powerful body, and refined and cultivated their underground city into an alluring black garden.

The lamp-bearing woman walking ahead stopped and spoke softly. At the end of the stairs, where the passage turned again to the right, there was an arched doorway, blocked by a great stone. Putting her mouth to a small hole beside the stone door, the woman with the lamp said,

"Open the door. The domina has come."

At once there came a rumbling beneath their feet, and the stone door of the entrance split apart to both sides. The group passed through into the space beyond. It was a hospital. In room after room lined along both sides of the passage stood white beds, and groaning patients lay on them.

An old woman in charge of the hospital came forward and bowed respectfully to Drusiana.

"Welcome, domina."

Looking over the patients, Drusiana asked the old woman,

"It seems many new patients have come in."

"That is so."

Drusiana told the opifices who had followed to put down the box of medical tools and then wait outside. When they set it down and withdrew, Drusiana asked the old woman again,

"There must have been another severe battle."

"Domina, it is the dominus's policy that one should not reveal unnecessary information outside one's assigned duty."

Drusiana began going around one by one, examining the wounds of the patients. Most were wounds caused by spears or swords. With practiced hands she continued cleaning their wounds, applying medicine, and binding them. She had already had experience caring for poor patients in Ephesus, but during the year and a half she lived in Rome with Andronicus, she had begun studying medicine in earnest.

"This...."

Removing the thin blanket covering one man's leg, Drusiana drew back her face. The man's leg had been horribly crushed by something, and was already rotting, sending up a stench.

"Gaius, take out the surgical tools."

Looking at Drusiana with a face full of pain, the patient said,

"Domina, must the leg be cut off?"

Stroking his brow, Drusiana said gently,

"Life is more important than a leg."

"Domina, I will not have my leg cut off."

"If you do not, the whole body will rot."

"Leave it as it is. If my leg is cut off, I become a man of no use at all. I will not be able to ride a horse or fight. I will have to live as a burden to my friends. That is a shameful thing for a man."

That was exactly the logic of their leader Callimachus. In the world of instinct, only the strong are true, and the defeated must cleanly acknowledge their defeat and withdraw. In his thinking, instinct alone was truth, it was the law of nature, and deception arose because men refused to accept it. Therefore, to this man, to live burdening one's friends was less honorable than to die exactly as one was now dying, according to the teaching of the chief. Drusiana shook her head.

"That is too simple a judgment. Even without a leg, there is much good work you can do. You can work in the mill, or in a hospital like this, or with your hands in any number of ways."

"Those are women's tasks."

In this place, the work of men was chiefly war and plunder. Nearly every other task had been assigned to women. It was Callimachus's contention that if women were left idle, the order of the group would become corrupted.

Thus the tasks that in the outer world would have been done by men, from mills and breweries and hospital work all the way to farming, were all here assigned to women. And that was Callimachus's version of equality between men and women. If women did not want to do hard work, he said, that was the same as falling into prostitution, and not making women work was itself corruption.

"But... your dominus said that wounded men could do such work."

"That is a gracious consideration. But think about it. Even if I did such work with only one leg, I would hardly do half of what a woman can do. To live burdening others while doing not even half their work is a greater shame than death."

Tears welled in Drusiana's eyes. This man, who thought it shameful to work only half as well as another... While she lived in Rome, Drusiana had seen many who did not do half of another's work, nor in fact any work at all, yet busied themselves managing, rebuking, and ruling others. Far from feeling ashamed, they spent every day going to the gymnasium and the baths to reduce their extra flesh, and every night attended banquets to gorge themselves on rich food.

And now this man, afraid that he would be able to do only half as much work as another, was saying he would rather die than lose his leg.

"Domina, I am deeply grateful for your consolation. Please understand me. If you have some medicine by which I may die more quickly, please give it to me instead. I wish to end this pain soon."

At last Drusiana could bear it no longer and began to weep. Gaius, who had been laying out the surgical tools, knife and saw and the rest, was also wiping away tears with the back of his hand. Taking the man's hand, Drusiana said,

"I can urge you no further. May I pray for you to the God in whom I believe?"

"Domina, your words are kind, but I refuse. A warrior of Korama does not hand over his life to another. Responsibility for oneself belongs to oneself, and that responsibility ends when one dies. Domina, I give thanks to you, to our chief Callimachus, and to all the friends who have stood around me until now."

Having said this, the patient closed his eyes. Struck by what she had heard from him, Drusiana could hardly collect herself. She could not tell how she treated the other patients. Even while washing, stitching, and bandaging their wounds, she kept praying under her breath.

(Lord... You said You would not break the bruised reed, nor quench the smoking flax. What... what will You do with these people?)

When the treatment was finished and she returned with Gaius toward the headquarters, Drusiana's thoughts were still entirely tangled. Gaius said in a low voice so that the opifices following behind would not hear,

"They say Quaestor Andronicus may attack."

Drusiana turned to Gaius in alarm.

"Where did you hear such a thing?"

"I heard the opifices talking to one another."

When Drusiana and Gaius had first been brought here captive, Andronicus had indeed come leading troops and attacked the fortress of Korama. In high spirits after smashing Andronicus's cavalry cohort, Callimachus had told Drusiana of it himself. He had laughed at Andronicus, saying he had fled with his guts hanging out after his forces were annihilated. To come against him as though Callimachus were no more than a neighborhood gang leader, bringing only one cavalry cohort! Callimachus had boasted loudly that if Andronicus meant to wage real war against him, he should bring all twenty-eight legions of the Roman Empire at once.

Even though there was no great war, Rome still had to maintain a vast army in order to keep anyone from even daring to conceive rebellion. That immense army not only drew high pay while having little to do, but because of its strength demanded huge privileges. The emperor, in turn, had to keep his nerves stretched at all times so that dissatisfaction should not arise in the military, had to raise their pay every year, and even had to build great brothel districts to satisfy the sexual appetite of the legionaries. Though the key administrative offices were all held by men from the cavalry, the emperor also had to grant land to ordinary retired officers so that their livelihood might be assured. Thus the farmland of every province became the property of those military powers, and the farmers who lost their land fell into tenancy. In the end they gave up farming and crowded into the cities, while war slaves took over the labor in their place.

"Didn't Marius come to Korama and suffer defeat?"

"Yes, but lately Uncle Callimachus seems to be holding repeated strategic councils with his followers. I think they are chiefly planning for the day when Quaestor Andronicus comes again leading regular legionaries."

Drusiana knew that Andronicus would surely come again. Unless he had died, he would never give up the restoration of the honor he had lost by returning alive. In Drusiana's eyes, though he had received baptism from John, he was not a man who truly believed in Christ. He knew much about Jesus of Nazareth, and had considerable curiosity about Him, but he had shown little sign of assenting to His teaching. In him there was only the pride and administrative ability of a core official of the Roman Empire. Therefore Drusiana knew that if Andronicus had been defeated at Korama and returned, he would surely come again to recover that defeat. His love for Drusiana was one matter, but a larger matter still was the honor of a Roman official.

When they returned to headquarters, the opifex standing guard bowed and said,

"You have labored much, domina. The dominus, who has just arrived, is waiting."

Passing through the stone gate of headquarters and then through another long passage, Drusiana came to the front of the chief's office. The guard standing there bowed respectfully and then reported her arrival inward. The stone door of the office opened.

Walking out toward the door, Callimachus spread wide both arms.

"O my beautiful wife and mother of Korama, Drusiana, now that you have appeared, this room has grown bright as though the sun itself had rolled in."

Perhaps they had been discussing some important matter, for the officers standing around the conference table were looking this way. Callimachus said to them,

"That will be enough for now. I have ordered a victory banquet prepared in the next room, so go there first and wait. My wife and I will join you presently."

When the officers had withdrawn, Callimachus offered Drusiana a seat and took one himself. Gaius too sat pressed close beside Drusiana, staring fixedly at Callimachus.

"I hear you went to the hospital?"

"The number of patients has grown again."

"This battle was a very great one, and we won a great victory."

"Then if there were so many wounded, many must also have died."

"A man's duty is to fight, and to die in battle is only natural. A true man should count it an honor to be pierced by an enemy's sword rather than to lie sick in a woman's arms and die."

"Callimachus, of course where there is war, one cannot help but fight. But I do not think fighting can be made the purpose of life."

"And if not fighting, what is there? Look at the beasts that run in the fields. They are born, they eat, they fight, they breed. That is honesty. Drusiana, the ones who deny that are exactly the deceivers and the cowards. One must know how to see that behind their dignified manner and thin reasoning are hidden their filthy schemes and cunning deceits. Their plots always begin to rot from within, and in time they fall. The warriors of Korama fight honestly. Only ceaseless struggle will keep the warriors of Korama proud and fresh."

"But Uncle Callimachus..."

Gaius, who had been sitting there with dark eyes glittering, broke in.

"If man was born in order to fight, why was he not born with sharp claws or strong teeth?"

"What?"

"Look at the fierce beasts that rule the fields. All of them have terrible claws and teeth. But men do not. Not only do they have no fearsome weapons, they do not even have thick shells or hides."

"You mean, uncle, that just as the other beasts have terrible claws and teeth, man has the head instead, don't you?"

"Then... if that head can do other things, is it only for making weapons? If the mind of man is better than that of beasts, might he not also think thoughts different from theirs?"

Truly there was something sharp in the boy Gaius's question. Callimachus smiled faintly and answered vaguely.

"There are many things a man must think about. He must think about battle plans, about how to handle men, about how to work and how to teach children. Gaius, have you completed the sword practice you were supposed to do today?"

"Ah... not yet."

"Then go quickly to the sword court. A man must have strength and skill with the blade if he is to live without being despised by others."

"So then is it to fight with sword and shield, with spear or bow?"

"But uncle, all living creatures have everything they need. Why then is it that only man is born without weapons? Might it not be because he does not need them?"

At Gaius's odd question, Callimachus was momentarily speechless. But soon he replied,

"That is exactly why man has the head that knows how to make weapons, is it not? Because with his mind he can make anything...."

"Exactly, uncle."

Gaius nodded as though he understood and stood up. He had almost reached the door when he turned around and said once more,

"Uncle, it seems to me that the head of man must think about more things than fighting."

Leaving that one sentence behind, Gaius went out. Callimachus watched the door for a moment, then rose and drew the mechanism that shut it. The stone door rolled out from both sides and closed. Then he came behind Drusiana, who sat in the chair, and laid his hands on her shoulders. Under the heat of his hands, her soft shoulders trembled.

"Drusiana........"

She sprang to her feet and turned to face him. Callimachus's eyes were flashing.

"Drusiana, it has already been a year since you came here. I took you away, and you are in my hand. I declared you my wife, and all the people here serve you. This may not please your heart, but you must submit to your fate. In Ephesus you left the orphans and went away. But now you will not escape again."

"I will not escape."

"Then now bear my son. A woman must bear the son of the victorious man."

"Do you mean I must bear a son, or that I must produce troops to send onto the battlefield?"

"Both. You know why the females of beasts bear the offspring of the victor, do you not? It is instinct, in order to bear strong young and preserve a strong race. The opifices of Korama must be continued through strong warriors. Drusiana, bear the son of Callimachus. He will become a warrior and a splendid leader of Korama. Men will say that Callimachus and Drusiana bore a great son."

"I will not. I do not want to bear a murderer."

Drusiana shook her head and cut off the words sharply. Callimachus stepped one pace closer. Her heaving breasts were almost touching his chest.

"Drusiana... in this Korama there is nothing my power cannot do. All this time, I could have taken you by force and made you conceive my son whenever I wished. But that was not what I wanted. I have waited for the time when you would submit to the law of nature. I have waited until the time when you would truly long to bear my son. I took you away, and you are mine. How much longer will you make me wait?"

Drusiana was trembling all over with fear. It was not because of the man Callimachus, but because the fact itself was so terrible: that one insignificant woman could so utterly overturn a man's life.

In truth, if only she had not been the wife of Andronicus, Drusiana felt that she might have fallen into his arms as she was. What in the world was a woman called Drusiana, that she could wound a man so deeply and overturn his thoughts so completely? In her heart Drusiana was crying out in anguish. The body of Callimachus seemed to her like the rocks of the valley of Korama, blown open with holes as volcanic lava burst and hardened. She wanted to leap into his arms just as she was and give him all the burning fullness within her. She wanted to fill with caresses of consolation every gaping hole of grief broken open in his breast.

"Drusiana!"

Callimachus sprang toward her, meaning to seize her in his arms. Drusiana stepped back swiftly and said,

"The officers are waiting. You must attend the banquet of celebration."

"Drusiana... will you attend the banquet with me?"

She nodded. Callimachus opened the stone door again and took her hand. Hand in hand, like a truly affectionate married couple, they walked side by side down the corridor. During the whole year she had lived in this place, Callimachus had endured time and again the desire to possess her by force. That was because he knew well that beasts never rape the female. As they passed, the opifices standing guard saluted. At length they arrived in a broad banquet hall. The hall was already crowded with officers and many beautiful women.

At the appearance of the chief, those gathered in the hall all stepped aside and made way. Callimachus and Drusiana went to the place prepared in the center and sat there. It was the high place, spread with leopard skins. Then the officers all sat around them, and women adorned with beautiful clothing and jewels began carrying wine to the men.

The opifices in charge of the food brought trays piled with every kind of dish and set them before the officers. Taking a cup of wine, Callimachus said to the officers,

"Comrades, you have labored much. Now we have become an invincible strong army that will not be defeated wherever it goes. In the world of falsehood we were cast aside, but in the world of truth we have won. This is the law of nature, and it is the honest outcome. Before us now there will be only everlasting victory and honor. You are heroes and pioneers changing the history of mankind. Come then, let us drink. To the glory of Korama!"

All lifted their cups together. Holding up his own cup, Callimachus looked at Drusiana. Though her face was hard, she too had no choice but to lift her cup. The officers cried aloud together:

"Long live the dominus!"

And Callimachus too answered in a loud voice:

"Long live the heroes of Korama!"

At once the men drained their cups. They swallowed the wine down. Callimachus looked at Drusiana and held out his cup. Slowly she raised her own and touched it to his. There came a clear ringing sound. As he urged, she drank a little of the wine. The moment the drink ran down her throat, her chest began to burn.

Music began. It was the traditional music of Cappadocia, which Callimachus loved. The mournful melody centered upon the pipe settled itself throughout the room. When that melody, not only desperate but almost eerie, reached its height, a half-naked dancer wrapped in gauze entered from the entrance. She fixed upon Callimachus a gaze so intense it seemed to stop the breath, and then slowly began to twist her body. As her body moved, at times softly and at times violently, the people fixed their eyes upon it, drawing in breath or sighing. Her body undulated with the music like water. The music gradually began to quicken. Little by little excitement mounted among those gathered, born of the shared atmosphere, and at last one man sprang to his feet and began to dance with the woman.

The eyes of the man and the woman were meeting with an intensity as though each meant to burn up the other. It seemed almost a gaze of hatred, or a gaze of cursing. As though fighting with swords, their eyes struck sparks in the air. Their bodies, approaching and retreating, trembled as though in spasm. Then again the music began to soften. Only then did the eyes of the man and woman change into the color of mutual desire. When someone let out a groan as though unable to bear it, low laughter rippled through the room.

Now the dancer flung away the gauze that had wrapped her body and laid herself upon the floor. Her smooth skin flashed in the firelight. The man threw himself upon her. There, in full view of all, the two bodies were now twisting together like snakes.

Then every woman in the room came forward and began to dance. One by one they stripped off the garments they wore. In the firelight their bodies began to be exposed. The bodies of dark women, pale women, and brown-skinned women were tempting the men who had long ridden the battlefields. At last the men rose and took into their arms the women each desired.

Pressing her burning breast with her hand, Drusiana watched the sight. These were people who had been cast aside by the world and had no place left to set their feet, and so had been driven all the way to this far end of Cappadocia. People for whom there was no one to take concern, no one to care, who had drifted like clouds until finally flowing into this valley of Korama. Yet now those neglected failures had found the joy of life. Riding the fields at the risk of their lives, they had at last learned this: that a man becomes strong only when he stakes everything on the outcome. Now there was in them no shame and no inferiority. What filled them entirely was only the pride that they themselves were taking responsibility for their own lives.

One of the men began singing in a loud voice. It was the popular song the nobles of Smyrna had sung while being kicked about in the theater of Pagus.

My love Lesbia,

Among all the women of this world,
what woman has been loved more hotly than this?

Beloved Lesbia,
Among all the men of this world,
what man has confessed a bond more precious than mine?

Callimachus's hand slowly came and softly took Drusiana's hand. Through that hand his burning heat was pouring into the deepest part of Drusiana. When she was a girl and held that hand as they ran here and there, she had not known it; but now his hand had become a fierce temptation that rushed upon her.

"Drusiana!"

At last, unable to restrain his passion, Callimachus drew her into his arms. The soft woman's body entered his embrace. No longer was she the thin little girl who once ran about tossing her braided hair. During the year and a half in Rome, and then the year spent here in Korama, she had without anyone's noticing ripened into a woman on the point of bursting.

That woman's body was burning hot in Callimachus's arms and did not know what to do. She had closed her eyes, her chest fluttering like a bird that had been caught. Callimachus's lips moved from the back of her neck, past the place beneath her ear, toward her own lips....

(Ah, Lord, what am I to do?)

Tears ran down from Drusiana's eyes. Those tears flowed down her cheeks and into Callimachus's mouth. And those tears became a sea, in the midst of which Callimachus was floating.

It was then. Suddenly Drusiana gave a cry and thrust Callimachus away with all her strength.

Drusiana could think of nothing. Nothing came to mind. There was only one thing: she could never thrust away this pitiful man Callimachus. She herself did not matter. She did not mind being cursed as a wicked woman, or denounced as an immoral woman. It seemed to her that she could not possibly turn away the plea of Callimachus, who now came to her in such desperate longing. Someone had to comfort his wounded heart. If God did not do it, then it felt as though Drusiana herself must do it.

"Drusiana!"

She was already outside Callimachus's arms. And her own hand was lifting the hem of her white stola. Beneath the lifted cloth, a black viper was crawling up her smooth leg.

Callimachus sprang in panic, seized the viper by the neck, and tore it from Drusiana's leg. The serpent again trembled its tail and tried to wind itself around Callimachus's wrist. He hurled it violently to the floor and drew the sword at his waist and struck down at it again and again.

Even after it had been hacked over its whole body, the viper was still writhing. One of the followers standing near Callimachus picked it up and carried it out. The women all shrank back and screamed.

Callimachus set Drusiana upright and examined her leg. Already it was swelling red. He found the marks of the fangs and put his mouth to them, fiercely sucking out the poison.

At last the face of Drusiana, which had gone pale, softened, and she looked down at the head of Callimachus bent over her leg. Callimachus, orphaned and raised in the house of Tyrannus without ever knowing the faces of his parents; Callimachus, who had grown up struggling all the more because his father had been an apostate; yet he had always been Drusiana's protector. Whenever the childish Drusiana had run through mountain and field, Callimachus had always watched over and cared for her from a little distance. Because his eyes had always been there, Drusiana could go anywhere with her mind at ease, no matter where she went.

In truth, even when she married Andronicus and followed him all the way to Rome, it was because she felt reassured by Callimachus's watchful gaze at her back. She had believed that his eyes were always beside her.

Except for the love of Andronicus himself, the year and a half in Rome had been lonely days. Andronicus was always busy with official business and absorbed in his own affairs to the point that she could hardly see his face. In Rome there had been no friends worth making, nor any place worth going. Moreover, fearing that harm might come upon the promising young official Andronicus, she had not even gone to seek the underground church that was there. Thus she had no choice but to spend her time talking with the boy Gaius whom she had brought from Ephesus. In the end, it was to fill that empty time that she had begun studying medicine.

During those lonely days, what had guarded and comforted her was the sense that Callimachus's gaze still remained beside her. When she was lonely, that gaze had encouraged her; when she slept, it had been the protector that drew the blanket over her.

After sucking the poison from her leg, Callimachus asked,

"Where is the antidote?"

"If you go to my room, it will be there. There should be a bottle marked antidote."

Callimachus lifted her onto his back. Long before, when she had fallen on Mount Ayasoluk and injured her leg, Callimachus too had carried her in this way. And now, as she was borne again on his back, she felt a deep peace in her heart.

Callimachus passed through the long corridor and came to Drusiana's room. He opened the stone door and entered her clean chamber, the first time he had ever done so. Setting Drusiana down on the bed, he asked,

"Where is the medicine?"

"Look in the cabinet. There should be a bottle marked for snake venom."

Drusiana's body was burning like fire. Her breathing grew rapid, and her head began to cloud over. By the time Callimachus turned back after finding the medicine bottle, Drusiana had already lost consciousness.

Callimachus's heart dropped. He seized her and shook her wildly.

"Drusiana! Drusiana!"

Her body had gone utterly limp. Like a man bereft of his wits, Callimachus kept calling only her name. Gaius, who had come running after hearing the news, was spreading medicine on Drusiana's leg and wrapping it with bandages.

Drusiana's stupor continued. Her head was burning like a live coal. The nurses summoned from the hospital took turns laying cold water bags upon her head. In a choked voice, Callimachus muttered,

"Drusiana... you must live. If you die like this, what is to become of all this bitterness knotted inside me...?"

He truly did not know what to do. So long as she had still been in Rome, there had been only burning anger and nothing like this. But now that he thought she might never awaken again, he was so overcome that he could think of nothing.

Looking at him with pity, the boy Gaius said,

"...You know what must be done at times like this, don't you?"

Not understanding what he meant, Callimachus stared at Gaius with bloodshot eyes.

"Honestly... you've forgotten already. At times like this, one prays. One prays."

Callimachus only sat there, eyes wide and blank, staring at the boy.

"At a time like this, what do you think you can do except pray? Strength? Conviction? A sword?"

Gaius was pressing him almost mockingly. Then he said again,

"If you've even forgotten how to pray, I'll teach you. Do as I do."

The boy glanced at Callimachus, closed his eyes, and began to pray.

"Almighty Lord... Your beloved daughter is now in danger. Lord, who created heaven and earth by Your word and orders all human affairs by Your wisdom... now we ask that You save this daughter. We pray that You hear the prayer of pitiful Callimachus and of this Gaius, and that You not take Drusiana away from us. We are full of sin and fault, yet we pray leaning on the merit of Jesus Christ, who shed His blood upon the cross... Amen."

While Gaius prayed, Callimachus sat in silence, only staring into empty space. It was Callimachus who had at last escaped toward freedom. And now he was once more caught fast in the trap called Drusiana. He tried stubbornly to shake his head. Is there really a god? There is no such thing. There is no one who can bind me. I will go my own road.

Yet each time he looked down at Drusiana sunk in her stupor, his heart weakened again. If there truly were a god, it did not seem likely that such a god would save her for the sake of Callimachus, who had betrayed Him and run away.

Callimachus had always praised death. He had always told others that a man should count as honorable a manly death on the battlefield. More than that, he himself had always resolved that such a splendid end would be his own. He had resolved that after loving everything beautiful in this world with all his strength, and living with all his strength, he would vanish upright into the everlasting darkness from which there is no return.

Yet now before Drusiana, that very resolve had become another snare wrapping around him. Let him die as he might; Drusiana herself must live.

(If only... if only I had not brought her here.... If only I had left her at the side of Andronicus... would it not have been better than this?)

Callimachus thought that Drusiana had come to this state surely because of himself. Because of him, she had come to this place and had been bitten by the serpent.

Yet even after being dragged to such a remote and harsh place, Drusiana had uttered not one complaint and not one word of resentment. More than that, she had always comforted and encouraged the pitiful people living in Korama, and cared for them with tenderness. Her service in the hospital was wholly devoted, and when she appeared the patients took heart as though an angel had come.

No matter how fervently Callimachus might seize men by his speeches, this underground city was in the end no more than a savage den of bandits. Drusiana, the moment she came here, had turned that barren underground city into a splendid garden. As everyone praised her, she was the mother of Korama.

(Ah... Drusiana!)

Now her life was no longer only Callimachus's own concern. If she did not awaken, the caves of Korama would become again a grim and desolate bandits' lair.

(Drusiana, it is all my fault....)

Callimachus's eyes were growing redder still.

(Why did I bring Drusiana to such a place? What was there in such a dark cave that I had to bring her here? Why did I drag her, who had been living happily in a good house and a good place, into this sort of black hole? Such holes are where men like me belong... where castoffs, bastards, and bandits belong. Why did I bring her here?)

And yet Drusiana had not uttered a single complaint. Lest Callimachus's heart be pained, she had not once spoken aloud of her husband Andronicus.

In any case, since he had been the man she herself had chosen, she must have loved Andronicus. She had lived with that husband for barely a year and a half before being brought captive to this cave. How greatly she must have longed to see that husband. And yet she had not shown any sign of it at all. She had never said she wanted to go, nor asked to be sent away. To such a woman, Callimachus had shamelessly demanded that she bear his son.

Again the nurse came, replaced the cold water bag, and withdrew. Callimachus was growing angry at all these things tangled and ruined because of himself. Looking into the empty air, he cried out in his heart:

(God... do You truly exist? Why do You not appear in better things, but only in things this hurtful? Why... if You are angry because of me, why do You strike not me but this pitiful woman? Why do You not face me openly? If there is something You want from me, come to me. Let this woman go and settle the matter with me. Come. I am ready to contend with You....)

At that moment a man came walking in through the door. He was a strongly built officer. He hesitated for a moment, then opened his mouth.

"It seems the snake came in through the water pipe. There was moisture on the snake's body, and when we examined the floor, there was dampness on the marks where it had crawled."

In truth, because in this underground cavern one had always to beware of all sorts of insects and snakes, many measures had been set up against them. At every entrance to the caverns, lime had been spread or repellent powders scattered so that beasts and insects would not enter. The man paused a little and then continued his report.

"We have ordered that screens be set in all the water pipes, both at the intake and at points in between, and we have also sent word to the neighboring settlements so that they too may take precaution from this case."

Callimachus nodded weakly and said,

"You have done well."

Yet the man did not go out. Noticing that, Callimachus asked,

"Have you still some further report?"

"It is... a report that has come from Rome."

"From Rome...?"

"Yes. After Domitian was assassinated, Nerva, who had been raised to the throne by election of the Senate, has, as expected, begun to show signs of failing health because of his great age. Therefore...."

"Therefore...?"

"The intelligence is that Emperor Nerva, sensing that he does not have long to live, has appointed his successor."

"And whom do they say he has appointed?"

"Nerva had for some time been under various pressures from the military, and in the end it was the military that nominated the successor. They say he has designated General Marcus Ulpius Trajanus, known as a capable soldier from Hispania. In order to gain the support of the Senate, Trajanus in turn resigned his governorship of Germania and entered the position of princeps of the Senate, that is, chief senator. Thus... it seems some change will come also to Quaestor Andronicus."

"What sort of change?"

"As you know, last September Quaestor Andronicus came against Korama leading the cavalry cohort of the governor of Ephesus, and was utterly defeated by our counterattack and fled. After returning to Rome, he made every effort to gain authorization for the dispatch of regular legionaries, but had been blocked by Emperor Nerva, who was pursuing a policy of liberalization. But now it seems Nerva has not much longer, and if the military man Trajanus comes to the throne, the proposal of Quaestor Andronicus for an expedition will stand in a far more favorable position."

"Very well. Is that all?"

"That is all."

When the man withdrew, Callimachus again turned his eyes to Drusiana. As though refusing to see anything at all, her eyes were still closed.

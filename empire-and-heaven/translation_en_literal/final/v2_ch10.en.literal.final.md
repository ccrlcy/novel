# Empire and Heaven (II)

## Chapter 10. The Vine and the Fig Tree

Even on the way to Jerusalem, Jesus' disciples were wrangling over which man ought to sit in which place when their teacher later took power. Though John had successfully approached Jesus and gained his trust, he still could not quite feel at ease and meant to secure an actual pledge. Because he felt awkward stepping forward directly, he put forward his own mother Salome, who was serving near Jesus and caring for him. Thus, at last, when their company had come near Jericho, Salome waited for her chance and told Jesus that she had a request.

"Speak what it is that you desire."

"When your kingdom is established... I desire that one of my two sons may sit at your right hand and the other at your left."

Having received John's request, Salome, in the momentum of it, had petitioned Jesus on behalf of both her sons together. But Jesus, wearing a quiet smile, looked at the brothers John and James.

"You do not know what you are asking of me now. Do you think that you are able to drink the cup that I am about to drink?"

Without understanding anything of the matter, John and James answered that they were able; only long afterward did they understand what a fearful answer that had been. In any case, the incident of Salome's petition leaked out to the other disciples, and they became greatly enraged. Jesus soothed those disciples and said,

"The one who wishes to be served must become one who serves."

As Jesus passed through the city of Jericho, he met two blind beggars sitting by the roadside and opened their eyes. This was the final miracle of his life. Of these two men, one later went with Lazarus to the region of Lugdunum, and another named Bartimaeus followed Peter.

Therefore Matthew, who witnessed this event, recorded two men, whereas Mark, who had not, recorded only Bartimaeus, whom he himself had met.

Thus, when their company arrived at Bethany, the people of Bethany, who had already guessed that Jesus would come at Passover, were making preparations for a great feast. They had arrived exactly one week before Passover. John, having already followed Jesus in secret and witnessed the raising of Lazarus, had expected that such a thing would happen. But while the disciples who had come along knowing nothing stood in great bewilderment, Jesus, as on other occasions, was trying to enter the house of Lazarus.

Then suddenly the disciples restrained Jesus. And they clearly expressed their opinion.

"Teacher, we will not enter the house of Lazarus."

All the people standing around were greatly startled, and Jesus too looked for a long while at the faces of the disciples with a surprised expression. Thus quite some time passed. Yet for some reason Jesus asked the disciples an unexpected question.

"To whose house do you desire to go?"

This time the disciples were struck dumb. But Peter looked at the people and said,

"Who wishes to receive our Teacher into his house?"

But no one answered those words. It was because the welcoming feast for Jesus naturally ought to be held at the house of Lazarus, who had come out from the tomb. When Peter shouted a second time, a woman standing at the back said in a small voice,

"If it pleases you, we will receive him into our house."

Everyone looked that way and was astonished. That woman was none other than the wife of Simon the leper. But more astonishing still was Jesus' own movement. He began to walk after the wife of Simon the leper. All the people began dispersing.

Though Simon the leper was at that time under isolation, the very thought of going to a house where a leper had lived was unpleasant. Martha and Mary, the sisters of Lazarus, who had prepared many foods, were at a loss. They had no choice but to ask people to carry the food to Simon's house, and Martha had to go into the kitchen of Simon's house and prepare the feast.

At that day's feast Lazarus naturally sat in the upper seat beside Jesus, and the faces of the disciples were all swollen with dissatisfaction. Once more Jesus used a parable to admonish the disciples. At a feast, he said, it is better to sit in the lower place, and later, when the master comes, he may draw that man up to the seat of honor. Even so, the disciples grumbled.

(Confound it... he says nothing at all about Lazarus sitting in the seat of honor....)

It was just as the feast was about to begin. Mary, the sister of Lazarus, came in carrying an alabaster jar, broke it, and poured what was inside upon Jesus' head and feet. Suddenly a deep fragrance was filling the whole room. What had been inside the jar was nard ointment, a precious thing difficult even to behold. All opened their mouths in astonishment, and then at last Jesus' disciples began rebuking her. Their dissatisfaction was starting to burst out against Mary.

"What is this? Perfume of that amount would bring three hundred denarii. It ought rather to be sold and distributed to the poor."

Their calculation was exact, and their argument was correct. The reason the disciples could think that way was because all of them had in the meantime learned the thought of Judas from Kerioth. In truth, three hundred denarii was a great sum equal to a laborer's wages for one whole year. But Jesus said to the disciples who were rebuking her, in a voice slightly flushed,

"Do not blame this woman so severely. The poor will always be beside you, but I will not remain long."

When Jesus grew angry, the disciples, embarrassed, were shrinking back somewhat. But Judas, who had guided them while they were apart from Jesus, could not simply retreat. Unable to vent his anger against the Teacher himself, he shouted harshly at Mary.

"You there, why did you not sell this costly perfume and give it to the poor, but waste it like this?"

At the same time the high voice of Jesus struck upon Judas's ear.

"Do not trouble that woman! She is preparing my burial!"

There was deep meaning in Jesus' words. Messiah was the Hebrew word that meant precisely the Anointed One.

Having been shouted at by Jesus, Judas stared at his Teacher for a moment with insolent eyes, then suddenly rose to his feet and strode outside. Sensing that Judas's look was no ordinary thing, John quietly stood up and followed him out. Judas was on the night road, flinging both arms about and going toward Jerusalem.

John hesitated a moment, then began following after Judas. Judas reached Jerusalem in very rapid strides and entered the city without hesitation. Astonishingly, the place Judas went to was the house of Caiaphas the high priest. As Judas slipped into the house of Caiaphas, John too, being well known to the people of Caiaphas's house, passed through the main gate and pressed close after Judas. It seemed that Judas had perhaps come here before. If so, it appeared that not only Annas and his son-in-law Caiaphas, no, perhaps the whole Sanhedrin council, had not been secretly in contact only with John, but had also won over Judas.

When Judas entered the reception room, John hid himself between the pillars and listened to the sounds flowing out from within. Inside, the voice of Caiaphas was heard.

"You have come at a good time, Judas. Where is Jesus of Nazareth now?"

"He is in Bethany."

"It is certain, then. They are planning an uprising by putting forward Lazarus, the man said to have come out of the tomb."

"What? Come out of the tomb?"

"What, are you in the company of Jesus of Nazareth and yet do not know the incident in which he called dead Lazarus out of the tomb?"

"No."

"Look here. If even this man, a disciple of Jesus, does not know it, then the affair of Lazarus is fabricated, pure fabrication. It is certainly fabricated. But...."

For a moment it seemed as if the speaking was cut off, then again the voice of Caiaphas was heard.

"But... if we are to seize Jesus, there must be an accuser. There must be some accuser other than ourselves if we are to have proper grounds."

Then the voice of Judas from Kerioth was heard again.

"I will accuse him. If I accuse him, how much will you give me?"

"You have thought well. It is wiser to secure some advantage than to go on following a swindler for no reason.... Here then, thirty pieces of silver. Is that sufficient?"

"It is sufficient. I will send word of his whereabouts when I see my chance, so arrest him then."

Having heard that far, John instantly flew out from the house of Caiaphas. Then, almost running, he returned to Bethany. Thus one more powerful rival was about to disappear from before him. In due time, John intended to tell Jesus of Judas's betrayal and monopolize his trust.

Thus the night in which Jesus was anointed passed, and the day broke. Having again taken breakfast in Simon's house, Jesus came outside and said to Matthew and his younger brother James,

"If you go to the village of Bethphage across the way, you will see a donkey tied there. Go and borrow that donkey, saying that Jesus wishes to use it."

The hearts of the disciples began again to pound. That Jesus wished to ride a donkey meant that he intended to appear in Jerusalem as the Messiah. The Jews remembered the word of the prophet Zechariah, handed down from long ago. Their prophecy said that the new king who came would be humble and ride upon the foal of a donkey. And now Jesus had commanded them to bring a donkey.

The brothers of Matthew, having gone across into the village of Bethphage, saw a donkey and a foal tied outside the village. The owner of the donkey already knew that if Jesus were the Messiah, he would send for the donkey. The reason he had set out both animals was that he wished to make one final confirmation as to which one Jesus would choose. When Matthew tried to lead the donkey away, the owner asked,

"Why are you taking the donkey?"

"Our teacher Jesus wishes to use it."

"If so, take both of them."

The brothers of Matthew too understood the mind of the owner. They led both animals before Jesus. Jesus looked at the two donkeys, nodded his head, and mounted the back of the young foal. That was precisely the declaration of the Messiah. A shout began to burst from among the gathered people. Along the way where Jesus went they stripped off their outer garments and spread them down, and they cut tree branches and spread them in the road. Waving palm branches, they cried aloud.

"Hosanna! Blessed be! The Messiah has come!"

The word Hosanna meant, Save us now. In an instant crowds beyond counting gathered and began marching toward Jerusalem.

"Long live the kingdom of our father David!"

"Long live the highest kingdom!"

"O Son of David! O one who comes in the name of God, save us!"

In an instant Jesus and the foal upon which he rode were buried in the crowd. And the mother donkey that had been brought along together with it vanished from the memory of the people. But Matthew, who had directly led the donkeys there, remembered the mother donkey. And later Matthew also remembered the teaching of Jesus that he had not come to abolish the Law but to fulfill it. Matthew was originally a descendant of a priestly house, and therefore he could not neglect the mother donkey. Though Jesus rode the young donkey, he had to record the fact that there was a mother donkey with it.

When Jesus entered the city, Jerusalem within the walls was boiling with rumors concerning the Messiah. In the temple of Jerusalem Jesus proclaimed that the kingdom of heaven had drawn near, and once again drove out from the temple those who sold goods and those who changed money.

The disciples of Jesus, who had been excited and carried away, gradually recovered their composure and began to feel that at this rate the initiative might be stolen from them by the people of Jerusalem. They at once began cutting off the space between the people of Jerusalem and Jesus, making a human wall. And they began spreading the rumor they themselves had created. It was that the new government Jesus would establish would be led by Galileans. This rumor too spread with speed. Little by little the fever of the people of Jerusalem began to cool. And just then a delegation from the Sanhedrin council came out, sent to examine Jesus. Pushing through the people, they came near Jesus and began shooting arrows of questions. First of all they poured out words of rebuke.

Pointing to the children who were still waving palm branches and crying Hosanna, they said,

"By what authority are you causing such an uproar? Why do you not stop those children from shouting?"

"How can one stop the mouths of little children? If the children are silent, then even the stones will cry out."

"Who gave you authority to do this?"

"I too will ask one thing. The baptism of John, from whom did it come? From heaven? Or from men?"

The men who had come from the council were struck dumb. If they said it came from heaven, fault would return upon themselves, because they had not believed him; and if they said it came from men, the people, who believed him to be a prophet, would make a disturbance. Then the leaders of the Pharisees stepped forward with a question.

"Is it right to pay tax to Caesar, or is it not right?"

"Render to Caesar the things of Caesar, and to God the things of God."

Next the priests of the Sadducees stepped forward.

"What do you think of the resurrection of the dead? If brothers die one after another, and a woman lives with seven men, then when they rise again, with which one will she live?"

"God is the God of the living, and therefore does not abandon the dead. When we are raised, there will be no need to bear children, and so there is no need to be anxious about marriage."

Then a lawyer stepped forward.

"To love God and to love one's neighbor."

Jesus' answers were so astonishing that those who heard them could only click their tongues in amazement, yet all those answers were ones that denied the independence of Judea. He did not oppose paying taxes to Caesar; his doctrine of resurrection did not satisfy the Jews who desired salvation in this present world; and his claim that above all things love must come first poured cold water on the hatred of the people, who longed for revenge against Rome.

About the time when the fever of the people began to wither, another rumor began spreading among them. It was that the Sanhedrin council had already designated Jesus a heretic and decided to arrest and execute him. Yet to the disciples, whose eyes had grown dark with desire for power and who had pitched a wall about Jesus, that information was not getting through.

But there were people who heard this rumor and came seeking Jesus. Because the disciples' wall was so thick that it was hard even to gain an audience with Jesus, these men asked Philip to arrange for them to meet him. They were Hellenes who had come from far-off Hellas in order to meet Jesus. In the end Philip went again to Andrew, who stood above him, and entrusted him with arranging the audience of the Hellenes with Jesus, and Andrew reported this matter to Jesus so that the audience was finally granted. As soon as they met Jesus, the Hellenes said in a secret voice,

"Teacher, you must hide yourself."

The Hellenes explained the danger of the situation and said that if Jesus would go with them to Hellas, they would provide him a place of refuge.

"The decisive reason the Sanhedrin council came to act in this way was the incident of the raising of Lazarus. Therefore the household of Lazarus too will have to flee together."

After hearing all their words, Jesus said to the Hellenes,

"Which commandment in the Law do you consider important?"

"I truly thank you that you are showing such concern for me. I entrust well into your hands the household of Lazarus."

"Then, Teacher, you yourself...."

"Can I flee, when the day of glory has drawn near? Night must come if dawn is to come; and unless one grain of wheat falls into the ground and dies, it cannot bear fruit."

In the end, the Hellenes could not persuade Jesus and withdrew. Saying that it was Jesus' instruction, they took Lazarus and his sisters and departed for Hellas. The household of Lazarus later moved again to the region of Lugdunum in Gaul.

That day Jesus and his disciples had to sleep out in Bethany. Because of the rumor that only Galileans would sit in the high places of the new kingdom of Jesus, the rumor that the Sanhedrin had decided to kill Jesus, and the rumor concerning the household of Lazarus, who had suddenly disappeared, none of the people of Bethany received Jesus; and because the household of Lazarus had already left Jerusalem with the Hellenes, they could not even obtain a proper breakfast. Until the sun was high in the sky, Jesus, having gone without breakfast, crossed over to the village of Bethphage and looked about, thinking at least to pick some fig fruit and satisfy his hunger. The name Bethphage in Hebrew meant house of figs. That was how many fig trees there were in Bethphage. But because both Bethphage and Bethany lay on the road into Jerusalem, many travelers took figs there to satisfy hunger, so it was difficult to obtain fruit there at all. Jesus said to the disciples,

"...The fig tree that bears no fruit will be cut down and cast into the fire."

This teaching of his continued again in the temple of Jerusalem. He stood before the treasury of the temple of Jerusalem watching as people cast in their offerings, and seeing a poor widow put in two small coins, he opened his mouth.

"This temple will soon be thrown down."

There was something that Jehovah, the God of the Jews, had entrusted to them. It was that they should welcome the stranger and care for the orphan and the widow. Yet there was no fruit on the fig tree meant for the traveler, and the widow of the temple held two small coins with which she could not even buy one loaf of bread. It was proof that the temple of Jerusalem was not doing the work appointed to it. If the temple failed to do its work, it was plain that God would not leave that temple alone. Jesus continued speaking.

"Jerusalem will be surrounded by armies and trampled down."

If the temple was destroyed, then Jerusalem too would soon be cast away. Jesus again foretold that the tribulation of Jerusalem would expand to the whole world.

"The sun will be darkened, and the moon will lose its light, and the stars of heaven will fall, and after great tribulation the Son of Man will come riding upon the clouds."

John stepped forward and asked Jesus,

"Teacher, when will that be?"

To him, it was always that when that mattered. Jesus answered.

"It will be when the branch of the fig tree grows tender and new leaves come forth."

About the time when the gaze of the people of Jerusalem toward Jesus had grown cold and lifeless, a small disturbance arose within the city. A band of Zealots had attacked Roman soldiers in broad daylight. Compared with other times, the disturbance of that day had several strange points. First, it had taken place not at night but in broad daylight; and second, it took place before the very temple of Jerusalem where Jesus was. At a glance the rising of the Zealots was so reckless that, after causing several casualties on both sides, it was quickly suppressed, and three Zealots were arrested by the Roman army.

As the three were being taken away, they shouted toward the people.

"People of Jerusalem, rise up! How long will you go on like this?"

"We must begin, and then God too will help. Will you sit still and die?"

And one of them shouted, as though meaning Jesus to hear it,

"Jesus of Nazareth, if you are the Messiah, save us!"

Needless to say, this incident was a stratagem manufactured by the Zealot party in order to hasten Jesus toward an uprising. Judging that Jesus was deliberately delaying the undertaking, they had brought about this incident. Of course, this was closely linked with the information of Simon, the party member they had planted among Jesus' followers, and with that of his friends Thaddaeus and Judas. But witnessing this incident, Jesus did not so much as blink an eye. The disciples, who had been looking on with grieved eyes as the Zealots were dragged away, breathed deep sighs and then said to their Teacher,

"Teacher... where will you keep the Passover supper?"

When Passover came, the Jews had the custom of reenacting exactly the acts of their ancestors, who had eaten unleavened bread and drunk wine when they came out from Egypt. But now, because even the household of Lazarus was gone and there was no house to receive them, the disciples asked in that way.

Yet Jesus had already prepared a second place. He called Peter and John and said,

"If you go into the place where the city gate stands, a man carrying a jar of water will be waiting for you. Follow that man and ask the owner of the house whether the preparations for Passover are all ready."

Only later did they understand that there had been another reason why Jesus sent those two. It was precisely Peter and John who had disliked most of all that Jesus should lodge in the house of Lazarus, and so Jesus, by sending those two first, was showing them an astonishing consideration: that they might inspect beforehand whether the house he had newly appointed suited their hearts.

Indeed, at the gate there was a man waiting for them with a water jar, and he led Peter and John to a very large house. The owner of that house was a widow who had inherited a comfortable estate from her husband, and her name too was the common name Mary. Peter and John had no objection to using that house. Since the owner was a widow, she was not a rival with whom they had to contend for power; and the widow's son Mark was still young, so he too was not a competitor. Only after obtaining their approval did Jesus at last rise in order to go to the widow's house.

The upper room of the widow's house was very large and spacious, making it a most suitable place for Jesus and the twelve disciples to take supper. And already the widow Mary, together with her maids, had prepared everything for the evening meal for Jesus' company. When they had all taken their seats, Rhoda, Mary’s maid, came out carrying water for washing the guests' feet. The Jews wear shoes that leave the toes exposed, so when they are invited to another person's house, they have the custom of washing their feet first.

As Rhoda brought the basin, Jesus suddenly rose from his seat, took off his outer garment, and wrapped a towel around his waist. Then he commanded all the disciples to put out their feet. He began to wash the feet of the disciples, who did not know what to do. Peter, bewildered, drew up his feet when his turn came and cried out,

"I... I will not be washed."

Then Jesus shot a look at Peter's face and said,

"Do you intend to cut off your relationship with me?"

At the Teacher's dreadful question, Peter could not move and stretched out his feet. As Jesus washed the feet of the disciples, a gentle expression of compassion appeared again on his face.

When the washing of the feet was finished, Jesus put his outer garment on again and said,

"You too, wash one another's feet. That is the kingdom of heaven."

Then the supper began. Jesus broke the unleavened bread eaten at Passover and distributed it to the disciples, saying,

"I give you my flesh."

and again, as he passed around the cup of wine, he said,

"I give you my blood also."

The disciples did not understand at all what Jesus meant by speaking such serious words, and after the cup had gone around a few times, they again began arguing with one another about who ought to occupy which place if Jesus' regime were to be established again. Jesus again soothed the disciples with warm words.

"You must become those who serve in this world if you are to be esteemed in the kingdom of heaven. But this matter will come only after the pain of a grain of wheat rotting...." He fell into thought for a moment and then said again,

"The truth loses its light when there is trouble from within. Truth is an only child, so if it is divided, it grows dark. There will certainly arise among you one who sells the truth."

As he said this, Jesus was shooting sharp eyes at Peter.

"Peter.... Even now Satan is targeting you. I will pray for you. You must hold fast to your beloved brothers."

Peter, feeling his heart painfully pierced, said in an agitated voice, "I... even if I die, I will not betray you."

"I hope so.... But tonight, before the rooster crows, you will deny that you know me."

Peter looked at John and signaled to him with his eyes. It meant: how on earth did suspicion fall upon me?

John happened to be seated slantwise toward Jesus, so he asked him in a low voice,

"Teacher, who is the one who will sell the truth?"

"One of those who dips his hand into the dish with me will betray me."

Jesus said no more and fell into thought for a while. He was quiet, like a man waiting for the night. Then the disciples, each one with a pierced heart, stepped forward one by one and began asking Jesus questions.

"Teacher, is it I?"

"...Is it I?"

Yet they were all the people who, a few hours later, would abandon their Teacher and flee. The last person to step forward and ask Jesus was Judas.

"Teacher, is it I?"

His expression was full of anguish. Judas, who had been rebuked by Jesus at the house of Simon the leper, had in a fit of anger promised Caiaphas that he would denounce his Teacher, but now his thoughts were gradually finding reasons and being dressed up as something more and more just. Judas concluded that Jesus was deliberately avoiding stepping forward before the people for whatever reason, and that in order to put him forward he had to be brought out before the people. So he contacted the Zealots and proposed that they stir up an uproar to bring about the appearance of the Messiah. But the Zealot disturbance that had taken place in front of Jesus had come to nothing without eliciting any participation from Jesus, and only three party members, including Barabbas, were arrested.

In the end, the final method for making Jesus appear as the Messiah was to drive him into a corner so that he would either stand up by his own power or be thrown down.

That was why Judas was now agonizing within a strange logic that Jesus had to be driven into the position of leader for the sake of the Jewish people.

Jesus looked steadily at Judas. And before long his eyes were filled with sorrow. When Judas realized that those eyes were directed toward him with such deep compassion, he could not bear to meet them and lowered his head. Jesus said in a gentle voice,

"I.... I know your suffering. The suffering of living in this world.... at times it is so overwhelming that one may even think it would have been better never to have been born...."

As Judas heard those words, the same loneliness as Jesus' own began to surge in his heart. He looked at Jesus with reddened, bloodshot eyes. Judas was seeing his own self in Jesus' lonely figure. In the image of Jesus comforting him, Judas was discovering his own sorrow. A hot tremor was rising from the depths of his heart. Jesus quietly broke off a piece of bread, dipped it in the sauce, and handed it to Judas.

"...Do not be in distress; do what you wish to do. I have already forgiven you."

Judas took the piece of bread given by his Teacher and stared at it for a moment, then, wiping the glistening moisture at the corner of his eye with his fist, he rose and went outside. Outside it was already a pitch-black night.

After Judas went out, Jesus began his final sermon. Jesus' sermon was beginning with two words of Greek.

"Agapate allelus.... Love one another. Just as I have loved you, so you too must love one another."

Because Jesus' voice was so gentle and calm, all the disciples began to grow solemn.

"As I have said many times, in a little while you will no longer be able to see me. Where I am going now, you cannot follow, but later you will follow."

Peter stepped forward and said,

"Lord, where are you going?"

This question was one that, thirty-four years later, Peter, as he was fleeing Rome, would repeat once more when he met a vision of Jesus on the Appian Way.

After receiving this question from Peter, Jesus again began to explain about the kingdom of heaven.

"You ask where the kingdom of heaven is. But the kingdom of heaven cannot be expressed by saying that it is here or that it comes at such and such a time. That is because the kingdom of heaven is the place where God is. I will prepare a place for you in the kingdom of heaven. I am the way, the truth, and also the life. Therefore no one can enter the kingdom of heaven without going through me. I will not leave you as orphans. Whatever you ask in my name, you will receive.... I am the vine, you are the branches, and the Father of the kingdom of heaven is the farmer. You must be together with the vine, bearing many beautiful fruits and bringing glory to the Father. If a branch falls away from the vine, it will wither and dry up and be cast aside and burned...."

After Jesus ended his final sermon, he lifted his eyes to heaven and prayed.

"Now I have delivered to them all the words that the Father gave me. I ask you, Father.... just as the Father and I are one, let them also become one in truth...."

Having spoken thus far, John again looked up at the night sky and drew in his breath. In the midst of everyone's quiet, suddenly from one corner came the sound of someone sobbing. It was the crying of a woman. Everyone looked toward the place from which the sobbing came. In the light of the torches held high by the people, the woman crying with her face covered was Milga, the wife of Maximinus from Patmos. John recognized her and opened his mouth.

"What is the matter, Milga?"

Milga removed her hands from her face and said to John,

"Apostle.... Those who believe in Jesus will enter the kingdom of heaven together with the vine, but what will happen to the Jews of our own country? Are our poor compatriots, who crucified that Jesus, to be abandoned forever? What of the Jewish people, who have gone through every kind of hardship and stretched out their necks while waiting for the Messiah?"

John was staring blankly at Milga's tear-streaked face. With the very same eyes that Jesus had used when looking at John, John was looking at Milga.

"Madam.... In truth, I too agonized over that matter for a long time. My concern in following Jesus.... was only the independence of Judea and revenge against Rome. Yet Jesus never once, even to the very end, spoke any word about his own people, that is, the Jews. Even while all my fellow workers who began with my own brother James were dying and many Christiani were dying in order to convey Jesus' words, I could not break free from that question. Even if Jesus' disciples and all Christiani are sacrificed for the salvation of humanity, for the fruit of the vine and the glory of God, what of the Jews? What of our pitiful nation, which has risked its life to keep the law of God?"

That indeed remained an unsolved riddle. At the Last Supper, Jesus had completed all preparations, yet there had been no mention of the Jews, his own country's people. John continued his words.

"But.... hearing your words now, one thought comes to me."

"If one looks closely at the Gospel of Mark, which was written after hearing Peter's account.... after Jesus had delivered his final sermon at the supper table, he went out with the disciples to a garden called Gethsemane. There Jesus left the other disciples to wait, and taking Peter and my brother James and me, the three of us, he walked a little farther. In the Gospel of Mark, at that time Jesus suddenly became greatly alarmed and sorrowful and said, 'My heart is exceedingly troubled, even to the point of death...' and going a little farther, he fell to the ground, put his face to the earth, and began an earnest prayer...."

Polycarp, who was supporting John at his side and helping him because he was struggling, added his own words.

"That is right. Jesus prayed.... 'If it be possible, let this cup pass from me. Yet not as I will, but as you will....'"

"Now I remember too.... That was certainly a sudden change. As Jesus walked with the three of us into the woods, his face suddenly turned ashen, and as if startled he gathered his two hands together and his whole body trembled.... Then he began to pray, and saying that if possible this cup...."

Polycarp asked again,

"Perhaps Jesus suddenly remembered some matter?"

"Yes. You were right. Perhaps Jesus was thinking about the final sermon he had spoken just a little earlier at the supper table...."

Then John suddenly brightened and called Papias.

"Papias.... Can you recite the words of the prophet Micah?"

Papias, who was writing down John's story on a sheet of papyrus, scratched the back of his head and said uncertainly,

"Well.... which passage do you mean?"

"From the line that begins, 'In the latter days....' I would like you to recite a few lines...."

Papias tilted his head and began to recite the prophecy of the prophet Micah haltingly.

In the latter days the mountain of the house of Jehovah will firmly stand atop the mountains

and be lifted above the little hills, and the nations will stream to it.

Indeed many peoples will go and say,

Come, let us go up to the mountain of Jehovah and to the house of the God of Jacob, and he will teach us his way. We will walk in his path. For the law will go out from Zion, and the word of Jehovah from Jerusalem. He will judge among many peoples and decide for strong nations far away; they will beat their swords into plowshares and their spears into pruning hooks, and nation will not lift up sword against nation again, and each person will sit under his own vine and under his own fig tree....

When Papias had recited that far with difficulty, John raised his hand and stopped him.

"Enough, that is exactly the passage.... In the latter days.... on that last day, each person will sit under his own vine and under his own fig tree.... Those who will sit under the vine are the Christiani who are carrying the fruits of glory.... and who are those who will sit under the fig tree? Could it not be that the sacrifice God prepared for the Gentiles was the fig fruit planted outside the gate for the sake of travelers and strangers....?"

The listeners were opening their eyes wide at John's astonishing reasoning.

"Jesus was remembering that very word that Papias had just recited. That is why, on the dawn of the day when we first met him, he was praying under the fig tree, and he also praised Nathanael, who was praying under the fig tree, as a true Israelite.... The fig was precisely Israel."

John paused again and, gathering his thoughts, said,

"God declared to Pharaoh of Egypt, Israel is my firstborn son. And Paul described Jesus as the firstborn among all brothers. .... Then the Jewish people were a people chosen as the sacrificial lamb who must accompany Jesus on the road of death.... That was the way of the fig tree planted for others, and the way of the firstborn. Jesus, even then, was thinking only of the logic of a single grain of wheat dying, and expected that the salvation of all humanity would be accomplished as his own solitary cross.... But in the Garden of Gethsemane a tremendous turning point occurred."

People were listening closely to John's urgent reasoning and could scarcely even breathe.

"The path of the cross run by one alone.... that is no different from the path of the world's common heroes. Anyone can do such a thing. If by the sacrifice of my own body a great work can be accomplished.... anyone can meet a heroic death. That is why Jesus' attitude could remain calm when speaking about his own death.... But God is not a dictator who handles matters by himself. He is the perfect ruler.... the one who brings about good through cooperation with all his people.... He desired to be one among the crowd, not a hero.... He desired to be one among all the suffering Christiani and Jews, not a hero on the cross...."

Only then did the eyes of those listening to John's words begin to grow bright with life. At last the God who had ruled from the high throne was now descending to the low place and existing among them.

Therefore Jesus had to journey together with all the Christiani and all the Jews. In the case of the Christiani, the fruit of the vine, glory, and joy would be given as their reward even in suffering. But to the fig fruit planted for others there was neither glory nor joy. The descendants of Isaac, the son who escaped being offered as a sacrifice on Mount Moriah, had now come to the very way of the firstborn that was Jesus. John began reciting the prophecy of the prophet Isaiah. It was precisely a prophecy pointing to both Jesus and the Jews.

Surely he has borne our griefs and carried our sorrows, yet we thought that he was stricken, struck by God, and afflicted. He was pierced for our transgressions; he was crushed for our iniquities.

Upon him was the chastisement that made us have peace,

and by his stripes we are healed. We all like sheep have gone astray; we have turned every one to his own way, and Jehovah has laid on him the iniquity of us all. He was oppressed and afflicted, yet he opened not his mouth; like a lamb that is led to the slaughter, and like a sheep before its shearers is silent, so he opened not his mouth....

While John was reciting the prophecy of Isaiah, Milga was sobbing with her shoulders heaving. John looked at her and said,

"Milga, the suffering of the Jewish people.... will continue until all the Gentiles enter as people of the kingdom of heaven."

"Apostle.... Is this not far too unfair? Why should the Jewish people, who have served God with their lives at stake, suffer such a thing?"

"That is the way of the firstborn, to share in the Father's pain.... Therefore God disciplined Cain, who protested against that unfair treatment, cursed Esau, who carelessly abandoned the rights of the firstborn, and repeatedly stripped Manasseh of the firstborn's portion.... and in this way he has continued teaching unconditional obedience to his firstborn, the Jews...."

It was indeed a great march of the cross. It was the march of vine and fig tree, and no one knew how long it would last. Looking up at heaven, John spoke.

"Lord.... forgive me for hating Lazarus. Lazarus was the representative of the Jews who must walk the path of thorns without light...."

Jesus had once preached about a beggar named Lazarus who begged at the gate of a rich house. Though he had done nothing good in life, he was recognized for that merit of having spent his life as a beggar in order to give passersby a chance, and thus he entered Paradise.

So Jesus' prayer began to change, with Gethsemane as the turning point. Because the suffering of the cross was not his alone, he could not gladly accept it. There were contained within it the nameless sufferings of countless fig trees.

That is why he asked God whether this could be delayed, or whether there was no way to escape it. But God's time was pressing in like a flood. In the march of Jesus and the vine and all the fig trees, the death and resurrection of truth and the night and dawn of creation were advancing beyond control.

"At last Jesus was arrested by Judas' denunciation. Truth was arrested by those who came with swords and clubs...."

Peter, who still believed in Jesus' authority, drew his sword and struck the ear of Malchus, the servant of the high priest. But Jesus rebuked Peter.

"Put your sword back into its sheath. All who take up the sword will perish by the sword."

Jesus was arrested, and the disciples scattered in all directions. John, at a loss, grabbed the sleeve of Peter, who was standing there bewildered, and pulled him.

John had two reasons for standing by without telling Jesus even though he knew Judas's intentions. One was that by letting Judas's betrayal go he could eliminate one rival. The other was that he hoped that if Jesus were driven into a corner as Judas thought, he would ultimately reveal himself as the Messiah. Therefore, now that Jesus had been arrested and was beginning to be driven into a corner, John had to do not the fleeing, but the following of his trail and the watching of how events changed.

Papias stopped writing John's stories on the sheet of papyrus and lifted his head.

"Apostle.... What was the name of the person whom Peter struck with the sword again?"

"Malchus.... His name was Malchus, the servant who worked in Annas's house."

In the records of Mark and Matthew the name does not appear, and the same was true in Luke's record. The young Mark had followed Jesus that night and fallen asleep covered with a single blanket, then fled naked when the crowd came; Matthew too had fled, and so he did not remember these events. But John had watched every detail of them, and in particular Malchus was a servant in Annas's house, where John had always come and gone, so he knew him clearly.

# Empire and Heaven (II)

## Chapter 8. The Gospel of John (1)

As they listened to his confession, all the people were startled. It was because of the new fact that John, the supreme leader of the Christiani, had once had a blood feud with Vespasianus.

Beginning with the time he met the Greek girl Sophia by the seashore of Tiberias, he explained in detail everything: how he and the man called John of Gischala, who bore the same name as himself, had competed over Sophia; how he had received a shell as a gift from her; and how, after she was violated by Vespasianus, he resolved upon revenge.

"...In order to take revenge on Vespasianus, and more broadly on the Roman Empire, John of Gischala entered the Zealot party with all his heart. But... because even after Sophia disappeared my spirit of rivalry toward John of Gischala was still alive, I wanted to walk a different road from his. So I chose to part from him and follow John the Baptizer, the one said to have appeared in the wilderness of Judea...."

The confession of an old man who had lived long years was shaking the hearts of the people. And the ground beneath the soles of their feet was still trembling faintly from the aftershock of the earthquake. John continued speaking as slowly as he could.

"...My whole life... was a life mottled with jealousy.... From birth I envied my brother James, I envied John of Gischala... and I spent my whole life with the obsession of revenge against Vespasianus.... Everyone who was around me became an object of my jealousy. Even after I entered the company of Jesus of Nazareth, that life of jealousy continued. Judas Iscariot, Lazarus, Peter, and even Paul, whom I met only after the Lord was no longer present, I envied and hated...."

Polycarp, who was supporting John's body, gave a look to Papias, who was sitting blankly. Papias had forgotten the task he was supposed to do. In haste he brought out the papyrus sheets and ink that he always carried ready. By then it had become very dark, and someone lit a flame and began raising torches.

"...When John of Gischala, whose knife wound had been treated in Sophia's house, entered the Zealot party, I at once set out to seek the prophet who had appeared in the land of Judea. But... before I met that prophet, I first went to see Annas, who was leading the Sanhedrin council in Jerusalem...."

Annas was the real power who had long been directing the Sanhedrin council from behind. Twenty-one years earlier he had risen to the position of high priest and remained in that place for nine years, until he was deposed by Governor Gratus, yet he had been the leader of the council. Even after his removal his power was not slight, so much so that not only was Caiaphas, who entered afterward as high priest, his son-in-law, but even his five sons and his grandson had all risen into the priestly ranks. Therefore, though Caiaphas was the high priest at that time, it was Annas, as the council's actual leader, who was exercising tremendous influence.

Moreover, because Annas was a man of outstanding organizational ability and political skill, he was secretly maintaining ties not only in Jerusalem but also with powerful men in the region of Galilee.

There was a political reason for the religious leaders, including Annas, to maintain relations with influential men of Galilee. After Jerusalem had been ravaged by Egypt and Babylon, it continued to be ruled by the Medes and Persians, and then by Alexander of Macedon; and the period of severest affliction was the time of King Antiochus, who received Judea as part of Alexander's divided dominion. He pursued a policy of Hellenizing the Jews and did not hesitate to carry out harsh religious persecution. It was Judah Maccabee who rebelled against this Antiochian dynasty and rose up. In the end he succeeded in driving Antiochus's power out of Judea and purifying the temple, but in less than a hundred years the Maccabean dynasty collapsed, and Judea again fell into the hands of Rome.

At last the Jews grew weary of such continuing affliction, and they began to doubt God's promise that He would preserve Judea forever. In the end they had no choice but to cooperate with the Roman government, which at least guaranteed freedom of religion, and many among the people who rebelled against this went scattering into various places, Galilee among them.

Among these Jews who had left Jerusalem and Judea and become the diaspora, that is, the scattered Jews, the group that had to be watched most carefully was the Jews of Galilee. Galilee had become the center of the dissatisfied forces, and it was also Galilee that served as the center of activity for the Zealot party, which was carrying on underground resistance against Rome.

The dissatisfied forces of Galilee, including the Zealots, were a deeply troubling existence for the religious leaders of Jerusalem. They could not help acknowledging them to some degree, because they were struggling for the independence of the nation and therefore it was necessary to be allied with them; yet because they were resisting Rome, they could not help being on guard against them because of the danger.

That was why Annas, a political man, had to maintain relations with several leading figures of the Galilean region.

Zebedee, who owned a considerable number of fishing boats at Capernaum, was a fairly well-established notable in Galilee. Moreover, because his wife Salome was related by marriage to Annas, he naturally stood in connection with him. Thus, when his son John set out to seek the prophet who had appeared in the wilderness of Judea, Zebedee advised him first to go up to Jerusalem and meet Annas, father-in-law of Caiaphas.

As John went into Judea, he took with him Simon, who was from Bethsaida and worked on their boat, and Simon's younger brother Andrew. The reason John took them and left without taking his own brother James was precisely the rivalry that had existed from childhood. He never informed James of the work he himself was doing. Moreover, because John was the son of Zebedee, an influential man of Capernaum and an owner of boats, he stood in the position of leader among the party; and if he went together with his elder brother James, it was obvious that even that order would be disturbed.

"So then, if that prophet really is the Messiah, what do you intend to do?"

Annas asked that while noting that John looked very young. The only reason he was even bothering to deal with such a mere stripling as John was out of consideration for Zebedee, his little contact in Capernaum.

"If he truly is the Messiah sent by God, then according to Nathan's prophecy he will be the Son of God who establishes the everlasting kingdom of David. If so, all the leaders of this nation must aid his work. It is for precisely that purpose that I have come here. And if he is the Messiah, then the Sanhedrin council too ought rightly to assist the new king and build the kingdom of God in this land."

Still looking John up and down, Annas said,

"You mean... that you intend to become an adviser to the Messiah? Have you come alone from Capernaum?"

"No. I have brought with me two men who can follow my word."

"Three men, then...."

Murmuring the number three and sweeping John himself together with them, Annas nodded carelessly. He was already considering how he might use the three rustics who had come up from the frontier. Deliberately setting his face in seriousness, he said,

"Concerning the Messiah who is to be born from the line of David, we all have already waited many long years. The advent of the Messiah is our Jewish people's tenacious hope and fervent desire. As one of the Sanhedrin, I send praise to your active conduct. And so..."

He leaned his upper body forward and lowered his voice.

"There is something I wish to ask of you. As you know, it is difficult for us religious leaders to go about carelessly and meet each person who calls himself a prophet one by one. But if you go and meet him, and if you come to hold the conviction that he is the Messiah, I want you to make a detailed report of his actions. Then we, on the basis of your report, will compare them with the prophecies of Scripture and verify them, and afterward we will support your work."

John was moved by Annas's proposal, which seemed exceedingly cautious. He agreed to keep in contact with Annas from time to time and withdrew from his house.

Yet within Annas's intention of using John were included several other calculations. First, Annas was not a naive person like the young John, and he was not expecting anything like the advent of the Messiah. Even at the time when Judah Maccabee had succeeded in his struggle against King Antiochus, the religious leaders of this nation had still been unable to cast away their expectation concerning the Messiah. But through the history that followed, from the collapse of the Maccabean dynasty to the domination of Rome, they were now left with no choice but to interpret the prophecies concerning the advent of the Messiah as a kind of symbolic consolation. That is, they could only understand the promise of the Messiah as no more than the encouragement of the prophets meant to comfort the suffering Jewish nation.

The psychological resistance that the religious leaders had toward the Messiah may perhaps have been because they did not in fact desire his coming. For, in order to preserve religion, they had compromised with Rome, and their faith had already lost much of the purity of the age of the Law; the self-reproach they felt for that was making them all the more set forth the Law. They fasted twice in seven days, gave a tenth of their income, prayed where many could see them, and always washed their hands before eating.

In this way they came to assure themselves that no one such as the Messiah would come, and even reached the point of believing that if anyone arose claiming to be the Messiah, he must be guarded against. For anyone who stepped forward as a prophet would, without exception, seek the support of the people by digging up and exposing the sore places of the religious leaders.

The man called John who had appeared in the wilderness of Judea was the same. This strange man, said to wear garments of camel's hair, gird himself with leather, and live by eating locusts, had abruptly begun by delivering an oracle that men must repent. He demanded that the gathered people confess their sins and repent, and to those who repented he gave baptism in the Jordan River. To the religious leaders who had gone out to see this scene, he had hurled abuse, saying,

"Brood of vipers, do you think you can escape the wrath of God that is at hand? If you do not bear fruit worthy of repentance, you will be cast into the fire."

Annas judged that this man too was challenging the authority of the leaders in order to win the popularity of the people, and he thought it must not be left alone long. Only, because there were still many people yearning for the Messiah who followed him, he could not lay hands on him carelessly. Thus Annas sent John to spy on the John in the wilderness so that he might seize a decisive opportunity.

But John too, though young, was not so easy a person. As he bowed politely to Annas and withdrew, he had already perceived all his real intention. Yet in any case it was necessary for John to keep up a connection with Annas. For to John, his hope fixed on the Messiah was frail, while the power of Annas was certain.

Having gone out into the wilderness of Judea, John was introduced by Simon to a man named Philip, Simon's townsman. Philip too had come up from Galilee, together with his friend Nathanael of Cana, after hearing the rumor of John the Baptizer. John instructed Simon to act together with them and win them over to his side, and then, taking only Andrew with him, he went to John and first received baptism. Then, by skillful methods, he approached John the Baptizer and became one of his close men. Whenever he approached someone, he always seemed like a youthful and innocent young man, with none of his hot blood visible at all. He always captivated the teacher with intelligent questions and had the talent of drawing others to himself. John the Baptizer had many disciples, but in a short time he had passed over the older ones and become a central figure.

When John and Andrew had entered as disciples of John the Baptizer and were establishing their place, several men sent from the Sanhedrin council came down from Jerusalem. It was in order to identify John the Baptizer, threaten him, and make it so that the people would no longer follow him. They asked John,

"Who are you?"

There were several meanings contained in that question. Together with the question of whether he was truly the Messiah, it also included the interrogating question of what sort of swindler he was and from where he had rolled in.

John already knew what they were asking. Therefore he answered them, who were bending toward him with the question, in a straight manner.

"I am not the Christ."

Christ, in Greek, meant Messiah, that is, "the anointed one." The prophet Daniel had described the Savior who would come to save the Jews with this word Messiah.

"Then who are you? Are you the Prophet?"

"No."

"We must go back and report who you are. Who do you think yourself to be?"

"You will have read the prophecy of the prophet Isaiah. I am the voice in the wilderness, come to prepare the way of Christ."

"The voice in the wilderness...?"

The religious leaders questioning John did not understand his words very well. Yet in any case, since they had extracted from John's own lips the answer that he was not the Christ, their purpose was achieved, and they returned to the city.

As they had predicted, many among the crowds who had followed John were disappointed by his answer and left him. And the one more disappointed than any of them was the young John who had come seeking him from Capernaum. Though John of the wilderness was speaking of Christ who would come again, to the people of Jerusalem, who were so worn out by prophecies concerning not the present but some future time yet to come, such words gave not the least hope. John of Capernaum was on the verge of having to pack up his things together with his friends and go back home.

But on that very day another event occurred that caught John's steps and held them fast. A rustic-looking young man said to be from Nazareth came out to the Jordan River and asked John to baptize him. But for some reason John was hesitating and refusing to baptize him. Yet the country young man said in a somewhat firm tone,

"It is fitting that we fulfill the will of God in this way."

John seemed as if seized by his words, as though led by some invisible power. He gave baptism, and the young man who had received it left that place. To John, who after he left was standing there as though out of his mind, his disciples asked,

"Teacher, who was that young man just now?"

John the Baptizer looked into the empty air for a long while and then opened his mouth.

"...The Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world."

John of Capernaum at once grasped the meaning of those words. In Isaiah, the Messiah was prophesied to become the lamb who would bear the sins of the people of the world.

"When he received baptism and walked up out of the water... I saw the Holy Spirit like a dove come down from heaven and remain upon his head...."

John the Baptizer was trembling too much to say anything more. John of Capernaum quickly pulled Andrew by the sleeve and left that place. It was to find the country youth who had disappeared.

Not far from the Jordan River where John the Baptizer was administering baptism, he met the young man they were looking for. The youth, noticing that John and Andrew were following him, turned around and opened his mouth.

"What is it that you want?"

Because he suddenly turned and asked them that, John and Andrew were greatly flustered. So the words that John answered on impulse became instead a question in return.

"Teacher, where are you staying?"

Then the young man looked quietly into John's eyes as though he meant to pierce them, and opened his mouth.

"...Come and see."

So the young man and the two began walking together. And there was one more man following behind them: Simon, Andrew's brother. He had originally remained with Philip and Nathanael and the others, but when he heard that John and Andrew had gone after some person, he could not endure his restlessness and had followed behind. Unavoidably Andrew fell a little behind and said to his brother Simon in a low voice,

"That man seems to be the Messiah."

"You fool, if so, you should have told me sooner."

Simon began striding forward. Andrew, with no choice, chased after his brother and again introduced Simon to that young man. The man halted his steps a moment, looked at Simon, and nodded. He seemed greatly impressed by Simon's eagerness in following after him without waiting for his younger brother's introduction.

"You said your name is Simon? You will become Peter."

Simon, hearing those words, was bewildered. The young man spoke with great compression, so it was too difficult for Simon to understand. Peter in Greek meant a rock.

The young man was comparatively a man of few words. John and Simon and Andrew also found it awkward to begin speaking first, so until they reached Jericho they scarcely spoke at all. The young man entered the city, then led them to a small house outside the gate where a fig tree stood. Though no one had sent any message, a young man came out from the door of that house and was greeting him respectfully.

He introduced that young man to John and his party.

"This is Lazarus, who lives in Bethany."

After greeting the young man called Lazarus, John introduced Simon and Andrew to him. Lazarus led them inside.

"Please come in. This house belongs to my relative, so you may stay at ease."

Only after entering the house and sitting down as Lazarus urged did John look at the young man he had met in the wilderness.

"Teacher... where have you come from?"

John was already using for the second time the word where. This word where, together with when, became words that would bewilder John all his life.

Those two words were always the most important things to John. His only concern was when he would avenge himself upon the Roman Empire, which had given power to Vespasianus who violated Sophia, and where he would strike them down.

The young man looked quietly at John and answered briefly.

"I am Jesus, from Nazareth."

"Nazareth...."

John was inwardly disappointed. It was because in the writing of the prophet Micah it was said that the Messiah would be born in Bethlehem.

After the introductions were done, Jesus began speaking together with Lazarus. John too, together with Simon and Andrew, was listening to his words. He said many things, but there was nothing about the restoration of the sovereignty of Judea or about plans of operation for conquering Rome.

He was speaking chiefly about the kingdom of heaven.

"...The kingdom of heaven belongs to the poor in spirit. In the kingdom of heaven, because the owner and the enemy have sown different seeds, when the harvest comes the tares and the grain will be separated. The kingdom of heaven is like a single mustard seed planted in the ground, so small at first that it cannot even be seen, yet when it grows it becomes a great tree. The kingdom of heaven is like leaven mixed into flour, swelling and spreading while no one knows it. The kingdom of heaven is like treasure hidden in a field, so that the man who finds it sells all he owns and buys that field. The kingdom of heaven is like a pearl of great price found after much seeking, worthy to be exchanged for everything a man has...."

Those were words difficult to understand not only for Simon and Andrew but even for clever John. What they wanted to hear was about the nation of Judea. Their hope was that the Messiah, descended from David and born in Bethlehem, would appear, raise up the trampled pride of the Jewish nation, subdue the Roman Empire and all Gentile nations, and establish an everlasting kingdom.

But this man, who said he came not from Bethlehem but from Nazareth, was only speaking strange things about the kingdom of heaven rather than the nation of Judea. While he was speaking, the master of this house, a relative of Lazarus, served them supper, and after supper too the words of Jesus continued. At length Simon began to snore while listening to his increasingly puzzling words, and even Andrew's eyes grew unfocused and he began to doze.

Compared with them, John had somewhat more patience. In any case, because John the Baptizer, whom people thought might be the Messiah, had clearly pointed to him as the Lamb of God, John had to identify who he really was.

Yet until deep into the night no more concrete story was coming from Jesus. He and Lazarus seemed to be rather close, yet because John and his party were people he had met only for the first time, perhaps he was being guarded. But Lazarus was still listening to the words of Jesus with eyes bright and shining.

As John sat fighting sleep while listening to stories in which he had absolutely no interest, stroking his drooping eyelids with the palm of his hand, at some point his consciousness began to blur. However much he tried to keep them open, his eyes kept closing.

To him the nation of Judea was now despair, and night. John the Baptizer had said that he himself was not the Messiah, and Jesus too, the one he had pointed out, was only speaking strange things about the kingdom of heaven and had no interest in the salvation of the Jewish nation. Dark night, together with sleep, was settling over John.

In his dream John met again and again with the pitiable figure of Sophia. And he stabbed the cold face of Vespasianus countless times with a knife. In such desperate struggle, twisting his body, John awoke before dawn had yet broken. Realizing that he had fallen asleep without even knowing it, John sprang up and looked around. Beside him Simon and Andrew were sunk in deep sleep, and at his feet the young man called Lazarus was sleeping. But nowhere in the room could the man called Jesus be seen.

John shook Simon and Andrew, who had been snoring, and woke them. To the two, opening their eyes in confusion, he put his hand to his lips and gestured that they should be quiet, then led them and silently slipped out of the room. Having come out of the room holding their breath, they crossed the yard and moved toward the gate. John, going out first, looked beyond the gate, then turned back and gestured that they should be careful. He had discovered the figure of a man kneeling beneath the fig tree outside the gate. Though it was still dark, John could instinctively tell that it was Jesus. Together with Simon and Andrew, John slipped out of the house with muffled footsteps and quickly left that place.

When they had walked quite some distance, Simon at last asked in a voice that still seemed not fully awake,

"Why did we slip away like thieves in the middle of sleep?"

"It seems to me..."

John, catching his breath, turned to look at Simon.

"It seems to me that man is not the Messiah. I think we fixed upon the wrong person. Listening to what he said last night, they were stories that had nothing at all to do with the Messiah. Perhaps we understood John the Baptizer's words wrongly."

Andrew shook his head and said,

"No, John the Baptizer certainly spoke while pointing to that man. When he came up from the water after baptism, he pointed to him and called him the Lamb of God."

"...We judged wrongly. Besides, did he not say that he came not from Bethlehem or Jerusalem but from Nazareth?"

In truth, even among the towns of Galilee, Nazareth was so insignificant that there were few who even knew the name. Moreover, though many place names appear in the Jewish Scriptures written by Moses and the prophets, the name Nazareth was not mentioned even once.

Simon said in an uneasy voice,

"Still, that man did seem somehow different from ordinary men...."

"Even so, we cannot waste our time because of a man whose origin we do not even know."

Even so, something kept troubling Simon. It sat ill with him that they had secretly slipped away from the young man called Jesus, but even more the words that man had spoken while looking at Simon kept irritating his mind.

(You said your name is Simon? You will become Peter....)

However much he thought about it, he could not understand what it meant. Perhaps John, being clever, had understood, but John had no interest in matters concerning Simon.

In any case, Simon no longer wished to be seized and dragged about by John. In truth, he was a fisherman by nature and a man with no dissatisfaction at all in living by catching fish on the Sea of Galilee. He had no interest whatsoever in things such as independence movements, the Zealot party, or the Messiah. The only reason he had had no choice but to follow John was that John was the son of the boat owner, and it was hard to oppose his word. To speak plainly, he had been unable to understand at all either the words of John the Baptizer or the words of Jesus, that young man from Nazareth.

"What do we do now? If it is neither this nor that, as for Messiah and all that, I would rather go back to Galilee. A fisherman ought simply to catch fish and live...."

Simon said that in a vexed voice, but John still could not give up. If there was no hope that the Messiah would appear, then at least he wanted to draw close to the Sanhedrin council and press forward with revenge against Rome.

"I think it is best to watch the turn of events a little longer. Just yesterday men sent from the Sanhedrin council came to question John, and his preaching too is growing harsher day by day, so it feels as though some change may come. Since we have come this far already, it will not be too late to go back after seeing a little more of how things develop."

While they were muttering in dejection, the day was already beginning to dawn. When they reached even the outside of the city gate, they felt hungry and looked for a fig tree. Originally fig trees were fruit trees grown so that they might provide fruit to travelers passing by. Therefore the Jews did not plant fig trees inside the house, but outside it. In the law of Moses it was said that all grain and fruit, their first fruits and their tithe, were to be offered at the temple, but fig fruit alone did not come under that rule.

But because Jericho was the crossroads connecting Jerusalem north and south and had become a center of traffic and commerce, with many travelers, the figs did not remain on the trees. Especially of late, because people were coming in crowds from every place to see John the Baptizer, that was all the more so.

Searching several trees, they were barely able to obtain one fig each. While John was eating the fruit, red with its tiny seeds, he suddenly made a gesture and hid himself behind a tree. From the direction of the city gate, where people were already moving about in considerable numbers, Jesus, the young man from Nazareth, was walking out. And he was not alone but had two young men with him. Simon looked that way with astonished eyes.

"Why... those are my friends Philip and Nathanael."

John too looked that way in some surprise. They were indeed Philip and Nathanael.

Because they were in the position of having stolen away secretly, they could not show themselves again, so they waited for Jesus and the three to pass by. Jesus was walking while saying something to them. John and Simon and Andrew waited until they had gone a good distance away and then began following behind them. They turned into the north road that went toward Galilee, but for some reason before they had gone far they were taking leave of one another. Jesus began walking into the desolate wilderness, while Philip and Nathanael kept walking on toward Galilee.

When Jesus had gone so far that he could hardly be seen, they quickened their pace and chased after Philip and Nathanael. Simon first called out and stopped Philip, who was walking ahead.

"Philip... how did you come to meet that man called Jesus?"

Recognizing Simon's voice, Philip turned and rather looked puzzled.

"Why, Simon... I heard you had gone to seek that Jesus. What has happened?"

"Well... we... had certain reasons, and so parted from him, but when did you meet him?"

"Just now. Because you did not return, we said perhaps the Messiah had at last appeared, and so we were searching for your whereabouts. Then, hearing someone speak, we found that you three had entered some house in the city together with that young man. At last we found the house, but it was already too late at night, so we decided to lodge at an inn nearby and wait for daylight. At daybreak Nathanael was offering prayer beneath the fig tree outside the inn, and I went near that house and loitered there watching what would happen...."

"And then?"

"That young man came out of the house together with one other man, and after they exchanged greetings they parted. I at once followed him and asked where you had gone. Without even answering my question, he asked where I was going. So I blurted out that I was going to Galilee, and then he said to go together."

John, who had been listening, stepped in and asked again,

"Besides that... did he say anything else?"

Turning to John, Philip said,

"As we were passing near the fig tree where Nathanael was sitting, I asked his leave and went to bring him. I told Nathanael that I had met the one who is called Messiah, and he asked who it was. So I said it was a man named Jesus from Nazareth... and at once this fellow said, could anything excellent come out of Nazareth...."

When he had come that far in the telling, Nathanael nodded and opened his own mouth.

"Because Philip dragged me, I went up to that man. But the moment he saw me, he praised me, saying I was truly an Israelite. So I asked him rather sharply, How do you know me?"

Nathanael's expression was still excited, as though his figure stood vivid before his eyes.

"He said, I saw you when you were under the fig tree...."

The fig tree was a special tree to the Jews. The prophet Micah had written that on the day the Messiah came, everyone would sit beneath his own fig tree and vine.

"But..."

John urged him again,

"But why did you part from him?"

"He said that he still had work to do here. After hearing me say that my home was in Cana, he said that in about two months he would have occasion to stop by Cana, and that I should meet him there. Then he went into that wilderness."

"Into Cana...."

"That is right. He said there would be a wedding in the house of some relative."

After hearing so far, John sent Simon and Andrew back to Galilee together with them. John himself went back again to Jericho, stayed there one more day, and then went up to Jerusalem and reported to Annas what had happened thus far. Annas too, after hearing the story about Jesus of Nazareth, behaved as though it did not interest him and did not even ask further questions.

John took leave of Annas, returned to Capernaum, and after spending two months there, went alone to Cana and found Jesus there, together with his mother Mary. John followed behind them and secretly entered the house of the wedding feast itself to watch their movements. Jesus looked greatly thinner and older than he had two months before. Though John could not show himself before Jesus, Philip and Nathanael were there attending the feast together with him. And when the wedding feast was at its height, something unbelievable occurred.

The wine ran out in the wedding house. The master of the feast stood in distress, not knowing what to do.

Then Mary, the mother of Jesus, said to her son,

"It seems the wine has run out."

Then Jesus quietly looked at his mother and said,

"Mother, my time has not yet come."

But Mary called the servants and instructed them,

"Whatever he tells you, do it."

Jesus sat still for a moment, then told the servants to fill six empty stone jars with water. The servants brought water and poured it into the empty jars. When the pouring of the water was finished, Jesus said again,

"Now draw some out and take it to the master of the feast."

It was truly an astonishing thing. A miracle had occurred. All the guests were delighted and said to the master of the feast that where had anyone ever heard of serving such good wine at the end. John realized that matters had gone wrong. He had judged Jesus too rashly and left him. He was so shocked that he did not know what to do. Among the guests of the wedding feast he caught a glimpse of Sophia's figure, but he could not acknowledge her. As John knew it, she was surely already a woman whose body had been ruined, and for him the shock of witnessing the miracle was greater still.

Even after the feast at Cana, John kept following Jesus in secret. Jesus, who had gone back from Cana to Nazareth, then took his mother and brothers and went to Capernaum. In Capernaum he went here and there looking at houses, and then returned again to Nazareth. Apparently he was preparing to move to Capernaum. But after bringing his family back to Nazareth and while they were preparing for the move, he again took Philip and Nathanael and went up to Jerusalem.

When Jesus arrived in Jerusalem, he did an astonishing thing. Entering the temple at Jerusalem, he began driving out the merchants who were doing a thriving business there for the sacrifices of the feast days. Originally, because the Jews had to offer oxen, sheep, or doves upon the temple altar at the feasts, yet it was difficult to bring them from far away, they used the expedient of buying them before the temple and then offering them. And because the diaspora who gathered from every region had brought with them the currencies of those many places, a considerable number of money changers too were doing brisk business inside the temple. Driving out all these merchants, Jesus rebuked them and told them not to make his Father's house into a place of trade.

At any rate, from the event at Cana onward John reported all these matters to Annas. But Annas would not believe John's words.

"Made water into wine? Even a magician can do such a thing."

Annas thought little of the event at Cana, but showed deep displeasure and concern over Jesus' action in driving the merchants out of the temple.

"It seems... we should keep our eye on that man called Jesus of Nazareth."

Annas immediately summoned a man named Nicodemus from among the members of the council and instructed him to meet Jesus of Nazareth and investigate what sort of figure he was. That night John guided Nicodemus and set out to find Jesus. He already guessed where Jesus would be staying. He would surely be staying in the house of that man named Lazarus whom they had met at Jericho. Lazarus had said that he lived in Bethany. Bethany was a small village outside the city of Jerusalem.

Finding Jesus was not difficult at all. Even in Bethany the deeds Jesus had done that day in the temple had already become known by rumor, and many people were gathered in the yard of Lazarus's house. John let Nicodemus enter the house of Lazarus and then mingled among the people, watching the situation within.

Only after quite a long time did Nicodemus come out from the house of Lazarus. For some reason he wore a very grave expression.

"What did he say?"

"I... approached him and said this: They say that you are one who has come from God.... Then he answered like this. Unless a man is born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God...."

"What? Then do you mean a man must go back into his mother's womb and come out again?"

"I asked him the very same thing you have just asked me. But he answered like this. Unless a man is born of water and the Spirit, he cannot enter the kingdom of God.... The wind blows where it wills, and though you hear its sound, you do not know from where it comes or where it goes; so it is with everyone born of the Spirit...."

Two months before, when John met Jesus at Jericho, he had spoken of the kingdom of heaven, and now again it was said that he had spoken to Nicodemus of the kingdom of God. That night Nicodemus, having received some shock or other, returned to Jerusalem wearing a grave face, while John continued trailing Jesus for several more days.

At last Jesus began to open his mouth. Just like John the Baptizer, he cried that the kingdom of heaven had drawn near and began baptizing people. And beside him Philip and Nathanael were always following.

John's feelings were extremely tangled. He had judged too rashly and easily abandoned the side of Jesus. The miracle at Cana had certainly been astonishing. Annas, father-in-law of Caiaphas, had thought it little and said that even a magician could do such a thing, but John had never heard of any magician doing a thing like that. Yet now Jesus had begun to speak, had begun to baptize, and Philip and Nathanael had become his disciples.

Hearing the rumor that John the Baptizer had moved northward to Aenon, near the upper Jordan close to Galilee, John followed him north in order to meet him and confirm once more the matter concerning Jesus. John the Baptizer recognized him and gladly welcomed him. Hot-tempered John, as soon as they had exchanged greetings, spoke of Jesus.

"Teacher... two months ago, at Bethabara below the city of Jericho, when you were baptizing, there was a young man whom you pointed out and called the Lamb of God, was there not?"

"That... that is so."

"The name of that young man is Jesus, from Nazareth... and he is now baptizing people in the very place where you were baptizing. Many people are following him...."

John added one more sentence in order to stir the temper of John the Baptizer.

"It seems that the number of those receiving baptism from him is even greater than the number of those who received it from you."

But John the Baptizer only nodded silently, and then spoke words even more shocking.

"As I have already said, I am not the Messiah. I am one sent before him, and a man acts according to what has been given him from heaven. He who receives the bride is the bridegroom, yet the friend of the bridegroom also rejoices with joy; so I now am filled with joy. He must grow greater, and I must grow less.... You must bear witness to these things...."

John the Baptizer still pointed to that Jesus of Nazareth and even said that he was the Son sent by God, bearing witness that God loved that Son and had given all things in the world into his hands.

Hearing those words, John was all the more thrown into confusion. As he again headed south toward Bethabara, where Jesus and his disciples were, he met the company of Jesus, which was moving northward, in a village called Sychar in the region of Samaria.

Originally Samaria had been the capital of the northern kingdom of Israel when Israel was divided into north and south. King David, a man of deep faith, had subdued the surrounding Gentile nations and established the foundation of the kingdom, and his son Solomon, who succeeded him on the throne, was a king who enjoyed the greatest wealth and splendor.

In obedience to David's dying command, he completed the temple of Jerusalem, and his wisdom and breadth were so widely famed that the kings of neighboring nations contended with one another to visit him. But in his later years his luxury grew extreme, and because he brought in many women from foreign lands and lived without self-restraint, this became the source of disaster.

Rehoboam, Solomon's son, who succeeded him after being raised in a comfortable palace without experience of anything, in the end failed to win the respect of the people and had to taste the pain of seeing the nation split in two.

Of the twelve tribes of Israel, ten tribes broke away and founded a nation in the north centered on Samaria, and in Judea there remained only the tribe of David and the tribe of Benjamin. At this time the man who led the founding of the northern kingdom of Israel was King Jeroboam of the tribe of Ephraim. The tribe of Ephraim was the very tribe of Joseph, who became prime minister of Egypt and fed Jacob's eleven sons, and of Joshua, the leader who received the people of Israel from Moses and brought them into the land of Canaan after the exodus from Egypt.

Thus Jeroboam, asserting his own legitimacy, established a new altar of Jehovah at Bethel and designated Mount Gerizim, where Joshua had declared the blessing of God, as the new holy mountain.

For that reason the Samaritans too, even now, were waiting for a Messiah different from the one the Jews awaited. They were waiting precisely for a Messiah who would be born from the tribe of Ephraim.

In the village of Sychar there was a well said to have been used by Jacob, grandson of Abraham and ancestor of the twelve tribes of Israel. Near that ancestral well, which linked the severed north and south together, John sat mingled among the people and watched the movements of Jesus.

Philip and Nathanael had gone into the village to prepare food, and Jesus sat by the well and then began speaking to a woman who had come out to draw water, asking her to give him some water.

But at once the woman picked a quarrel with Jesus. Why, she said, did a Jew ask a Samaritan for water? Such was the division between Jew and Samaritan. At last the dispute with the woman went so far as to touch upon the Messiah, and even grew into the question of whether sacrifice offered to God should be at Jerusalem or at Mount Gerizim. Then Jesus answered the woman,

"For God, what matters is not Jerusalem or Mount Gerizim, but those who truly love Him."

At that one word, the face of the woman who had been fiercely confronting him changed with emotion. And hearing the words of the disciples who had returned, she learned that he was the Jesus whose fame was spreading. Entering the village, she told every person she met that the man she had met seemed to be the Messiah.

At the well of Sychar, Jesus had in effect declared that he was not the Messiah of the Jews only. But once again John sat there shaking his head alone. The tribe of Judah was, among Israel, the chosen people and the tribe of David. When the northern kingdom of Israel was ravaged by Shalmaneser king of Assyria, he transplanted Gentiles into Samaria. The Samaritans were trampled by all those Gentiles and became a mixed people, and for that reason the Jews treated the Samaritans as a people abandoned by God.

Yet that young man from Nazareth named Jesus had declared in Samaria that Jerusalem was not what mattered. He was already no longer the Messiah John knew. John slipped away from that place again and returned to Capernaum. For him, the expectation that the Messiah would come had completely collapsed. The opportunity to avenge himself upon Vespasianus was slipping farther and farther away.

Yet strangely enough, the rumor of Jesus kept stubbornly pursuing John from behind. When Jesus had passed through the region of Samaria and again reached Cana, where the wedding feast had been, many people had already heard the rumor of him and came crowding out in order to see him. Among those people was one high official serving under King Herod. He met Jesus and pleaded with him concerning his son.

"Teacher, my son is now in Capernaum, and he is dying of a terrible sickness. Please come to Capernaum and heal my son."

Jesus looked at the official as though pitying him and then said,

"What matters is not being healed from sickness, but the faith that escapes the authority of death. Yet in order to help your faith, I will heal your son. Go and see your son. And when your son is healed, believe the truth because of it."

For some reason the official felt strength in the words of Jesus and went back to Capernaum. Before he had even arrived there, he met servants running out to tell him that his son had revived.

This rumor at once spread through the city of Capernaum. And the one who came to John and told him this rumor was Simon. He reproached John for having rashly abandoned Jesus at Jericho and thereby lost a once-in-a-lifetime opportunity. By now the fame of Jesus had already risen as high as it could rise, and even if they went again and pleaded to enter his company, it was obvious that they would not be received.

Thus Jesus was coming toward Capernaum bringing with him a storm of rumor. Simon beat his breast, and John too, regretting the rash act he had taken, felt nearly out of his mind. At last, on the day when the rumor spread that Jesus would arrive at Capernaum, John had gone out upon the Sea of Galilee with his father Zebedee, his brother James, and their hired men, and was catching fish. John's father Zebedee kept pressing him about the matter of Jesus. He had gone to Judea saying that he would meet the Messiah, yet why had he met Jesus and returned without him? James his elder brother was the same. He slandered his younger brother before their father, saying that John had become somewhat witless ever since he had begun to haunt Tiberias. But John had no answer to give. He was only chewing down by force the rage surging up within him.

Because there was no peace of mind between father and son and between brothers, the fish naturally were not biting well. They had brought the boat in close to the water and were washing the net. And at that very moment an enormous crowd was pouring out to the seaside. John at once recognized who the man at the very front of that great multitude was. It was Jesus of Nazareth. The figure of Jesus, coming at the head of countless people, was more imposing than any Roman general, no, even than an emperor.

Jesus, in that manner, was coming straight toward where John was. John was deliberately pretending not to notice, yet his heart was pounding like hammers. But the Jesus who was drawing near stopped before Simon's boat, which was a little off to one side. Simon had already gone pale and could not even act acquainted with Jesus. In a low voice Jesus said to Simon,

"May I... borrow your boat for a moment?"

While Simon was still flustered and unable to answer, Jesus stepped into Simon's boat, which was in fact Zebedee's boat, and asked that they push it a little away from the shore. Andrew jumped down and shoved the boat, and Simon thrust with the pole and moved it. When the boat had gone a little away from the land, Jesus at once began preaching to the people gathered on the shore. His sermon too was about the kingdom of heaven, but Simon could not hear one word of what he said. Seeing the rumors, that at Cana he had made wine from water, and that by a single word he had healed the son of a certain high official who served King Herod, he was certainly no ordinary man. But Simon had followed him, then secretly run away from him. And now that Jesus had approached Simon himself and stepped into his boat of all boats.

At some point the sermon ended, and Jesus was gazing intently at Simon. As Simon lowered his head in confusion, he heard his voice.

"...If you wish to catch fish, go out into the deep and let down the nets."

"...What?"

Simon seemed not quite to know what he had said. It seemed he had told him to go out into the deep and let down the nets. If there was one thing in which Simon had confidence, it was fishing.

"The truth is, we worked hard all night and caught noth...."

But when Simon's eyes met the eyes of Jesus, who was standing and quietly looking at him, he hurriedly said with a frightened face,

"Ah... all right. I will do as you say."

He went a little farther out to the place Jesus indicated and let down the net. At the very moment he caught hold of the band of the net, thinking it was all for nothing, Simon's face hardened. Something had been caught. The net was too heavy to be pulled up. Andrew rushed in, and when they shouted, the hired men from Zebedee's boat too came running to help. Truly the amount of fish brought up was enormous. Even John, who was watching from a little distance, opened his mouth in astonishment. There were so many fish that the net was tearing and the boat was on the point of sinking under the water.

Simon fell prostrate on the spot. Trembling all over, and speaking with a loud cry like the howling of a beast, he said,

"Lord, depart from me, for I am a sinful man!"

Then Jesus said to Simon in a gentle voice,

"Do not be afraid. If you follow me, you will become a fisher of men."

Jesus not only did not blame Simon and his brother for having gone away from him, but rather meant to draw them back to himself. Simon and Andrew were so full of joy that they did not know what to do. Again Jesus came toward Zebedee's boat and reached the presence of John, who was pretending to mend the nets while watching for his chance.

"If you too... wish it, follow me."

John's spirit flashed awake. Jesus was willing to receive John too. Not only that, Jesus also said to James, John's elder brother, who had come running over after helping pull up the net from Simon's boat,

"...You as well."

And they followed Jesus. Leaving behind the boat, the nets, and even their father, they followed Jesus.

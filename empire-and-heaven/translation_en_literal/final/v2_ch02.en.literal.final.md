# Empire and Heaven (II)

## Chapter 2. The Voice That Came

A chilly spring wind was blowing through the harbor at dawn. Though day was only just beginning to break, the harbor of Ephesus was already crowded with merchants waiting for the unloading of new goods, dock laborers, and warehouse keepers.

In one corner among them stood two men who had been gazing out to sea for some time, and when they saw the small ship appearing in the distance they looked at one another.

"The ship is small, so it seems likely it is coming from Miletus."

"So it seems."

Gray dawn light was seeping through the slanting clouds hanging in the dark sky. The small ship that had crossed the night sea was slowly beginning to show itself. Polycarp, hunching his shoulders and folding his arms, spoke again.

"They say many miracles have appeared at Miletus."

"The rumor of those miracles seems to have begun at the quarry on Patmos. Prochorus, who came to deliver the Revelation, told us of the miracle on Patmos, did he not?"

"He did. He said the apostle healed the son of Commander Maximinus by prayer."

"When that event became known, the people of Miletus must have invited the apostle there, after hearing that he had been released from Patmos."

Polycarp spoke to old Tychicus, who was gazing at the sky as though sunk in thought.

"When I compare the Revelation the apostle wrote and sent with the state of the times these days, I cannot help thinking that the time of the Lord Jesus' return may be drawing near."

"Even the mother of Quaestor Andronicus, who died last year, was such a case...."

The rumor had spread not only in Miletus but here in Ephesus as well. There was a reason why people were so interested in divine healing, in the curing of sickness through prayer. As the age changed from one of war to one of peace, strange illnesses began continually to spread. These were not diseases known from ancient times, but bizarre afflictions in which the skin rotted for no reason, sores spread over the whole body, or the limbs became paralyzed; and once such sickness struck, even famous physicians could do nothing but shake their heads helplessly. Especially, the more prosperous the city, like Ephesus or Smyrna, the more common these illnesses were, rather than in the countryside. It seemed as though every household had one or two such patients.

"What do you think, master? I..."

"It was said that when the last times came, all kinds of strange diseases would spread through the world. What do these strange illnesses now spreading through the cities mean?"

"Did not even the mother of Andronicus die with blood flowing from every opening in her body though nothing outward seemed wrong?"

Old Tychicus nodded. Since the abduction of Drusiana he had continually cared for the people of the house of Andronicus, and so he knew the matter well. It was precisely that disease which had seized Andronicus's mother.

It was a sickness in which blood flowed from every opening in the body. Beginning as a light discharge of blood, it later worsened until blood came from the ears, nose, mouth, and every other opening. It was not a great hemorrhage all at once, but a small flow almost impossible to detect: when it stopped from the ears it would begin from the nose, and when it ceased from the nose it would begin from the eyes or the mouth, and so it continued. Tychicus himself ran about, summoning famous physicians not only from Ephesus but from all the cities of Asia Minor, such as Smyrna, Laodicea, and Miletus, yet in the end Andronicus's mother died in that way.

"And yet... in the apostle's Revelation many wars are recorded...."

Of course wars had not disappeared. On the frontiers of the Roman Empire, armies still clashed in order to expand occupied territory and suppress small rebellions, but there was still no sign that the vast wars described in the Revelation were about to arise.

"But, master, if Rome falls and the great cities are visited by tribulation, when Pax Romana is shattered, will not the world become turbulent again as powers begin to be rearranged?"

Tychicus was still looking up at the brightening sky. It was indeed the desire of every Christianus that Jesus should return before the world had rotted past saving, for no matter how much the gospel was preached, the condition of the world grew no better. Rather men were falling away into corruption more and more deeply. If things were polluted at such a rate, even the Christiani themselves might be swept together into that filthy flood.

"Could Rome really collapse so simply? Is not Emperor Nerva extending Rome's life once more?"

After Emperor Domitian was assassinated through the plot of his own wife and his intimates, at last the Roman Senate recovered the honor of choosing the emperor with its own hands. They elected as emperor the senator Marcus Cocceius Nerva, the oldest and most respected man among them.

Emperor Nerva, taking advice from the assassins who had supported him and from progressive men within the Senate such as Senator Ovidius, began pushing forward policies of liberalization. First, he dismantled the information network that during Domitian's time had driven the people into terror.

By this all public officials gained fresh vigor in the discharge of their duties, provincial administration was rapidly granted more autonomy, the power of the Senate was strengthened, and the voice of the popular assembly, which had shrunk back in fear, was restored. The empire, which under Domitian's coercive rule had felt as though it were treading on thin ice, was entering a time of renewed stability, and the banner of Pax Romana was recovering its authority.

Yet hidden within this current of liberalization there was another kind of anxiety. The process of liberalization necessarily involved a restructuring of power, and especially the weakening of military influence. And when military influence weakened, the form of selecting talent naturally had to change, and the system by which men from the cavalry rose into the core of the bureaucracy could not remain the same.

The military's strong backlash against such circumstances still remained a burden upon Nerva's government. Again the military had begun to express its dissatisfaction with the new order by protesting the amnesty granted to the assassins of the emperor.

"To tell the truth, I once thought perhaps the beast in the apostle's Revelation might refer to something of this kind. That monster ridden by Babylon, symbol of the last city, had seven heads and ten horns, did it not? If one counts ten emperors from the time the Lord Jesus suffered and rose again, they are Tiberius, Caligula, Claudius, Nero, Galba, Otho, Vitellius, Vespasian, Titus, and Domitian. Of those, the ones that seem to have horns but no real heads would be the three emperors who briefly sprang up in confusion after Nero, Galba, Otho, and Vitellius, men who bore the name of emperor but held no real power. Subtract those three and there remain seven. So I had thought that Domitian was the last ruler of this world."

Tychicus nodded as he listened to Polycarp's explanation, then opened his mouth.

"The revelation given to the apostle points toward great turning points in human history, so I do not think we can think about it lightly and make firm judgments. Papias, for his part, seemed to wonder whether those seven heads might refer simply to the seven hills of Rome itself: the Capitoline, the Palatine, the Aventine, the Caelian, the Esquiline, the Viminal, and the Quirinal. Bishop Ignatius of Antioch, on the other hand, seemed to guess that they might mean the nations that in succession ravaged Jerusalem: Egypt, Babylon, Media and Persia, Greece, Rome, and one kingdom still to appear, making seven in all."

"Hearing it that way, that too seems possible."

"At any rate, the matter of the seven heads and the ten horns will remain a subject of concern until the Lord returns. It is right for us to watch the changing of the age and ask whether perhaps this might be the time, but it does not seem right to attach our own interpretations to it as we please."

"That is true. For man to predict this way and that the time known only to God would be reckless indeed."

"Exactly. The Lord Himself said that the times and seasons are in the Father's hand, and told us to stay awake and be prepared, for we do not know when the bridegroom will come. From ancient times until now, it seems men have been interested not in being ready for the time, but only in the time itself. Even in the Gospels, do not both the disciples of Jesus and the Pharisees always ask when that time will be?"

At last understanding dawned on Polycarp, and his expression was growing brighter.

"That is true. Because men live within limited time, they think the moment itself is important, but from God's side the time is always present. So what matters must be the attitude of living as though now, always now, were that time."

"Precisely. The Jews were so preoccupied with that time that they failed to recognize Jesus and crucified Him. Men, staring at a time that is not now, are missing countless times that are now. That is why I think the word Paul wrote to the church in Corinth is exact: I die daily."

"Then for us every moment is the end."

"Exactly so. Living in this world, we should always smell the end, and as we feel ourselves growing slack, whip ourselves onward. Might not that be what Jesus meant when He asked us to watch?"

Polycarp looked at the profile of old Tychicus as he spoke. His life, faithful only to the gospel ever since he met Paul in Ephesus, was written upon that face. He was a man who had carried Paul's requests and run as though gathering up the whole world, and one who had lived thirty years in the mines of Hispania waiting for Christ. Yet he was in no haste at all. Looking out at the sea, he continued:

"And... I do not think we can press the Lord Jesus to return simply because our suffering is severe, Polycarp. Is not His return for the judgment of this world? The first time He came, He opened the opportunity for forgiveness and salvation by giving His own blood, and then chose to wait until the final judgment. But if Jesus were to come now... what about you, Polycarp? Is there perhaps still any soul among those entrusted to you that remains on the side of the world?"

Those words pierced Polycarp sharply, and pain came into his face. He was thinking of Callimachus, who had gone outside the fold. Glancing again at old Tychicus's face, he felt that perhaps this old man too still had unfinished work, and suddenly, catching at something, he turned the subject.

"But... how is Hesthenes, the servant of the house of Andronicus, these days?"

Sure enough, the old man's face instantly darkened.

"...His wounds are healed, but since there is no one to tend the empty house, it seems he still remains there."

After the household had been attacked by Callimachus's band, old Tychicus had often come and gone, caring for Andronicus's sick old mother and for Hesthenes, who had been badly wounded.

"Master, it seemed to me that you had grown rather close to Hesthenes during that time. Did you try preaching the gospel to him?"

The old man nodded heavily.

"Of course. Unexpectedly, he had a great deal of knowledge about our way."

"And then?"

"As a Greek, he is far too philosophical. He simply could not understand the claim that Jesus was at once man and God. If anything, his thinking was closer to that of Deacon Nicolas or of Cerinthus. He acknowledged that man has a soul, but said resurrection is nothing but illusion.... He had a deep loathing for the bodily side of man. It is a thoroughly Greek way of thinking."

They fell silent and looked out at the sea. The small ship from Miletus was already entering the harbor. Tugging on old Tychicus's sleeve, Polycarp cried out in an excited voice,

"There He is. I can see the apostle."

Indeed, upon the deck there stood John with his white hair flying, and beside him could be seen the sturdy figure of Prochorus. Polycarp, beside himself with joy, began jumping up and down and waving both hands, while old Tychicus too was wiping away tears with the back of his rough hand. That John should return alive was truly miraculous. He had escaped the fierce examination of Domitian, and had endured the quarry of Patmos, from which even the young came out only in death, and now was returning alive. Polycarp, forgetting even to wipe away the tears streaming down his face, lifted his eyes to heaven and offered a prayer of thanks.

(Lord, thank You. Thank You for preserving alive the last apostle who keeps You, the one on whom the hope of all the brothers throughout the world depends....)

The ship reached the dock, and the anchor was lowered. A wooden gangplank was set between ship and pier, and the passengers on board began coming down one by one. And at last, supported by Prochorus, John came walking down. In his darkened face there still lived a fierce passion, and in his flashing eyes there remained unchanged the loyalty of the watchman who guards Christ.

"Apostle...."

The moment John stepped down onto the pier, Polycarp could hold himself no longer. Grasping his legs, he knelt and sobbed. John took hold of him and raised him up, and then embraced old Tychicus as well. Afterward he introduced the companions standing with Prochorus.

"Greet the brothers who were baptized in Miletus. This is brother Cleobius, this one brother Daimonicus, and this lady Marcella...."

As Polycarp exchanged greetings with them, he guessed that they must be the people who had invited John to Miletus. During this, John kept looking around as though something still remained unfinished.

"Apostle, what are you looking for?"

"Why... why is Callimachus nowhere to be seen?"

Polycarp's face went pale. John was fixing those flashing eyes upon him.

"What has happened to Callimachus?"

Now Polycarp could endure no longer. Bowing his head deeply, he said,

"Callimachus... disappeared on the very day of Drusiana's wedding. But...."

"But...?"

Polycarp reported in full about meeting Callimachus in Smyrna, and even the fact that he had attacked the house of Andronicus. John's voice trembled slightly.

"Then... are you saying that the bandits who abducted Drusiana are Callimachus's own company?"

Polycarp answered with difficulty in a faint voice,

"Yes."

For a moment John lifted his eyes into empty space with a deeply sorrowful expression. Old Tychicus changed the subject.

"Apostle... have you heard the news that Bishop Onesimus was martyred?"

John nodded heavily. While John had been laboring in the quarry on Patmos, Domitian had once again sent the imperial guard to Ephesus and seized Onesimus, bishop of the church there. By a strange turn of fate the emperor had been unable to execute John himself, and judging that if he simply let the matter pass the emperor's command might be treated lightly, he had taken Onesimus as a substitute for John.

Onesimus, after a swift examination, was burned in the arena. Slave Onesimus, who had stolen his master's money and run away because he longed for freedom, was the representative of all slaves longing for liberty. Yet in the Mamertine Prison of Rome he met Paul, and his life was overturned. Becoming a new man, he took Paul's letter and went with Tychicus to seek out his former master Philemon. Philemon not only forgave Onesimus at Paul's earnest request, but even made him free. Having become a freedman and returned to Rome, Onesimus preached the gospel of hope to slaves and city poor who were as miserable as he once had been. He testified with his whole body that true freedom does not lie in this world but in Jesus Himself, and he became a comfort beyond measure to the poor of Rome. After Timothy was martyred he served for thirty years as bishop of the church of Ephesus, and when he was martyred many brothers in Rome still remembered him. When he stood in the flames in the arena full of joy, many watching the scene covered their faces and wept. Already בתוך the fire Onesimus was victorious. Many of those in the arena, whom the emperor imagined to be his own people, were in fact invisible Christiani.

In truth, the ones the emperor feared most were slaves and city poor like Onesimus. Once the empire came to dominate the world, the number of supervisory and administrative layers increased in every field, and to survive in Rome one needed an excellent mind and vast knowledge. Yet not everyone could be excellent, nor could all be learned. Thus in Rome there began to form a huge class of the left-behind. They were simple failures, men discarded from competition, and because of that they had no specialized skills and were not able to do hard physical labor either. All they possessed was a miserable sense of frustration.

It was they who kept emperor and nobles awake at night. No one knew when revolt might break out, or when their discontent might explode. Therefore emperors, while strengthening their information networks and the army, were also absorbed in soothing that class by carrying out grain distributions, increasing holidays, and staging free spectacles in the arena.

Yet more than any such policy, what reached that class most effectively was the gospel of the Christiani, which brought hope and comfort. That was why Jesus of Nazareth could not help being the enemy of the emperors.

Suddenly the noisy pier was falling quiet. John, Polycarp, and the others looked around. The procession of Governor Lycomedes, leading a body of infantry, was coming straight toward the place where they stood.

Because of Governor Lycomedes's sudden arrival, the Christiani at the pier were deeply tense. They had already felt uneasy over the thin-ice truce of recent days, and no one knew whether persecution might now begin again. The governor strode all the way up to the place where they stood, looked over the company, then fixed his eyes on John's face and said,

"Which of you is the man John who has returned from Patmos?"

His voice and expression were so heavy that no one could read his meaning. John stepped forward one pace and said in a low voice,

"I... I am John."

Then the governor gave a light nod and spoke again.

"For a moment... I have business, and would ask that you come with me."

John nodded as though he understood and looked around at those who were with him. The governor noticed this and said,

"Everyone... may come as well."

Again John looked at the others. His meaning was that there was no need for them to go. If something ill should happen, there was no reason for all to suffer together. But Polycarp had only just at last met his teacher after such a long time, and had no wish to let him go.

"Apostle, we will come too."

The governor replied as though agreeing.

"Very well. Let us all go together."

The governor and John went in front. The three from Miletus, Polycarp, Prochorus, and Tychicus walked with them. Behind them came the governor's infantry, armed and following along.

In front of the harbor memorial gate a carriage stood prepared. The governor seated John in the carriage and climbed in beside him. With the carriage at the front they began once more to walk along the marble-paved Harbor Avenue.

Polycarp and Tychicus had stiffened faces from tension, but the three believers from Miletus were, if anything, calm. They talked without ceasing about the miracles of healing that had taken place in Miletus, and their faces shone rather with the joy of having met Jesus.

Inside the carriage, Governor Lycomedes sat with a gloomy expression, then, as though with some purpose, spoke words of comfort to John.

"You suffered much on the island of Patmos, did you not?"

"I had many experiences there.... By them this old body has grown healthy in such a way."

"It is fortunate that you have come back alive. Consul Clemens and his wife, who were arrested at nearly the same time as you, were executed...."

John looked in surprise and asked,

"Lady Domitilla too...?"

"Yes. Since Lady Domitilla had been exiled to the island of Pontia, it seemed at first that she might preserve her life. But because she refused the emperor's command to apostatize, she was finally poisoned to death."

Seeing that John cared about her, Governor Lycomedes continued the explanation.

"It seems Emperor Domitian exerted himself greatly to persuade Lady Domitilla. That was because he thought that if Lady Domitilla were made to apostatize, many Christiani would lose heart and their faith could be shaken. So later he even sent to the island two of her friends, the ladies Leodora and Euphrosyna, in order to persuade her, but they too were Christiani. All three women died together."

Thinking of how resolute those three women had been, John bit his lip.

"When Lady Domitilla was arrested, the brothers Nereus and Achilleus, who had been chamberlains to Consul Clemens, were arrested together with her. What became of them?"

"They too were all executed on Pontia."

For some time John lowered his head and said nothing. What he had seen and heard this time in Rome had left him deeply moved by the courage and service of the Roman church. Though Rome was indeed under the emperor's rule, that was only the shell; John had clearly seen that all Rome was being transformed into a vast church of Christ. This was because of the life-risking love and service of the Roman church. John had seen that the heavenly kingdom was already coming upon Rome. He remembered the letter of Paul, who had shed his blood in that Rome:

Love suffereth long, and is kind.
Love envieth not.
Love vaunteth not itself, is not puffed up,
doth not behave itself unseemly,
seeketh not her own,
is not easily provoked,
thinketh no evil;
rejoiceth not in iniquity,
but rejoiceth in the truth;
beareth all things,
believeth all things,
hopeth all things,
endureth all things.
Love never faileth.

That was Rome. In Rome, where fear, threat, corruption, and discontent were intertwined, the zeal of Paul, the loyalty of Peter, and the courage and love that stretched from Consul Glabrio and Consul Clemens and Lady Domitilla to countless martyrs were creating a new kingdom. Light was coming upon Rome. It was being raised upon the very ground soaked in the blood of the Roman brothers. John remembered the answer of Lady Domitilla, so firm and beautiful:

- Of course there are still many things I can do for the church. But at this hour, the noble role that cannot be done unless by me is to show before all the world that the wife of Consul Clemens was a daughter who remained fully faithful to the Lord, and by that to give courage to the brothers who suffer.

Lady Domitilla had not at all been an enthusiast for martyrdom for its own sake. She had known well what it was that she alone could do. In the end, the heavenly kingdom is made up of those who bear beautifully the tasks that they alone are able to do. Thus among the Christiani of Rome, leaders as leaders, nobles as nobles, slaves as slaves, were all men and women who knew precisely what their role was.

"..."

When the carriage had turned right beside the great theater, John opened his mouth.

"Your Excellency governor, do you know of Quaestor Andronicus and Drusiana of the house of Tyrannus, who were married here two years ago?"

"I know of them. At that time I too was invited to the wedding and attended the banquet of the house of Tyrannus."

"I have heard that Drusiana, wife of that Quaestor Andronicus, was abducted by bandits...."

"That is so. On that very day last September, when the news of the assassination of Emperor Domitian was made known, Lady Drusiana was abducted by bandits together with a boy called Gaius who was in her care."

"And what became of those bandits after that?"

"There are several lineages of bandits that appear in this region, but since there was a large-scale bandit raid in Smyrna that very same day, we suppose they were the bandits of Korama."

"Korama?"

"Yes. The bandits who hold the region of Korama in Cappadocia. They are a group that has built a vast underground city in the valleys of Korama, made of volcanic tuff, and use it as their stronghold. When Quaestor Andronicus heard that his wife had been abducted, he came here at once and led one ala under my command, that is, six hundred cavalry, in a campaign as far as Cappadocia, but he was utterly defeated by the bandits who sallied out from their fortress in the caves and returned in defeat."

"Is Quaestor Andronicus now in Rome?"

"He is. Three Roman legions are stationed in this part of Asia, but the only troops I may move by my own authority are a single cavalry unit. Therefore Quaestor Andronicus has gone to Rome to petition the emperor for the dispatch of regular legionary forces."

Clouds were gathering more heavily over John's expression. If Andronicus marched out at the head of legionary troops, then the Roman legions and Callimachus's Korama bandits would bring about a great war.

"Do you think the emperor is likely to approve the movement of troops?"

"Sir."

"I think that will be difficult. Emperor Nerva is carrying out a policy of liberalization in order to wash away the atmosphere of coercive rule from the age of Domitian, and in such circumstances he will not wish to disturb the public mind by dispatching a large force. More than half a year has passed since Andronicus returned defeated from Cappadocia, and there has still been no word. That surely means he has not been able to obtain the emperor's authorization."

When the carriage passed through the civic square and turned into Crete Street, the governor at last spoke in a particularly grave voice.

"The reason I went to meet you at the pier today was because I have one request to make."

Governor Lycomedes's tone was becoming more and more respectful.

"What request could there be from such an old man as I?"

"Sir, I have for some time been hearing from those who came from Miletus the reports concerning you. They say that while there you healed many patients through prayer."

"The truth is... my wife fell ill last year with a strange disease, and has been lying in bed now for more than a year."

Seeing John say nothing, the governor let his head droop as though disappointed. John had not even asked what sickness his wife had. Yet, as though unable to give up, the governor spoke again.

"Sir, is such a healing miracle something that cannot be hoped for unless one is a Christianus?"

John still had his head lowered, deep in thought. Then at last he began to speak.

"Your Excellency governor, in the end every life dies once. What difference is there whether one dies now or dies a little later? My Teacher Jesus of Nazareth healed many people's illnesses, and even called out and restored a man already dead and buried. Yet those people too in the end died again."

The governor stared at him with a bewildered expression, unable to grasp what John was saying.

"Suppose your wife were healed and rose again. Then what would she do? Of course you would rejoice and it would be good. But in the end, how is that different from lying there?"

The thoughtful governor's expression began to change. He looked fixedly into John's eyes, striving to seize something.

"Jesus of Nazareth comforted many people and healed them because He had pity on them. That is, He wished their lives to become different. There is an immense difference between a life that simply lives and dies and a life that becomes different and then dies."

Because only a little earlier he had been thinking of the courageous life of Lady Domitilla, John's words carried astonishing force.

"Whether or not one bears the name Christianus matters little. What matters is what Your Excellency may gain through your wife's sickness."

"Through sickness...?"

"It happened while my Teacher Jesus was in Jerusalem. We were walking along and met a man blind from birth, and suddenly a question came into our minds, and we decided to ask it of the Teacher. For in the Scriptures of our Jewish nation it was written that sickness is punishment that comes because of sin. So we asked the Teacher: was this man born blind because of his parents' sin, or because of his own sin?"

The governor was listening to John's words with shining eyes. This was a matter directly tied to his wife.

"Jesus answered. He said that it was not because of his own sin, nor of his parents' sin, but that the works of God should be made manifest in him."

Still the governor stared at John as though he had not yet fully grasped it.

"Jesus opened that blind man's eyes. And at once he came to see a new world he had never seen before. He ran everywhere boasting that he had received his sight. The religious leaders of Jerusalem, who envied Jesus, seized him and questioned him whether he truly had been blind and whether Jesus truly had opened his eyes. That blind man found it most strange and said: how strange. I certainly was blind, and now I see; why is it that those great men do not believe it...? Your Excellency, that blind man saw the world of truth, but the religious leaders could not believe that truth. Because they were not blind, they could not see the truth. That is, because they were not sick, they missed the opportunity for a new life. And they crucified Jesus."

Only then did the governor's eyes grow bright with understanding. He had seized something now. Taking John's rough, scarred hand in both of his own, he said,

"Sir, thank you."

Without their noticing, the carriage had arrived before the governor's residence. Governor Lycomedes got down first and supported John as he descended after him. The people from Miletus, Polycarp, and even old Tychicus all looked on in surprise.

They were all led into the residence. Entering through the front hall, John had all those who had followed wait in the drawing room, and went with the governor alone into the inner chamber.

"What could be happening, Master Tychicus?"

"Who can tell...?"

It did not feel as though something ominous was taking place, yet they could not help wondering what exactly John was doing inside with the governor. An anxious time passed. They were now inside the residence of the governor of Ephesus. They were Christiani, and by a single command of the governor they could be arrested and executed. In just that way the family of Gaius, Timothy, and even Bishop Onesimus had all died.

Then all at once they turned their heads toward the inner chamber. Suddenly there had begun to ring out the loud sobbing of a great man.

A little later John came walking out alone from the inner chamber. Polycarp jumped to his feet and hurried toward him.

"Apostle, what has happened?"

Old Tychicus too stood up and asked,

"Who is it that is weeping like that?"

John spoke as though in solitary reflection.

"When a little child is newly born, it is right that he should cry...."

He was walking out toward the entrance hall. The others too came out of the drawing room and followed behind him. The soldiers standing outside looked bewildered, unable to understand what had happened. John said to the governor's chief steward, who did not know what to do:

"Bring something for the lady to eat."

They came out of the governor's residence and began walking toward the Magnesian Gate. Though no one spoke the direction, Polycarp and Tychicus already knew that John would be heading toward the assembly place in the cave on Mount Coressus. By now many of the Christiani of Ephesus would surely be gathering in that cave.

They also knew already that John had prayed for the governor's wife, and that some miracle had taken place. The reason John had told the governor's chief steward to prepare food for the lady was the same as the instruction of Jesus in the Gospel of Mark. After Jesus raised the dead daughter of the ruler of the synagogue in Capernaum, He had ordered that something be brought and given her to eat.

Again Polycarp began to think of the last times described in the Revelation. Domitian, who had ruled through terror, was dead. The persecution of the Christiani had been eased. And in John's prayers miracles had begun to appear. It was indeed a new world. The very fact that Christiani had been summoned into the residence of the dreaded governor and had then come walking out openly was itself a miracle beyond miracles.

Walking silently behind John, Polycarp spoke carefully when they were passing by the tomb of Luke.

"Apostle...."

"The record of Luke that you sent from Rome through Bishop Clement was safely delivered by Master Tychicus."

"It was an excellent record.... God's plan was truly marvelous. God made use of timid Mark by causing him to accompany Peter, and thus had him record the Christ seen through Peter's eyes with a pen like a lion's. He chose Matthew the tax collector to leave a detailed and exact record. And then He used Luke the physician to record the teaching of Christ, warm as a young ox...."

Again Polycarp thought of the four heavenly captains in John's Revelation. They had the forms of a lion, a man, a young ox, and an eagle. Again Polycarp asked:

"Master Tychicus told us... that you said Luke's record was wholly without error and exact, did he not?"

Polycarp's question sounded so much like an interrogation that even Prochorus and the three from Miletus glanced uneasily toward John. But John nodded and said,

"Mm... it was an excellent record."

Even now, John laid a hand upon his chest, as though the emotion of first reading Luke's record in the cave beneath the villa of Clemens was returning to life.

The kingdom of God cometh not with observation; neither shall they say, Lo here! or, lo there! for, behold, the kingdom of God is within you.

When Jesus of Nazareth had spoken those words on the road up to Jerusalem, John had not known what they meant. Yet somehow the physician Luke had singled out that very passage and written it down. The intense shock of that still held John in its power at this very moment. Luke, who had all his life followed Paul and helped him from the shadows, had in his faith grown to that point. That was the voice of Luke, which had come to John across the long years.

When he had gone about following Jesus of Nazareth, John had naturally thought himself the foremost among the disciples. But now many elders in faith had passed ahead of him, and now, though he had become a white-haired old man, he was still hurrying and stumbling after their footsteps.

Polycarp pressed closer to John's side and spoke again.

"When we received Luke's record, Bishop Papias of Hierapolis and I examined it closely, comparing it with the records of Mark and Matthew."

"That must have been a fine study."

"It was. And we...."

Polycarp paused for a moment, then said,

"In Luke's record we found certain points a little different from those in the former two records."

"You mean the matter of Jesus' genealogy?"

"Ah... concerning that point, Master Tychicus already explained it in detail to us. But...."

"Then is it a question of whether the blind man at Jericho was one man or two... or whether the donkey in the village of Bethphage was one animal or two?"

"In Luke's record too, the blind man at Jericho was one man, and the donkey of Bethphage was only one. But...."

"But?"

"For example... the sermons on blessing written by Matthew were spoken after the Lord went up on the mountain and sat down. Yet in Luke's record they were spoken not on a mountain but standing on a plain."

"That is how it was. What Jesus first said, He gave only to us disciples. We had gone up the mountain and sat around Him, and Jesus, sitting, spoke quietly. But when Jesus came down to the plain, not only from Jerusalem and Judea but even from Gentile lands such as Tyre and Sidon an immense crowd had gathered, and so He had no choice but to speak standing and with a loud voice."

"So that is how it was."

"The content of the words was similar to what He had said on the mountain, but because the audience was different, the purpose also was different. The words on the mountain, though spoken gently, were terrible words demanding harsh self-sacrifice from the disciples. The words spoken on the plain were words of comfort and encouragement addressed to the great crowd. If you read carefully, you will see it. Only... perhaps Matthew, because he regarded the apostolic mission as important, recorded the Sermon on the Mount, while Luke, like Paul, because he regarded the salvation of the common people as important, emphasized the sermon on the plain."

"Hearing your explanation, apostle, the background of both sermons rises before my eyes."

Yet it seemed that this was not the matter Polycarp most wished to bring up. He kept walking in thought, laboring to prepare another question. John sensed it first and asked:

"It seems there is something more you want to know?"

Only then did Polycarp lift his head as though at last he meant to come to the main point.

"Luke's record also contained the beautiful story of how Peter and your brother first met Jesus on the shore of the Sea of Galilee. Only, in Luke's record, a somewhat unusual scene has been inserted."

"You mean the scene where Jesus told them how to fish?"

"Yes. In the records of Mark and Matthew, Jesus was passing by the shore of Galilee when He saw the brothers Peter and Andrew casting a net, and He called them. He said, 'Follow me, and I will make you fishers of men.' They immediately followed Jesus, and going a little farther, Jesus saw James and John, sons of Zebedee, mending their nets, and He called them, and they too left their father and the nets and the boat and followed Him. That is as far as Mark's and Matthew's records go. But...."

"But...."

"In Luke's record, Jesus passed along the shore, got into Peter's boat, asked him to put out a little from the land, and then preached to the people on the shore. When the preaching was finished, Jesus saw that there were no fish in Peter's boat, and told him to go into the deep and let down the nets. Peter had labored all night in vain and his mood was poor, but he could not resist the word of Jesus and went where He directed and let down the net. And then so many fish came up that the net was nearly breaking. Brother Peter found the nets so heavy that he had to ask the boat beside him for help. And it says that James and John were astonished as well. But then... suddenly Peter fell down before the knees of Jesus."

"Peter said a strange thing then. 'Lord, depart from me; for I am a sinful man....'"

John kept walking in silence, looking only up at the sky. The morning sun had already risen high. Polycarp, now fully committed, determined to say all that remained.

"Why did Brother Peter say such a thing? And if your brother and you were certainly astonished as well, why did you not leap over into that boat, and instead remain only mending the nets there? Why did Jesus, after calling Peter, wait until you and your brother came up rather than coming to your boat Himself?"

Polycarp's question was so bold that even Prochorus and the people from Miletus glanced anxiously toward John's face. But John looked at Polycarp with a slight smile.

"And... is there anything more you would ask besides that?"

"In truth there remains something even more important."

"Something more important?"

"As I told you before, in the records of Mark and Matthew the story of Jesus' going up to Jerusalem at the Feast of Booths and the event of the raising of Lazarus are entirely missing. Yet Luke, here and there, mentions the events surrounding that Feast of Booths. In particular, Luke clearly records that Jesus visited the household of Lazarus in Bethany. Jesus entered their house; Martha was preparing food in the kitchen, while Mary sat before Jesus listening to His word. Thus there is the story of Martha in the kitchen rebuking her sister Mary. Yet strangely, Luke omits the story of Lazarus, the brother of Martha and Mary. And just about where the story of the raising of Lazarus ought to appear, Luke suddenly inserts one of Jesus' parables, that is, the story of the rich man and Lazarus. There was a rich man and a beggar named Lazarus lying at his gate, and when they died, Lazarus entered paradise while the rich man entered the place of torment."

"And after that...?"

"After that the record joins once more to the earlier narratives. Just as Bishop Ignatius testified, it records how the apostles became irritated with the children, and how Jesus said that whoever caused another to stumble would receive a great punishment. It is clear that Luke is hinting at something in this record. Luke not only brings up the story of Martha and Mary, which the other two never mentioned at all, but by inserting a parable of Jesus in which the name Lazarus appears, he seems to be steering the reader toward some story that lies at the heart of the matter."

Having said so much, Polycarp looked at John. It meant that now John alone could supply the answer. Indeed Luke's hint was strong, and Polycarp's pressing was persistent. In the question Luke set forth there was clearly some secret not yet made plain. And Lazarus, though he was a chief figure in the events that brought about Jesus' passion, did not join the apostolic missionary work, but instead went off with his sisters to the region of Lugdunum in Gaul.

Looking at Polycarp, John said,

"Polycarp, I have heard from Governor Lycomedes that strange diseases are spreading in this Ephesus. Beginning at noon today, we must gather the sick and the old into the great theater and do what can be done for them."

It seemed that John did not mean to open his mouth after all about the matter of Lazarus.

# Empire and Heaven (II)

## Chapter 7. The Cup and the Gate

Looking down upon the city of Ephesus, within the triangle formed by Mount Pion rising like the breast of the earth, Mount Ayasoluk seated to its north where the slope fell away once more, and the winding flow of the Cayster River, the temple of Artemis stood holding its ground in giant form.

This temple of Artemis, whose tall Ionic colonnades stood in order upon a high substructure and supported a vast roof, had been built in the time of Croesus, ruler of Lydia, by the architect Chersiphron. Thus Ephesus became the city of Artemis with her twenty-four breasts, and King Croesus dedicated golden cattle to this temple. About two hundred years later, on a certain night, the temple was burned and completely destroyed. It was revealed that the man who had set fire to this giant temple was one called Herostratus, who had wished desperately to become famous, and on that very night Alexander was born in the royal house of Macedonia. That Alexander, having grown to manhood, conquered Ephesus again, and the citizens of Ephesus, in order to recover the city's pride, began rebuilding the temple with the architect Dinocrates at the center.

For that reason this giant temple of Artemis was the pride and hope of the citizens of Ephesus. The more fervently they served Artemis, the more Ephesus prospered and grew rich.

Before the square of that temple of Artemis, three legions of Rome's forces stationed in Asia and three auxiliary cohorts had assembled. And some among them were setting up two tall crosses right in the court before the temple. Below the crosses a mountain of firewood had been piled, and the soldiers had set ladders there so that one could climb up to the crosses.

"Pour oil on the woodpile."

When Andronicus, in military dress and urging on the work, gave that order, the soldiers brought oil in tubs and began pouring it upon the wood. Andronicus lifted his eyes and looked up at the sky. The sun was already racing toward the middle of heaven.

"Curator Andronicus."

Without even turning, Andronicus knew whose voice it was. It was Governor Marcus Ovidius. He too was in military dress and mounted on horseback.

"Andronicus... do you truly intend to roast your father-in-law and mother-in-law whole?"

"If Callimachus does not come, that is what will happen."

"Will there still be any affection or loyalty left in a bandit?"

"That does not matter. Whether Callimachus comes or does not come, we win."

"We win even if he does not come?"

"The greatest problem for the Roman Empire now is that in every province the shoots of rebellion are sprouting. The resentment against rulers that anyone may feel raises its head and rises up whenever it has the chance. The problem with Callimachus now is not the damage caused by his plundering, but the fact that a considerable number of the people are applauding him. But if Callimachus does not come today, he will cease to be a hero and be reduced to a monster of impiety who abandoned Drusianus and his wife, who raised him, to die, and to a coward who fears the army of Rome."

Originally Governor Ovidius had, from the days when he was in the Roman Senate, been a man who opposed rash military expeditions. But once he had actually taken office as the person responsible for a province, the situation changed. When the people were being plundered by the appearance of bandits and the nobles were suffering damage, the governor charged with the province's security could not simply pretend not to know it.

Moreover, Curator Andronicus was confidently saying that he would draw the bandits out of the caves and root them out. Ovidius could no longer ignore his demand.

"There comes General Vegetius. It seems we should discuss once more the formation to counter the bandits."

They moved toward Commander Vegetius, who was coming this way.

"The petty thieves still do not appear."

The angular face and the strong jaw made it plain to anyone who saw him that he was a soldier by nature. Andronicus smiled at ease and said,

"Since we have baited the hook, the petty thief will not endure but will appear. There is still some time left before noon."

"Look here, Andronicus... I do not like this little soldier's game at all. However much one searches military history, there is no record of legionary troops being mobilized to catch a petty thief. It would be troublesome if battle-hardened Vegetius became a general who made future generations laugh."

"General, that is not so. The bandits of Korama possess a scale and fighting power greater than those of a regular army. At noon today, you will become a great soldier who pacified the most serious crisis on the frontier."

"Curator Andronicus... you speak far too well. I have always disliked soldiers who speak well.... What do you think, Your Excellency Governor Ovidius? Is this thief-catching today not like trying to catch a flea with an axe?"

From the beginning Vegetius had not thought much of Governor Ovidius. Not only had Ovidius, while in the Senate, been a central figure of the progressive forces that restrained the power of the military, but he had even been the very man who argued that soldiers' pay should be cut. Pretending not to hear the commander's swagger, Ovidius said,

"And yet, General... as Curator Andronicus says, the enemy seems to possess formidable fighting strength."

"Ho... so that is why this young genius sent me an official document telling me to practice a new formation."

Andronicus set his face and looked at Commander Vegetius.

"Forgive me, General. Because Callimachus is a crafty opponent like a fox, he will have studied the formations of the Roman army. I thought it would do no harm to prepare for that...."

Then Vegetius, who had been glaring at Andronicus with flashing eyes, suddenly shook his body and began to laugh.

"Curator Andronicus... it seems you have a natural talent for making men laugh. The bandits study formations? Is formation needed for theft as well? Men of office like you may have things worth stealing, but we soldiers have nothing between our legs except what hangs there, so you may rest easy."

"It seems rather presumptuous of me to say this before my senior...."

Vegetius was Andronicus's senior from the cavalry. The mention of seniority had its effect, and Vegetius's expression softened somewhat.

"The whole world knows that younger men these days are insolent, so I do not mean to rebuke you. Well then, say quickly what presumptuous thing you wish to say."

Andronicus gazed for a moment at Vegetius's flashing eyes, then slowly opened his mouth.

"As for the legions stationed in Asia, one legion has only three hundred cavalry, and all the rest are infantry. So even if one gathers together the cavalry squadrons of the three legions and the auxiliary cohorts, our cavalry is fewer than two thousand. But the bandits of Korama are impossible even to estimate in number, and all of them are cavalry. As you know, Cappadocia is famous as a producing region of fine horses, and the people there grow up learning to ride from childhood. Today's battle will be a battle of mobility and speed."

"If that is so... why did you send me an official document telling me to drill the circular formation? Is not the circular formation a defensive formation used to block the enemy?"

Only then did Andronicus dismount and draw the sword at his waist, and while drawing lines on the ground began to explain.

"As I understand it, our Roman legion's traditional field formation is the pincer formation that shattered the Macedonian army's dense sixteen-rank formation. That formation made the fullest use of the rigid density of the Macedonian spear ranks. That is, Roman mobility was matched against Macedonian rigidity."

Listening to Andronicus's explanation, Vegetius was inwardly admiring him. Vegetius himself was a master of formation studies within the Roman army and a commander who had never known defeat in the field. Yet now, seeing this young junior explaining Roman formations, he was feeling strangely troubled.

"But... in today's battle there seems likely to be another change. If the bandits possess more cavalry than we do, the situation may bring about the opposite of the field at Cynoscephalae."

"Do you mean... that our position will become the rigid one?"

"That is if the opponent possesses greater flexibility than we do. The Roman formation, whose strength lies in the mobility of its left and right wings, has the weakness that its movement is linear. Linear deployment is strong in force, but difficult in responding to flexibility. Because Callimachus is sly as a fox, if he knows the Roman formation, he will certainly wind upward in a curved formation like a serpent. If that happens, our limbs may be wrapped by the serpent that presses in, and we may be thrown into confusion."

Vegetius nodded with a groaning sound.

"Then... why must we use the circular formation?"

"If the other side uses a curved formation that seeps in like a serpent, a straight formation will be wrapped wherever it goes. The only formation that can block the entry of serpents is a circular formation like a jar."

"A jar?"

"That is right. If all the soldiers stand encircling in a ring, no serpent's head can crawl in. As you know, the formation used by a herd of bison when attacked by beasts is precisely this circular formation. This circular formation may be one, or it may be many. The reason I asked you to drill them so that they could form circular formations by unit according to the trumpeter's signal is precisely that."

"Andronicus... I understand that the circular formation is advantageous for stopping the enemy's attack. But are you telling Commander Vegetius to wage only womanish battles of blocking?"

Andronicus looked at the pouting Vegetius and smiled faintly.

"General... is defending not also war?"

"I do not wage that kind of battle. You will remember my speech in the Roman Senate, where I said the best defense is attack. I bore witness thus before cowardly senators. The way for Rome to maintain world rule is endless attack."

Andronicus nodded his head.

"That is so. The best defense is attack. In today's battle as well, the General may give the command to attack his men."

"How can one attack the enemy with that jar-like circular formation you speak of?"

"That is precisely it, General. The jar."

"The best way to catch serpents is to put them into a jar. When the serpents come up to our circular formation and circle around looking for a place to enter, we quietly open the mouth for them."

"You mean open one side of the circular formation?"

"That is right. Then the serpent will think it has found its chance and crawl into the circular formation. Once enough have come in, our side immediately closes the mouth of the jar and then all at once turns inward. The serpent trapped inside the jar will be hacked to pieces beneath our blades."

"Hm... then we must make several jars."

"We must make them while observing the enemy's formation. If the serpent has seven heads, we make seven jars; if ten, then ten jars."

Vegetius's eyes were growing wider and wider. He could not understand how such an astonishing method of war could come from this young curator. Moreover, the formation Andronicus had proposed was neither difficult nor complex at all, so Vegetius had already been able to drill the soldiers in it within no great time.

"Andronicus... today you have recorded a new page in Roman military history. You have given operational instruction to this Vegetius, master of formation studies."

"No, General. This is not instruction but a proposal."

Vegetius at once assembled the commanders of the spearmen, the cavalry, the scout force, the reserves, and the main force, and began explaining the method of the circular formation.

Andronicus again looked up at the sun in the middle of heaven and then ordered his adjutant to hang Drusianus and his wife upon the crosses. The soldiers of the command post rushed over in a body and dragged out Drusianus and his wife, who had already been bound within the camp. As they passed before Andronicus, he turned his head away and pretended not to know them. Once the daughter was gone, the son-in-law had become a stranger.

The soldiers climbed up atop the woodpiles and were hanging them upon the crosses. The soldiers made them step onto footholds set midway up the crosses and bound their arms and legs to the wood. Now if fire were only set to the firewood piled beneath them, they would be dead in the flames in an instant.

On the northern slope of Mount Pion, the citizens of Ephesus had come out in crowds and were watching the battle of that day. In the mood of watching an exciting match at the chariot races, they were expecting the grand spectacle that would unfold that day. To them, who always wanted a more stimulating contest, the sight of battle between the Roman legion and the bandits was a spectacle not to be missed. Moreover, the couple of the house of Tyrannus hanging high upon the crosses was also an extra sight for the blood-hungry citizens of Ephesus, like offerings presented to the goddess Artemis.

Now the soldiers drilling formations in the square and the citizens watching from Mount Pion were all gazing only at the sky. Some among them had already begun wagering money on whether Callimachus would come or would not come. Thus that suffocating time was passing. The sun had now almost entered the middle of the sky. Andronicus's command fell.

"Prepare the fire!"

Fire was lit in the small pile of wood between the two crosses. Now, when Andronicus gave the next order, that fire would be transferred to the woodpiles on both sides.

"Curator Andronicus, my congratulations."

Andronicus turned his head. Tribune Musicus, commander of the scout force, had come up on horseback and was saying so.

"Congratulations?"

"The time has come for you to avenge your defeat at Korama. Callimachus's bandit horde has already appeared."

"What?"

"There... look upon Mount Ayasoluk. The scout force of Musicus is raising the signal smoke."

Andronicus quickly looked toward Mount Ayasoluk. Indeed, black smoke was rising straight toward the autumn sky. And before long white dust arose along the western foot of Mount Ayasoluk, and it could be seen that countless men and horses were surging in like a tidal wave.

The bandits of Korama swept around Mount Ayasoluk in an instant and came crashing toward the temple of Artemis.

Andronicus, looking at the momentum of the bandits, shouted toward Vegetius.

"General, the heads of the serpents have begun to appear. Order the circular formation!"

Vegetius immediately gave instructions to the trumpeter, and the Roman army formed great circular formations unit by unit. In front the main force formed circular formations, and on their left and right the spearmen and the scout force also spread circular formations. Behind them the cavalry of each legion and the mounted squadrons of the auxiliary cohorts likewise formed large circular formations, and at the very rear stood the reserves. The cavalry of Korama, having rushed in with terrible force, wavered like giant sea anemones at the signal of the standard-bearer, and then soon writhed like serpents and began charging upon the Roman legions. The bandits, drawn in curves, were ravaging the enemy lines in every direction while sharp sounds of wind arose. It was the sound of the bandits swinging their whips.

With the confused cracking of whips and the sharp clash of spear and sword, the square before the temple of Artemis had become a scene of carnage.

Callimachus, who from the center of the serpents was brandishing whip and sword, suddenly felt that something about the progress of the battle was strange. The Roman legion was not being wrapped up easily as he had expected. Only then did he realize that he had fallen into Andronicus's new method. Because the other side had all formed circular formations and were swinging spear and sword outward, Callimachus's serpents could not coil around them. The cavalry of Korama were only circling the outer edge of the enemy. Brandishing his whip, Callimachus said to the staff officers following after him,

"The Roman legion has changed its formation. They are deploying not a straight formation but circular formations. With our serpent-shape we cannot coil around a circular formation."

"Then what should we do?"

"The Roman legion is now using a jar formation to catch serpents. Send word that even if they open their mouths, no one is to go inside the circular formations."

"Yes, dominus."

The liaison officer was galloping away to inform the unit commanders in the midst of the fierce battle. Indeed, Andronicus was no easy opponent. The Roman army's circular formations were so large that even Korama's mobility could do nothing with them. If only Korama's serpents were trapped inside those jars, they would plainly be unable to move and be cut to pieces.

Callimachus cried out within his mouth.

(A jar... yes. There is no way but to break a jar. Breaking it is the best method. To shatter a circular formation, a club is needed!)

He quickly examined the shape of the battle. Korama's three main forces, unable to break the Roman legions' circular formations, had circled round and round and at last were passing beyond the circular formations of the cavalry and coming out toward the reserves at the rear. He again gave instruction to the liaison officer who had returned to his place.

"Tell each unit not to use curved shapes, but to change to thick straight formations. Each straight column is to advance in four ranks and break through the circular formations from the middle. The tail must never be severed. Using the club, shatter the enemy's jar into two, and after the two sides are divided, annihilate them."

When the liaison officer withdrew, the battle again began to enter a new phase. The bandits, who had merely been circling outside the Roman circular formations, now became one solid mass and began breaking through those circular formations. The Romans, thinking the serpent-shaped formation was coming in as it had been, opened the circular formation, but instead of being trapped inside the jar, the enemy broke straight through to the opposite side with rigid force, and the circular formation was completely split into two pieces.

Andronicus too, having grasped this development during the battle, again proposed a change of operation to General Vegetius.

"The enemy is using a tactic of concentrating strength and breaking the circular formations. If we are to disperse their strength, there must be many circular formations. Deploy circular formations centered on the centuria."

"Very well."

Vegetius at once ordered the trumpeter to change the formation. At the signal of the trumpeter, the Roman formation again began to alter its shape.

Originally the Roman army's basic organization was the centuria, that is, the hundred-man unit, and those centuriae were commanded by centurios, that is, centurions. Therefore orders for battle by centuria could be carried out in the Roman army as systematically as one pleased. At last the Roman formation changed into two hundred seventy circular formations made up of one hundred men each.

Callimachus instantly perceived this change in the Roman army. It was clear that the Romans had spread circular formations centered on the centuriae. Callimachus would now have to prepare two hundred seventy clubs. He ordered the standard-bearer and for the moment commanded a withdrawal.

It was to reform the ranks. At the signal of the standard-bearer, the bandits began slipping out like water between the circular formations. It was then. Toward Callimachus, who had turned his back and was withdrawing outside the formation, a Roman officer thundered aloud.

"Callimachus, if you are a man, do not turn your back in battle!"

It was Andronicus himself. He was chasing after Callimachus at dreadful speed. Callimachus instinctively recognized whose voice had flown from behind him. He knew now that he could no longer avoid the confrontation. Suddenly he wheeled his horse and swung his long whip toward the direction from which the voice had come.

"Ah!"

All the soldiers around cried out. Caught by Callimachus's whip, Andronicus's helmet went flying away. Andronicus's face was flushed red. Kicking his horse's flanks with his spurs, he rushed upon Callimachus, but with the short sword of the Roman army he could do nothing against Callimachus's long whip. At that moment an officer of the spearmen cried out,

"Curator, receive this spear!"

and threw up a spear. Andronicus took the spear he had thrown with practiced skill and leveled it at Callimachus. A savage duel between whip and spear began. The Roman soldiers and the bandits, already parted to either side, were watching the fiery fight of the two young men with sweat in their palms.

The weapons of the two were already flying, breathing out the venom of mortal combat. The shadow of the whip raged as though it would cover the sky, and the tip of the spear, cutting the wind, poured in between the shadows of the whip.

At the moment Callimachus sent out the whip with a sharp cry, Andronicus jerked his head back and brought his hand to his cheek. Struck by the whip, his cheek had been torn, and blood was staining his palm. A blue fire poured out from his eyes. Suddenly his long spear turned once in the empty air, and a streak of blue light flew through the whips. This time a cry burst from the side of the bandits. At Callimachus's breast the black garment had been torn and was hanging in rags, and through it red blood was oozing out.

At that moment Tribune Musicus called to Commander Vegetius.

"General, what is that?"

With the tip of his sword he was pointing toward Mount Pion. Vegetius turned his head toward where he pointed.

A strange thing was happening. From the distant Magnesian Gate, turning around the foot of Mount Pion, an enormous crowd was approaching this way. They were all clothed in white. Because of that, the multitude looked even more immense.

"What, are all those people citizens of Ephesus?"

The crowd was pressing ever nearer. And they could see at the head of the people a white-haired old man riding a white horse.

"Who is that old man?"

"Ah, that old man...."

Musicus looked at Vegetius with startled eyes.

"Do you know him?"

"That old man is John, the only one still alive among the disciples of Jesus of Nazareth. Because lately he has been helping the poor and praying for the sick in the great theater of Ephesus, I too have come to know his face."

"Then... do you mean all this great crowd are Christiani?"

Musicus's face was growing pale.

"This is unexpected. I did not know they would be so many."

It was truly a fearful thing. If the white-clad multitude following John were all Christiani, it seemed as though they were more numerous than all the citizens of Ephesus put together.

The procession of the Christiani with John at its head was entering the battlefield without hindrance. They crossed between the Roman army and the bandits and cut off the space between them. Now the interval between the two forces had been completely blocked by a sea of white-clad people.

Suddenly John's thundering voice flew toward Callimachus.

"You wretch, Callimachus! Will you not cast down your weapon at once?"

Callimachus, still absorbed in the duel and not yet noticing the change in the situation, was startled on hearing John's voice ring out like thunder. Andronicus too, surprised, withdrew his spear and looked that way.

The white-haired old man seated high upon the white horse was indeed the Apostle John. And behind him countless crowds were cutting off the space between the two armies. Above all, what astonished Andronicus was that among the waves of white garments he discovered the figure of his wife Drusiana.

"Drusiana!"

But Andronicus's voice was scattering among the countless people and not reaching his wife. Among the white-clad multitude were mixed quite a number of familiar faces. Papias, Polycarp, Prochorus, the boy Gaius, old Tychicus... and even Hesthenes, Andronicus's servant, was among them. Not only that, among them were also former Governor Lycomedes and his wife.

Originally, before entering the confrontation with Andronicus, Callimachus had intended to release Drusiana. But if he released her beforehand, she would surely run to Andronicus and plead for reconciliation, and then Andronicus would certainly think that Callimachus had sent Drusiana and petitioned for peace. Callimachus disliked that, and so, while bringing her together with the boy Gaius, just before the battle he had ordered his men to escort them into the city of Ephesus, and there they had met the ranks of the Christiani coming out from Ephesus.

Again John's voice sped toward Callimachus.

"You wicked fellow! Will you not cast down your weapon at once?"

John's voice was overwhelming the people like a great mountain. Callimachus bit his lip, glared at John, and shouting harshly in a loud voice swung his whip.

"Who are you to command me? Foolish old man, hypocrite!"

Before the white-clad crowd had even time to be startled, Callimachus's whip was flying toward John. The whip struck John's shoulder. It struck his chest. And it lashed across his face. Again John's shout rang out as though to rend the sky.

"You villain! Today I shall kill you and break the Sixth Commandment!"

Truly it was a fearful declaration. John's face was burning like a firebrand, and from his eyes it seemed crimson flames were pouring out. Neither Roman soldiers nor bandits nor the crowd of Christiani had ever before seen such a terrible figure. Again John shouted in a voice as though it would smash rocks.

"You villain! For this day I practiced for a year and a half on Patmos how to be whipped!"

When John suddenly lifted his hand, the flying whip was caught in John's hand. John seized the end of the whip, wound it around his sleeve, and jerked it away. In an instant Callimachus's whip passed into John's hand. John began to swing the whip as though he truly meant to beat Callimachus to death. The Christiani, who had always seen only his gentle figure, were trembling all over at the too dreadful sight. Only then were they realizing why Jesus of Nazareth had given him the nickname "son of thunder."

Callimachus's spirit was already broken. Suddenly he wheeled his horse and began to flee toward Mount Ayasoluk.

"You villain! Stop there!"

John did not miss him, but spurred his horse and began pursuing Callimachus from behind. Then the bandits too began riding toward Mount Ayasoluk after their fleeing chief, and the ranks of white garments also began moving toward Mount Ayasoluk after their leader.

Musicus looked at the commander and said,

"General, this is a fine opportunity. You must pursue the bandits and annihilate them."

But Governor Ovidius stepped forward.

"That must not be done. The citizens of Ephesus will be harmed."

"No, Your Excellency Governor."

Musicus stepped forward.

"These men cannot be called citizens of Ephesus. They are adherents of a cult which the Roman Empire is making unlawful, and an anti-state horde. Even if they are killed, the law of the Roman Empire will not ask after that guilt."

"Do not push yourself too far, Tribune Musicus. The one who governs Ephesus is Marcus Ovidius. General Vegetius, I as governor of Ephesus request the protection of the citizens of Ephesus."

It was then. Musicus shouted.

"Look there, the Christiani are trying to release hostages of the Roman Empire!"

"What?"

Already great numbers of the Christiani had climbed atop the woodpiles where Drusianus and his wife hung and were dragging them down from the crosses. Among them could be seen Maximinus from the island of Patmos and his wife Milga, Cleobius from Miletus, Daimonicus, and Lady Marcella as well. Musicus ran that way and shouted to his subordinates.

"Do not let the hostages go! Kill the lawbreakers!"

The Roman soldiers rushed upon the white-clad people and began swinging swords and spears. In an instant several Christiani fell. Andronicus could no longer merely watch. Among them his wife Drusiana too would be mixed.

"Musicus! Do you intend to disobey the command of His Excellency the Governor?"

"Curator, I do not need to receive an order to protect lawbreakers."

Andronicus ordered the soldiers under Musicus who were brandishing swords,

"Stop the killing! As curator of Ephesus, I order it. I will arrest your tribune Musicus for the crime of insubordination."

"Arrest?"

At that moment Musicus's face twisted, and fixing his eyes on Andronicus with a sneer almost like a laugh, he looked at him.

"Curator Andronicus... I thought you a young man of some intelligence, and so I meant to help you, but you will not receive my good will. Fine. Today I will refuse your order and receive judgment before His Majesty Emperor Trajanus."

Turning away, he cried out to his own men.

"Men of the scout force, hear! Pursue the fleeing bandits and the Christiani, and leave not one alive, but strike them all down!"

At that, Vegetius, who had been listening to their conversation, turned red in the face and drew his sword.

"You villain, Musicus, brandishing the fact that you were the emperor's delator, you have behaved lawlessly enough, and today at last before my eyes you have shown your true colors. How dare you, a mere tribune, push aside the commander and issue operational orders!"

General Vegetius brought down his sword upon Musicus. Musicus dodged aside and received his blade with his own. At last pandemonium broke out within the Roman camp. A deadly battle had arisen between Musicus's scout force and the other troops under the commander's authority.

Watching the Roman legion's internal strife, Andronicus pulled himself out of the camp and began riding toward Mount Ayasoluk. It was to find Drusiana. Already black-clad bandits and white-clad Christiani were mingled together and climbing toward Mount Ayasoluk. And Andronicus could witness a still stranger sight. A great number of Roman soldiers were leaving the camp and going toward Mount Ayasoluk. They were not following in pursuit of bandits or Christiani. They no longer carried weapons, and even their helmets were off. Andronicus seized one among them and asked,

"Look here! Why are you walking empty-handed?"

Then that soldier looked at Andronicus and answered in a clear voice.

"Curator, forgive me. I am a Christianus."

"What?"

It was truly a shocking thing.

(Then... do you mean that these soldiers now going toward Mount Ayasoluk were all Christiani?)

He struck his horse's flank and rode toward Mount Ayasoluk. When he arrived, Mount Ayasoluk was already covered with people. Black-clad bandits, white-clad Christiani, and Roman soldiers who had cast away their weapons and followed... Andronicus drove his horse up the mountain through those people. There John could be seen. And in John's arms there was held one young man in black clothes.

That young man was sobbing, his shoulders shaking. He was crying and shouting something wildly. John, who held him and was patting his back, was weeping too. Andronicus dismounted and walked toward where John was. A desolate wind was blowing through his breast.

(What wretched lives are these...? Why must life be so painful?)

It was all futile. Anger, and the war of revenge, all of it was vain. Looking at that one young man who was weeping so, Andronicus was realizing one more thing he had never known before. After his wife had been taken from him, how madly had Andronicus himself spent those years in Rome. Yet even so, he had not understood the sorrow of Callimachus, from whom Drusiana had been taken by Andronicus. In the end, war and conflict come from being unable to understand another's pain because of one's own interest. Andronicus was feeling fresh disgust at the cold cruelty of mankind.

(Why must only Andronicus become Drusiana's husband? Why may Callimachus not become her husband?)

It was as though he had at last escaped from the net of illusion. Until now he had been wandering in an absurd labyrinth.

It was then. Suddenly people began murmuring. All their faces were changing color. Andronicus too soon understood the reason. A strange sensation was rising from the soles of his feet and spreading through his whole body.

The ground beneath their feet was shaking. Andronicus heard someone nearby whisper,

"...An earthquake!"

Soon the earth began to heave like the sea. Someone pointed a finger toward the city of Ephesus and said,

"Ephesus is collapsing!"

Part of the city of Ephesus, visible beyond Mount Pion in the distance, was collapsing. White dust could be seen rising thickly. And that heaving was moving again toward Mount Pion. The people who had crowded onto the slope of Mount Pion to watch the battle of the Roman legion and the bandits were scattering like ants in every direction. Mount Pion, rounded and swelling upward, was violently shaking its body.

"It has reached the temple of Artemis!"

They could clearly see the temple of Artemis collapsing. The temple of Artemis, the greatest building in the world and the great boast of the citizens of Ephesus, was collapsing in vain. The temple of Artemis, set on fire by Herostratus and rebuilt by Dinocrates, was now hiding its form beneath the earth. Artemis, the goddess believed to give abundance to Ephesus, Artemis who had ruled Ephesus baring her twenty-four full breasts, was now slowly withdrawing that glory.

The ground was splitting open wildly, and the Roman soldiers who had been fighting were sinking into the earth. Musicus's forces and Vegetius's forces alike were all going into the ground in that way.

Suddenly Marcus Ovidius the governor came to Andronicus's mind. Governor Ovidius the pacifist, who had stubbornly opposed the dispatch of the troops and troubled Andronicus. He too must now be sinking into the ground together with that Roman legion. Andronicus bowed his head.

(Governor Ovidius... after all, your words were right. Though I knew it, I contradicted you because of my own private stubbornness....)

At that moment someone gripped his shoulder and said,

"Andronicus... I did not know the Roman legion also might have a day of collapse."

"...What?"

Andronicus turned his head and looked at him. He almost cried out.

"Why... how is Your Excellency here...?"

It was none other than Marcus Ovidius, governor of Ephesus. The governor smiled faintly and said,

"Now the Roman emperor will have to appoint yet another new governor."

He drew out from inside his military garment the necklace hanging around his neck. There a metal fish was gleaming.

Andronicus said in a sunken voice,

"Your Excellency... please forgive the many ways in which I have treated you discourteously."

"Not at all... rather, I think I must apologize for not speaking even one word of comfort to you while hiding my condition."

"When did Your Excellency receive baptism?"

"Under Domitian. I became a Christianus through the witness of His Excellency Consul Clemens."

Andronicus remembered him in the Senate three years before, asking sharp questions and taking John's side when Emperor Domitian was interrogating him.

"But Curator Andronicus... it seems to me that you are more concerned than with me about those standing behind me...."

"...?"

Only then did Andronicus look behind him.

"Drusiana!"

Behind him were standing Drusiana and her parents. And the boy Gaius was there too. They had been hard to see because branches concealed them.

Drusiana stepped one pace forward. Andronicus's gaze stroked her forehead. There was no longer mixed in it either jealousy or hatred toward Callimachus. He embraced his wife and, without words, comforted her by patting her back with his palm. And he was being deeply moved by the generosity of Callimachus, who had already released Drusiana and Gaius before engaging in the contest between the men.

The earthquake continued for quite a long time, yet strangely upon Mount Ayasoluk there were only small tremors that shook the soles of the feet. However much had collapsed, thick white dust still covered Ephesus, and the temple of Artemis was sinking completely into the earth. And the Roman legion that had been in the square before the temple, somehow, could now no longer be seen even in traces. The earth had already opened its mouth and swallowed them all.

The earth that had been shaking so began little by little to subside. And only silence remained. The people who had been watching this dreadful sight from Mount Ayasoluk stood there without moving. Some knelt down and prayed, and many looked at the sky waiting for the return of Jesus of Nazareth.

The sun had already begun to sink toward the Mediterranean. The winding Cayster River, receiving the golden sunlight back from it, was sending it toward Mount Ayasoluk.

At length white-haired John separated Callimachus from his embrace, seated him upon a rock, and then himself sat down upon that rock. And John's voice began to flow out.

"Callimachus... do you know why, from the first time I saw you, I have thought of you as a natural son?"

Callimachus wiped away his tears with his fist and shook his head.

"Your mother came back to this Ephesus heavy with child and gave birth in the house of Tyrannus... it was two years after Emperor Vespasianus took the throne and Jerusalem fell. At that time I happened to be at the school of Tyrannus to give discourse concerning Christ, and because the mistress of the house and the maidservants had all gone out, it was with my own hands that I received you."

Callimachus looked at John with startled eyes.

"It was not only for that that my heart was stolen away by you. After hearing your mother's words, I came to know that you were the son of Onesiphorus the apostate. The reason I thought of you as my own son was precisely... because I myself had been an apostate...."

At that, all the people standing around them were startled and looked at one another's faces.

"But Callimachus... today I wished to introduce to you a precious man."

John turned his head, looked behind him, and cried out,

"Phygelus, where are you now?"

Then a middle-aged old man who had been standing a little apart from John walked forward. At John's call, many people already remembered who he was. He was one of those who had argued for apostasy together with Callimachus's father Onesiphorus when old Tychicus came through Ephesus just before Paul's martyrdom. John turned toward Phygelus and said,

"Phygelus, as you told me, could you explain the reason why you and Onesiphorus came to advocate apostasy?"

Phygelus lowered his head for a moment and sank into thought, then opened his mouth.

"...At that time, in Ephesus, a great persecution was breaking out by command of Emperor Nero. Christiani were being burned in the arena by the dozens every day. It was at that very time that the household of Gaius suffered dreadful martyrdom. If things went on so, it was clear that the Christiani of Ephesus would be exterminated. Then Onesiphorus, who was in hiding, came with Hermogenes to find me. As you know, he was not a man who feared any persecution. Yet he proposed to me that we should apostatize."

Now that at last he was able to hear the story concerning his father accurately, Callimachus was listening to Phygelus's explanation with all his mind.

"Because I did not understand what he meant, I sprang up in alarm when I heard Onesiphorus's words. But he explained. Persecution was now breaking out throughout the whole world, and if all the saints suffered martyrdom, who then would preserve Christ? And even if fervent Christiani willingly suffered martyrdom, it was likewise not desirable that new Christiani, who had not yet even entered deeply into the faith, should simply suffer martyrdom without condition. So, in order to give the saints of Ephesus freedom of choice, we ourselves became cowards who apostatized. Onesiphorus said that because we do not believe in Christ in order to be seen by men, we must not fear disgrace in this world. He was a man who trusted God more than men. Thus many people, according to their free will, apostatized and escaped death. Onesiphorus said that when the time came God would call them together again.... After leaving those words Onesiphorus disappeared from Ephesus. I too left Ephesus and lived elsewhere, and only recently in the great theater I met the Apostle John and confessed those things...."

All the people were being moved by the true faith of Onesiphorus. They remembered Paul's letter.

"May the Lord grant mercy to the household of Onesiphorus.... You know better than anyone how much service he rendered when I was in Ephesus. May the Lord grant him mercy on the day when the Lord returns...."

Having finished speaking, Phygelus approached Callimachus and took his hand.

"Seeing you grown so great, it is as though I am seeing your father himself. Whatever others may do, at least you must not despise your father. Whether his judgment was right I do not know, but he certainly did not apostatize in order to save his life. He apostatized in order to preserve Christ. I do not know how God evaluates him, but at least, seeing that God has raised you in this way... I believe He did not regard your father with displeasure...."

All the people bowed their heads together and prayed for Onesiphorus. Onesiphorus, who had truly been willing to bear even the filthy name of apostate for the sake of the church of Ephesus, that deep faith moved the people.

"Callimachus..."

John opened his mouth again.

"Your father Onesiphorus was a far finer and truer man than this John. I now wish to tell you everything concerning myself...."

John looked down with lonely eyes upon the place where the temple of Artemis had collapsed away, then opened his mouth.

"There was another reason why I thought of you as my son and took interest in you.... As you grew up, though at a glance you seemed gentle, you were growing into a child very full of envy and quick to jealousy. That... that was just like me when I was young."

Again silence was flowing over Mount Ayasoluk. The sun had already gone down into the Mediterranean, and the surroundings were beginning to sink into darkness. John's lonely voice was beginning to be heard now and then. He was breathing very hard. Because he was extremely old, and because he had cried out with all the strength of his soul while rebuking Callimachus, he had in an instant become exhausted. His strength had failed so greatly that unless Polycarp and Prochorus supported him at the sides, it would have been difficult for him even to sit there.

"From birth I was born a man full of jealousy. Because of my brother James, who was one year older than I, I could never endure the jealousy that kept boiling up within me. Because of that one year's difference, James always came first in everything, and the right of the firstborn was James's. Moreover, James was not generous in character like the other brothers, but, just like me, had a quick temper and a jealous disposition. In everything I opposed my brother, and at the slightest occasion we fought. I was so frantic that I always wanted to kill my brother. That kind of disposition in me grew worse from the time I met John of Gischala when I was grown...."

John was again gasping, steadying his breath, and only after a long while was he barely able to continue the words.

"John of Gischala too was a fellow quick in temper and sharp in mind. He and I entered into a triangular rivalry over a certain Greek girl. But that girl was carried off by Vespasianus, who was serving as a cavalryman in Tiberias, and was violated...."

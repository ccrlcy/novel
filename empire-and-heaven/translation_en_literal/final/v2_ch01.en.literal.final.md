# Empire and Heaven (II)

## Chapter 1. The Banner of Rebellion

"Callimachus, is this how you mean to repay the apostle's love?"

Callimachus glared at Polycarp with eyes wide open.

"Love? That old man officiated at the wedding of Andronicus and Drusiana. Is that your love? What is such an old man to me, one who sold himself to some petty Roman quaestor and even stood there officiating? Either he has gone senile in his old age, or he has rotted to the core and festered through. Tell the old man this: for old times' sake, I will refrain from cutting off his breath with my own hand!"

Again he hurled the wine cup that had fallen before him at Polycarp.

"Drink! Is it because this wine does not equal the delicacies of the house of Andronicus that you will not drink it? All of you call yourselves my friends and brothers, and yet you crowded into the wedding of Andronicus and Drusiana and received with those filthy hands the feast they set before you. You are cowards when it comes to fighting for justice, and only strong when it comes to eating what others give you. Drink! Is it that the wine of Andronicus is clean, but the wine of Callimachus too dirty to drink?"

It was then. There came the sound of hoofbeats from the entrance, and one of the bandits ran all the way up before Callimachus, halted his horse, and leaped down.

"News has come from Ephesus."

"What do they say?"

"Drusiana, wife of Marius Andronicus, returned today at noon from Rome to Ephesus. They say she came because Andronicus's mother is gravely ill and needs to be cared for."

"Who came with her from Rome?"

"Only one maidservant, a child called Gaius, and the servant of Andronicus, Hesthenes."

"Very well."

Callimachus shoved away the woman he had been fondling, flung aside his cup, and rose.

Several of the bandits rose with their chief. Callimachus gave them instructions one by one concerning what each must do.

"Only ten from headquarters are to follow me. All the rest are to assemble at Sardis after the festival is over. I will tell you the next target when we meet in Sardis."

"What shall we do with the captives?"

"Toys are meant to be thrown away after one has played with them. Search their bodies, strip off every ornament they carry, and then release them. Once they have bathed, they will become new goods again. Be careful in transporting the things collected, and see that no individual can hide any along the way."

When he had finished giving orders, Callimachus sprang onto his horse like the wind and in no time was already riding out of the great theater. After him rode ten bandits on horseback.

Polycarp too shoved aside the woman leaning against him and dashed out of the theater without another thought. From the way Callimachus had spoken, he seemed clearly to be going to attack the household of Andronicus.

"...?"

By chance, outside the theater several horses were tied there. Apparently the bandits had tethered them there and gone inside to take part in the revelry of the theater.

(Lord... is it theft to take a bandit's horse?)

He repeated to himself the Eighth Commandment of the Ten, then, as though he did not know the answer, untied one horse and mounted it.

(Lord, forgive me!)

Polycarp, bishop of the church of Smyrna, stole a bandit's horse and began to ride hard. Coming down the slope of Pagus Hill in one rush, Polycarp, once he passed out through the gate, at once rode south toward Ephesus. He was cutting through the cool wind of September, racing onward. Even while he sped like an arrow, he kept striking the horse's sides and urging the road on.

The recent events with Callimachus kept rising before his eyes, and again and again he wiped at them. Polycarp could not possibly believe that Callimachus, who had been so quiet and orderly, had changed into another man altogether. And now Polycarp's own heart too was growing angry along with Callimachus. He did not know who the true object was, yet Polycarp was startled to discover that he too was full of complaint and resentment.

(It cannot be. It cannot....)

To speak honestly, Polycarp could not approve Drusiana's decision to marry Andronicus. He was not a Christianus, and by Polycarp's standards he was not a worthy bridegroom. Even if all the women of the world sought bridegrooms handsome in appearance, rich in money, and strong in power, he had believed that Drusiana at least would not be such a one. More than anyone, Callimachus had been the bridegroom most suited to her.

"Women are like that. Perhaps that is why the Jews, when they count the people, do not include the number of women. Perhaps the Apostle John never married for the same reason...."

Before he knew it, Polycarp was beginning to take Callimachus's side. There were simply too many bitter and maddening things in the world. As Callimachus said, there truly seemed to be nothing in mankind worth expecting. If even Drusiana, one of the truest among women, who had served zealously for the orphans and won praise from the church, in the end, at the fork where one must choose one side or another, had chosen the young quaestor Andronicus, then what need was there to speak of ordinary women? Polycarp felt that he was beginning to hate this world. The sight of himself preaching to them the spirit of the cross, telling them to live sacrificially, seemed so shabby to him that tears were standing in his eyes.

Then suddenly Polycarp realized what he had just been thinking and shuddered in horror.

(...What was I just thinking? Was I not hating Drusiana because she chose Andronicus as bridegroom?)

It was true. Already he had been comforting himself by imagining Drusiana seized by Callimachus's band and suffering humiliation.

"O Lord, forgive me. I have been thinking a dreadful thing. I was wishing to see Drusiana repaid. But... Lord, what would You do? If You were Callimachus, what would You do?

You planned this matter, so You must know, must You not?"

When he passed the Eastern Gymnasium and entered through the Magnesian Gate, Polycarp saw a strange sight and reined in his horse. A great crowd of people was pouring from the civic square toward the upper market. They carried torches, and they were shouting. It was an immense throng, just like the great crowd of forty years before, when the silversmith Demetrius had incited the merchants and raised a riot.

"The god of suspicion is dead. Domitian is dead!"

Polycarp was startled and leaped down from the horse. Waving torches, the crowd was turning in front of the governor's residence and going on toward the temple of Domitian. Polycarp ran up, seized anyone at hand, and asked,

"What has happened? Why is everyone in such an uproar?"

Then one man turned toward Polycarp and shouted,

"What, you truly do not know?"

"They say Domitian is dead. Just now Master Apollonius announced it in the marble hall of the Harbor Gymnasium."

"Apollonius?"

That meant none other than the famous philosopher and rhetorician Apollonius of Tyana. Polycarp's heart began pounding violently. In the Revelation of the Apostle John, together with the end of the ruler of this world who called himself god, the end itself was coming. And after that end came at once the second coming of Jesus. Trembling all over, Polycarp cried out,

"Lord, if You come now, what is my friend Callimachus to do?"

Polycarp mixed in with the crowd and went on toward the temple of Domitian. There rose before him the gigantic stone image standing upon a pedestal held up by four marble columns. Domitian, who had wanted to become a god while still alive, Domitian, who had been forced to live his whole life in anxiety under the fame of his father and brother, had erected that image to demand worship for himself, and it was four times his own height.

Suddenly the people burst into cheers and applause. One man came walking out from the side of the temple of Domitian, where eight marble columns stretched across and thirteen stood in depth, and stood before the statue. It was Apollonius, the great scholar of whom Ephesus was proud. In a voice ringing like a trumpet, he opened his mouth.

"Honored citizens of Ephesus, this day, September 18, has become for us an unforgettable day of history. Ephesus has now cast off the disgrace of serving a suspicious pig. We have now regained the true honor of being neokoros!"

Again the crowd shouted and stamped their feet. Originally Ephesus, as the city that served the goddess Artemis, bore the title neokoros, that is, guardian of the god. Yet Domitian had built here his own image and temple, and had made Ephesus into the city that served Domitian, dominus et deus, lord and god.

"Citizens of Ephesus, now I tell you once again of the end of Domitian. Domitian was slain in the imperial palace by his own wife Domitilla. It was Stephanos, the chief chamberlain of Domitilla, who drove the dagger into the fat body of the emperor, and the emperor fell upon the marble floor in a pool of blood. The god whom Ephesus hated has fallen. Domitian, the god of suspicion and the god of loathing, is dead."

"The god is dead! The shame of Ephesus is gone!"

Apollonius raised both hands high.

"Citizens of Ephesus, let us throw down this image of the pig and wash away the disgrace of Ephesus!"

Again the shouting broke out, and from below countless ropes were flung upward. Already several strong men had climbed upon the terrace and were fastening ropes around the statue's neck. The Emperor Domitian, who had worried and labored so greatly for the preservation of the Roman Empire, was now on the point of being hanged. When Apollonius and the men who had gone up to fix the ropes about the statue's neck came back down, the crowd shouted and pulled upon the ropes with all their might. The image of Domitian, holding up his right hand as he looked down upon the citizens of Ephesus, was now wavering and losing its balance. Someone shouted out again,

"His Majesty the Emperor is dancing!"

At once a roar of laughter broke out. The emperor, with his short jaw, thick neck, and swollen body, staggered like a drunken man, and then at last came rolling down toward the ground.

Its body was smashed, its limbs were broken off, and its head fell away and rolled aside. Again the crowd began their march, dragging one by one the emperor's body, limbs, and head.

"Ephesus, rejoice. The false god is dead. The counterfeit god is dead."

"Great Artemis! Our Artemis!"

Polycarp had no more need to remain among the crowd. Before anything else, he had to go to the house of Andronicus and confirm Drusiana's safety.

(...If Domitian is dead, perhaps the apostle on the island of Patmos may even be released. If in such a moment the apostle returns, and I must tell him that our friend Callimachus has become a bandit and Drusiana has even been abducted, what will the apostle say? And with what face will I meet him?)

At the thought that John might return, Polycarp's heart grew all the more urgent. In one rush he ran through Crete Street and entered toward Mount Pion. The mansions of the nobles of Ephesus were appearing. And among them, in the finest position of all, there came into view before his eyes the great mansion of the house of Andronicus, the largest in Ephesus.

Unlike the crowded Crete Street full of people, this quarter of the nobles was strangely quiet. Polycarp came up before the house of Andronicus and watched the situation. The arched entrance visible above the marble steps gaped open as though inviting him in.

He climbed the steps and again observed the movement inside. Again there was no sign of anyone. He stopped a moment and thought what he should do. If what Polycarp had felt at the great theater of Pagus was correct, Callimachus must surely have come here.

(He would certainly have arrived before me....)

However hard Polycarp had ridden without pause, had he passed them on the road they would have stopped him. More than that, after arriving in Ephesus, Polycarp had lost a considerable amount of time listening to the speech of Apollonius. After thinking a moment longer, he seemed to reach a decision and shouted loudly toward the inside:

"Hello! Is there anyone inside?"

Having shouted so, Polycarp waited a while for an answer from within. But from the house there came no answer at all. He shouted once more.

"Hello, is there no one inside?"

When Polycarp shouted the second time, a strange sound came from deeper within. It sounded like the cry of a beast, or perhaps the sound of a ghost. At any rate, it proved that someone was in the house. He passed through the main gate, crossed the outer garden, went beyond the middle gate, and entered the inner court. No person could be seen there either. He passed through the inner court and approached the main house. Within the house it was still quiet as death. Because the bandits might spring an attack at any moment, he crouched himself and darted inside the entrance of the drawing room.

When Polycarp entered the drawing room, he at once realized that the thing he had feared had indeed taken place. The table in the drawing room had been overturned, and flower vases lay rolling on the floor.

Nor was that all. Here and there on the floor of the drawing room bloodstains could be seen.

(...)

Polycarp felt his sight going dim. The thing he had hoped would not be so had in fact happened. Callimachus had in the end carried out the deed. When Polycarp had been invited to this house a year and a half before, there had been many servants here, but now the house stood empty.

He remembered what Callimachus had said at the great theater of Pagus:

- Only ten from headquarters are to follow me. All the rest are to assemble at Sardis after the festival is over. I will tell you the next target when we meet in Sardis.

Then surely Callimachus must have taken the abducted people away toward Sardis. But since their number had been only ten, they could not have carried away every member of the household on horseback. In that case, apart from Drusiana, the burdensome people may all have been disposed of.

(...?)

It was that sound again from a little before. Once more there came that cry like the howling of a beast. At once he went around to the back of the drawing room and turned through the arched entrance leading toward the inner chambers.

"Why, you are...?"

A man lay there covered in blood. Polycarp knew him well. He had gone with them when they went to look for Andronicus in the cave on Mount Coressus, and Polycarp had also seen him at the wedding feast in this very house. He was Hesthenes, the servant of Andronicus.

(Where could Callimachus have dragged them all away to?)

"Tell me, what happened?"

"Bandits... bandits...."

Again he pointed with his finger toward the room beside him. Polycarp leaped to his feet and ran into the next room. Drusiana was not there either. Only Andronicus's mother and the other servants were there, bound and gagged.
